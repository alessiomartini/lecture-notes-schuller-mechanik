\section{Recall: the field of two particles; exclusion of the self-interaction}\label{sec:tb-recall}

\begin{recall}[Potential and field of two particles (\cref{ch:c11})]
Last time we dealt with the gravitational potential, also as produced by two particles. The gravitational interaction field is (minus) the gradient of a scalar field $\Phi$ for which we derived the Poisson equation $\Delta\Phi=4\pi G_{\rm N}\rho$; since we assumed a metrically flat absolute space we could choose coordinates in which $\Delta=\sum_\alpha(\partial/\partial x^\alpha)^2$ (``in these coordinates, and only in these'').
The general solution is the integral \eqref{eq:ng-solution} (with the Newton constant, which the lecturer says he forgot to write on the board), and for two particles, with positions $x_1,x_2$ in this chart and the equivalence of inertial and gravitational charge (\cref{def:ng-equivalence}),
\[
  \begin{aligned}\Phi(x)&=\;-\,\frac{G_{\rm N}\,m_1}{\sqrt{\sum_\alpha(x^\alpha-x_1^\alpha)^2}}\\&\;-\;\frac{G_{\rm N}\,m_2}{\sqrt{\sum_\alpha(x^\alpha-x_2^\alpha)^2}},\end{aligned}
\] (This is \eqref{eq:ng-two}.)
The square root is once more only the abbreviation of the root of the sum of the squares of the components, not the norm of a vector (``it is not a path in space, it is only a chart''; there are no position vectors) \ts{12}{1}{2:10}--\ts{12}{1}{4:18}.
\end{recall}

From this potential the interaction field follows by a simple differentiation: at a point $x$ (written $W^\alpha(x)$ with the chart components)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-field}
  \begin{aligned}W^\alpha(x)\;=\;&-\,\frac{x^\alpha-x_1^\alpha}{\bigl(\sum_\beta(x^\beta-x_1^\beta)^2\bigr)^{3/2}}\,G_{\rm N}m_1\\&-\,\frac{x^\alpha-x_2^\alpha}{\bigl(\sum_\beta(x^\beta-x_2^\beta)^2\bigr)^{3/2}}\,G_{\rm N}m_2 ,\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
the $\alpha$-th component of $x-x_i$ times $G_{\rm N}m_i$ (a student reminds the lecturer twice of the exponent $3$ in the denominator, which had been left out on the board) \ts{12}{1}{4:40}--\ts{12}{1}{5:44}. But this holds only if $x$ does not lie on one of the positions where a particle is, and exactly these positions are interesting: with two particles of two different masses, the potential and the interaction field are everywhere, and if one imagined a third particle
which itself produced no field (``however that is supposed to go''), it would be attracted by both and one would get some effective interaction field. But what we want to know is the field at $x_1$ and at $x_2$ \ts{12}{1}{5:44}--\ts{12}{1}{6:26}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: exclusion of the self-interaction (Ausschluss der Selbstwechselwirkung)]\label{def:tb-self}
What we do \emph{not} want to consider is that a particle interacts with itself: all particles together produce \emph{one} interaction field, but the contribution which a particle makes to the production of the field is \emph{excluded} from the action on that particle itself. Concretely, the interaction field \emph{at the position of particle $1$} is the field of the other particle only,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-selfex}
  W^\alpha(x_1)\;=\;-\,G_{\rm N}\,m_2\,\frac{x_1^\alpha-x_2^\alpha}{\|x_1-x_2\|^3},\qquad
  W^\alpha(x_2)\;=\;-\,G_{\rm N}\,m_1\,\frac{x_2^\alpha-x_1^\alpha}{\|x_1-x_2\|^3},
\end{equation}
where $\|x_1-x_2\|:=\bigl(\sum_\beta(x_1^\beta-x_2^\beta)^2\bigr)^{1/2}$ is again only an abbreviation (the double bars are no norm), and the term which comes from the particle itself is simply left out \ts{12}{1}{6:26}--\ts{12}{1}{7:50}. (The formula of the board has the factor with the sign written the other way for $W(x_2)$; with the exchanged indices this is the same expression, as written here.)
\end{definition}

\begin{coursenote}[On the self-interaction (as said in class)]\label{cn:tb-self}
Why this exclusion, and does it not look strange? One usually divides the particles into \emph{field-producing particles} and \emph{test particles}: a test particle has so small a charge that its influence on the interaction field is negligible, and one considers only its passive role, how it moves in the field produced by the others. That is a very common idea in classical physics.
One can express it differently, saying that \emph{all} particles produce the field (why should one suddenly assume that only the electrons ``over there'' do, and not just the one I am looking at?), but on a concrete particle the contribution that comes from \emph{itself} does not act: ``that is always the same and always equally artificial''. If one inserted $x=x_1$ in the formula of the particle $1$ itself there would be a \emph{divergence} (``unpleasant, but that is how it is''), and one has to exclude it.
One can elegantly sweep this under the table; here we do it explicitly. The self-interaction ``accompanies you through physics'', and in quantum mechanics there are new insights about it (an outlook) \ts{12}{1}{8:11}--\ts{12}{1}{9:55}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{theorem}[Newton's third law as a consequence (``actio gleich reactio'')]\label{thm:tb-third}
With \eqref{eq:tb-selfex}, and since in the gravitational interaction the charge of a particle is its inertial mass,
\[
  m_1\,W^\alpha(x_1)\;=\;-\,m_2\,W^\alpha(x_2)\qquad(\alpha=1,2,3),
\]
that is, the force of particle $2$ on particle $1$ is the negative of the force of particle $1$ on particle $2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$m_1W^\alpha(x_1)=-G_{\rm N}m_1m_2\,(x_1^\alpha-x_2^\alpha)/\|x_1-x_2\|^3$ and $m_2W^\alpha(x_2)=-G_{\rm N}m_1m_2\,(x_2^\alpha-x_1^\alpha)/\|x_1-x_2\|^3$. The numerators differ by a sign, the denominators are equal since the double bar is symmetric in $x_1,x_2$ by definition, so an overall minus sign remains \ts{12}{1}{9:55}--\ts{12}{1}{10:40}.
\end{proof}
This is what one usually calls the \textbf{third Newtonian law}: ``for every force the force exerted by one body on the other is equal and opposite to the one exerted by the other on the first'' (vaguely formulated), where ``charge times interaction field'' is the force on particle $1$, and the other the force on particle $2$. But we did \emph{not} introduce it as an axiom; it is a \emph{consequence} of our definition of the Poisson equation, with the exclusion of the self-interaction, and so on.
That is the modern way of implementing it: the interaction fields are built (by their field equations, here the Poisson equation) so that it comes out automatically, and the mysterious ``one acts on the other and vice versa'' rule is obsolete: \emph{the third Newtonian axiom is absorbed in the way we think about the field equations of the interaction fields}. This is the modern point of view (originally from Faraday, and by now realised in the whole of physics) \ts{12}{1}{10:40}--\ts{12}{1}{12:23}.

\section{The two coupled equations of motion}\label{sec:tb-eom}

Now we set up the Newton equation, the second axiom (\cref{ax:nd-second}), for two particles, purely in the absolute space \ts{12}{1}{12:43}. For particle $1$ and particle $2$ it reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-eom}
  m_1\,\ddot x_1^{\alpha}\;=\;m_1\,W^\alpha(x_1)\;=\;-\,\frac{G_{\rm N}m_1m_2\,(x_1^\alpha-x_2^\alpha)}{\|x_1-x_2\|^3},\qquad
  m_2\,\ddot x_2^{\alpha}\;=\;m_2\,W^\alpha(x_2)\;=\;-\,\frac{G_{\rm N}m_1m_2\,(x_2^\alpha-x_1^\alpha)}{\|x_1-x_2\|^3},
\end{equation}
where the covariant-derivative terms (the $\Gamma$'s) have disappeared: we chose the coordinates in such a clever way that all the $\Gamma$'s vanish on our absolute space (that was one of the assumptions: the space is flat), so the acceleration is just the second derivative of the components \ts{12}{1}{13:04}--\ts{12}{1}{13:46}.
We do not yet need the explicit form of the right-hand sides: all that matters is that they are \emph{opposite}, which follows from \cref{thm:tb-third}.

These are two \textbf{coupled} differential equations: no separation into $x_1$ and $x_2$, the right-hand side of each contains both. It is a system for \emph{six} unknown functions ($x_1^\alpha$ three, $x_2^\alpha$ another three), and at first sight it is quite intimidating: quotients, coupling, and so on. ``One would perhaps think about it for three weeks, if one is fast for a few days''; but of course I show you how it goes \ts{12}{1}{14:07}--\ts{12}{1}{14:48}.
The first trick is to turn these two equations into two \emph{new} equations: one adds them and one subtracts them \ts{12}{1}{14:48}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The sum: since the right-hand sides are opposite,
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-sum}
          m_1\ddot x_1^\alpha+m_2\ddot x_2^\alpha\;=\;0 .
        \end{equation}
  \item The difference: $m_1\ddot x_1^\alpha-m_2\ddot x_2^\alpha=-2G_{\rm N}m_1m_2\,(x_1^\alpha-x_2^\alpha)/\|x_1-x_2\|^3$ (same right-hand sides, hence the factor $2$) \ts{12}{1}{15:09}--\ts{12}{1}{15:29}.
\end{enumerate}
The first looks already great: a $0$ on the right-hand side. And the idea that one reaches fairly quickly is to go to \emph{centre-of-mass coordinates} \ts{12}{1}{15:50}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: centre of mass (Schwerpunkt) and relative coordinate]\label{def:tb-cm}
Define the \textbf{centre of mass} (the name: the point where one could support the system in balance, the ``weighted mean'' of the positions) and the \textbf{relative coordinate} (all quantities depend on $t$, which is not written)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-cm}
  S^\alpha(t)\;:=\;\frac{m_1\,x_1^\alpha(t)+m_2\,x_2^\alpha(t)}{m_1+m_2},\qquad
  r^\alpha(t)\;:=\;x_2^\alpha(t)-x_1^\alpha(t)\ \ (\alpha=1,2,3).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
Note that the centre of mass of two particles at the \emph{same} absolute time is needed: to define $S$ at all, positions at one time are taken \ts{12}{1}{16:12}--\ts{12}{1}{16:33}.

\begin{coursenote}[Centre of mass: no vectors, and no relativity (as said in class)]\label{cn:tb-nocm}
This sounds pedantic (``what else?''), but in the theory of relativity there is no simultaneity any more, so \emph{in relativity there is no centre of mass any more}: the centre of mass is a ``fantasy of non-relativistic mechanics''. Moreover the $x_1,x_2$ are \emph{not vectors}: each is a collection of three numbers describing a position in our chart. Like the positions, the centre of mass is not a vector either, and defined only in non-relativistic mechanics. The same holds for $r^\alpha$: in the chart it is an element of $\R^3$ and one can write the three components as an arrow,
but it is not a vector (``I do not like to draw it in the chart, since the impression arises that this would be a path vector'') \ts{12}{1}{16:33}--\ts{12}{1}{18:56}.
\end{coursenote}

From the two definitions one reads off the inverse: $x_2^\alpha=S^\alpha+\frac{m_1}{m_1+m_2}r^\alpha$ and $x_1^\alpha=S^\alpha-\frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}r^\alpha$ (put in a picture: the two masses at $x_1$ and $x_2$ with an arbitrary origin and $S$ on the line between them) \ts{12}{1}{18:56}--\ts{12}{1}{19:38}. (Check: the second minus the first gives $r^\alpha$; $m_1x_1+m_2x_2=(m_1+m_2)S$.) ``That is just a flash of thought, a definition; it comes out of this after a bit of playing around.''

\subsection{The centre of mass moves uniformly}\label{ssec:tb-cm}

The sum \eqref{eq:tb-sum} is, by the definition of $S$, simply the statement $\ddot S^\alpha=0$ (the second derivative of the centre-of-mass components vanishes): so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-cmmotion}
  S^\alpha(t)\;=\;v^\alpha t+s^\alpha\qquad(\text{constants }v^\alpha,s^\alpha),
\end{equation}
the centre of mass of the whole system moves \emph{uniformly in a straight line} (``in these coordinates, but since the $\Gamma$'s vanish it really is a uniform rectilinear motion'') \ts{12}{1}{17:34}--\ts{12}{1}{17:55}. Thus the first three of the six unknowns are solved.

\subsection{The relative motion and the reduced mass}\label{ssec:tb-reduced}

For the other equation, insert $x_1^\alpha=S^\alpha-\frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}r^\alpha$, so $m_1\ddot x_1^\alpha=m_1\ddot S^\alpha-\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}\ddot r^\alpha=-\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}\ddot r^\alpha$ (since $\ddot S=0$). The right-hand side of the first equation \eqref{eq:tb-eom} is, in terms of $r=x_2-x_1$: $x_1-x_2=-r$, so
\[
  -\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,\ddot r^\alpha\;=\;+\,G_{\rm N}m_1m_2\,\frac{r^\alpha}{\|r\|^3}.
\]
(The lecturer does the same with the difference equation, setting $m_1\ddot x_1-m_2\ddot x_2=-2\mu\,\ddot r$ with $\ddot S=0$ and cancelling the $2$ against the $2$: ``you see immediately on the left the same expression twice''. The two routes are equivalent; the direct one avoids the factor $2$ \ts{12}{1}{20:20}--\ts{12}{1}{22:26}.)
So there remains one single equation for three unknown functions, instead of six: ``the other three we have already dealt with in the centre of mass'' \ts{12}{1}{22:47}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: reduced mass (reduzierte Masse) and coupling constant]\label{def:tb-reduced}
Because these expressions come up again and again, they have traditional names:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-mu}
  \mu\;:=\;\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}\quad\text{(the \textbf{reduced mass})},\qquad k\;:=\;G_{\rm N}\,m_1m_2\quad\text{(the \textbf{coupling constant})}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
With these the system of six coupled equations has been reduced to the equation for three quantities, the \textbf{equation of the relative motion}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-rel}
  \mu\,\ddot r^\alpha\;=\;-\,k\,\frac{r^\alpha}{\|r\|^3},\qquad \|r\|=\Bigl(\sum_\beta(r^\beta)^2\Bigr)^{1/2},
\end{equation}
``that is all that remains of the problem; we are very happy'' \ts{12}{1}{23:08}--\ts{12}{1}{23:51}. (If $m_2\gg m_1$ then $\mu\approx m_1$: the lighter body moves around the practically resting heavy one.) But it is still not easy to solve: it is a second-order differential equation for each of the $r^\alpha$, with this quotient on the right-hand side ``which looks terrible'' \ts{12}{1}{23:51}--\ts{12}{1}{24:31}.

\begin{intuition}[A lesson: the logic of ``integrating'']\label{int:tb-conserved}
If one now tries to solve this by brute force one runs one's head against the wall; and this teaches a lesson that will come back again and again. One says ``one wants to integrate'', but that is only a manner of speaking: one cannot just put an integral sign. The logic is that one wants to get rid of the derivatives, since one wants the solution $r^\alpha(t)$;
and it would be incredibly good if one could turn, at least partly, the second-order equation into first-order equations. In physicists' jargon this runs under the question: are there \textbf{conserved quantities} (Erhaltungsgr\"o{\ss}en) for this kind of motion? A conserved quantity is a quantity in which the dynamical variables occur and which does \emph{not change} in time \ts{12}{1}{24:31}--\ts{12}{1}{25:34}.
\end{intuition}

\section{Angular momentum and planar motion}\label{sec:tb-L}

\subsection{Conservation of the angular momentum components}\label{ssec:tb-Lcons}

The heading is ``angular momentum conservation'' (Drehimpulserhaltung); ``I could also have called it conservation of flowers'': I do not pull the conservation of angular momentum out of the hat, I argue: \emph{stare at the equation}. It is often important: one sometimes thinks one needs secret knowledge; everything is in there, and by staring at the equation one learns everything. We stare at it in the following way \ts{12}{1}{25:34}--\ts{12}{1}{26:38}.
Take $\ddot r^\alpha=-\frac{k}{\mu}\frac{r^\alpha}{\|r\|^3}$ and multiply both sides by $r^\beta$ (one may multiply both sides as one likes) and by a constant, the \textbf{Levi-Civita symbol} (also called epsilon symbol) $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}$, with a summation over $\beta,\gamma$ (Einstein convention; the $\beta,\gamma$ stand on the right-hand side). Here we rename the free index $\alpha\to\gamma$ on the left, so:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-eps}
  \varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,r^\beta\,\ddot r^\gamma\;=\;-\,\frac{k}{\mu\,\|r\|^3}\;\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,r^\beta\,r^\gamma\;=\;0 .
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the Levi-Civita symbol $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}$]\label{def:tb-eps}
$\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}$ is the symbol with $\varepsilon_{123}=\varepsilon_{231}=\varepsilon_{312}=+1$ (cyclic permutations: even), $\varepsilon_{213}=\varepsilon_{132}=\varepsilon_{321}=-1$ (odd permutations), and $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=0$ whenever two indices coincide (e.g.\ $\varepsilon_{112}=\varepsilon_{111}=0$). It is \emph{antisymmetric} under the exchange of any two indices \ts{12}{1}{27:20}--\ts{12}{1}{28:01}.
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[A student asks to explain the symbol]\label{cn:tb-epsq}
A student asks the lecturer to explain the symbol again (``then ask it, please''); the answer is the definition above, with the remark that all other components, ``also $112$, $111$ and so on'', are zero \ts{12}{1}{27:40}.
\end{coursenote}
The right-hand side of \eqref{eq:tb-eps} vanishes because $r^\beta r^\gamma$ is symmetric in $\beta,\gamma$ while $\varepsilon$ is antisymmetric (``if this is not clear, write out the summation and use what is below: one sees it at a glance''). The left-hand side is not trivially zero (it contains other numbers), but we can claim that it equals the time derivative
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-Ldot}
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\Bigl(\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,r^\beta\,\dot r^\gamma\Bigr)\;=\;\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,\dot r^\beta\dot r^\gamma+\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,r^\beta\ddot r^\gamma\;=\;\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,r^\beta\ddot r^\gamma ,
\end{equation}
by the product rule (the $\varepsilon$ are constants), and the term $\dot r^\beta\dot r^\gamma$ is again symmetric, so it drops out against the antisymmetric $\varepsilon$ \ts{12}{1}{28:23}--\ts{12}{1}{30:06}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the angular momentum components $L_\alpha$]\label{def:tb-L}
Define, for $\alpha=1,2,3$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-L}
  L_\alpha\;:=\;\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,r^\beta\,\dot r^\gamma ,\qquad\text{so that}\quad \dot L_\alpha=0
\end{equation}
by \eqref{eq:tb-eps} and \eqref{eq:tb-Ldot}. We call the $L_\alpha$ the \textbf{angular momentum components} (Drehimpulskomponenten) of the relative motion (here per unit of the reduced mass; with the factor $\mu$ one gets the usual angular momentum) \ts{12}{1}{30:06}--\ts{12}{1}{30:50}.
\end{definition}
Each of the three components is therefore conserved: ``this is the conservation of the angular momentum (components)'' \ts{12}{1}{32:33}.

\begin{coursenote}[The angular momentum is no vector; the cross product (as said in class)]\label{cn:tb-Lvec}
``Now you say: clear, the angular momentum is a vector, we have learnt. No: the angular momentum is not a vector.'' One sees this in many places, most clearly in the $r^\beta$: these are the positions in the chart and \emph{they do not transform as a vector}. One could also define the $\varepsilon$ a little differently so that it looks the same in these coordinates but transforms better in other coordinates; the main problem is the $r^\beta$.
Also: if one thinks of an extended system that rotates, ``there stands the angular momentum vector and the system turns'', one must ask in which tangent space this vector should live; as long as one can push vectors around freely this is swept under the table. So the $L_\alpha$ are \emph{not the components of a vector}; but that is not bad: each of them is conserved, and we call this the conservation of angular momentum;
no vector is needed. The statement comes directly out of the equation that interests us: ``I do not pull the miraculous principle of angular momentum conservation out of the pocket; it comes out of this equation, and I need not understand anything for it''; I could call it otherwise, ``I call it angular momentum only so that you do not look into the book and wonder''; the name has no content, I have only defined a quantity \ts{12}{1}{31:11}--\ts{12}{1}{34:38}.
If $r$ were a vector, $L$ would be what you know from school as $\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}$, with the rule ``this with this and that with that''; ``with this epsilon one can calculate wonderfully, with that school rule hardly anyone can'' \ts{12}{1}{33:57}.
\end{coursenote}

\subsection{Planar motion and polar coordinates}\label{ssec:tb-plane}

Next: for a concrete constellation the $L_\alpha$ are three numbers which do not change their value during the whole course of time \ts{12}{1}{35:02}. Multiply $L_\alpha$ by $r^\alpha$ and sum:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-plane}
  L_\alpha\,r^\alpha\;=\;\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,r^\alpha r^\beta\,\dot r^\gamma\;=\;0,
\end{equation}
again because $r^\alpha r^\beta$ is symmetric and $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}$ antisymmetric in $\alpha,\beta$ \ts{12}{1}{35:24}--\ts{12}{1}{36:05}.
This is a \emph{linear equation} for the $r^\alpha$ with constant coefficients $L_\alpha$: the equation of a \textbf{plane}. (``In school one would carelessly say: a plane equation with normal vector with components $L_\alpha$; but these are no components of a vector, and if anything one would make a projector.'') It is a plane equation all the same. Hence: \emph{the $r^\alpha$ never leave a certain plane}; which plane depends on the constants $L_\alpha$ \ts{12}{1}{36:05}--\ts{12}{1}{36:48}.

Without loss of generality we may assume $L_1=0$, $L_2=0$ and $L_3\neq0$ (choose the chart suitably; the case with $L_3$ also zero, i.e.\ all $L_\alpha=0$, is a very boring solution, \emph{homework}). Then \eqref{eq:tb-plane} gives $L_3r^3=0$, hence $r^3=0$: the motion takes place in the $(r^1,r^2)$-plane \ts{12}{1}{36:48}--\ts{12}{1}{37:50}.
In this plane we introduce polar coordinates at the same time (the lecturer: ``I use the angular momentum conservation to identify a certain planar motion and choose polar coordinates in the plane'', as seen on the problem sheets):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-polar}
  r^1(t)=r(t)\cos\varphi(t),\qquad r^2(t)=r(t)\sin\varphi(t),\qquad r^3(t)=0,
\end{equation}
where now $r(t)=\|r\|$ denotes the radius and $\varphi(t)$ the angle (and one has the inverse transformations) \ts{12}{1}{37:50}--\ts{12}{1}{38:32}. Differentiating, $\dot r^1=\dot r\cos\varphi-r\dot\varphi\sin\varphi$, $\dot r^2=\dot r\sin\varphi+r\dot\varphi\cos\varphi$, $\dot r^3=0$ \ts{12}{1}{38:32}--\ts{12}{1}{39:15}.
Now compute the angular momentum components in the new coordinates. $L_1$ and $L_2$ vanish (``one could verify it quickly again'': they contain $r^3$ or $\dot r^3$). For $L_3=\varepsilon_{3\beta\gamma}r^\beta\dot r^\gamma$ only $\varepsilon_{312}=+1$ and $\varepsilon_{321}=-1$ contribute, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-L3}
  L_3\;=\;r^1\dot r^2-r^2\dot r^1\;=\;r\cos\varphi\,(\dot r\sin\varphi+r\dot\varphi\cos\varphi)-r\sin\varphi\,(\dot r\cos\varphi-r\dot\varphi\sin\varphi)\;=\;r^2\,\dot\varphi ,
\end{equation}
the terms with $\dot r$ cancel and $\cos^2+\sin^2=1$ remains (the lecturer first loses a sign and corrects it: ``the last plus is in fact a plus'') \ts{12}{1}{39:15}--\ts{12}{1}{42:28}.
``We keep in mind: $L_3=r^2\dot\varphi$; one needs it again and again.'' The polar coordinates were a good idea: it looks much simpler \ts{12}{1}{42:48}--\ts{12}{1}{43:33}.

\section{The radial equation}\label{sec:tb-radial}

Now go back to the equation of the relative motion \eqref{eq:tb-rel}, the remaining problem: we have only drawn conclusions from it, but have also changed coordinates; we compute it in polar coordinates, in $r$ and $\varphi$ \ts{12}{1}{43:33}--\ts{12}{1}{44:16}. We need the second derivative of $r^\alpha$: by the product rule
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-acc}
  \begin{aligned}
    \ddot r^1&=(\ddot r-r\dot\varphi^2)\cos\varphi-(2\dot r\dot\varphi+r\ddot\varphi)\sin\varphi,\\
    \ddot r^2&=(\ddot r-r\dot\varphi^2)\sin\varphi+(2\dot r\dot\varphi+r\ddot\varphi)\cos\varphi,\qquad \ddot r^3=0 .
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
(The lecturer computes this on the board with several slips, finally ``this looks horrible and one thinks: oh dear, this has hardly become simpler''; the result above is the corrected one \ts{12}{1}{44:16}--\ts{12}{1}{46:45}.)
But we keep in mind that $L_3=r^2\dot\varphi$ is conserved: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(r^2\dot\varphi)=2r\dot r\dot\varphi+r^2\ddot\varphi=0$, hence $r\,(2\dot r\dot\varphi+r\ddot\varphi)=0$, and thus $2\dot r\dot\varphi+r\ddot\varphi=0$: ``this whole block goes out because of the angular momentum conservation'', in both components \ts{12}{1}{46:45}--\ts{12}{1}{48:32}. What remains is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-acc2}
  \ddot r^\alpha\;=\;(\ddot r-r\dot\varphi^2)\,(\cos\varphi,\ \sin\varphi,\ 0)\;=\;(\ddot r-r\dot\varphi^2)\,\frac{r^\alpha}{r} ,
\end{equation}
since $(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi,0)=r^\alpha/\|r\|$: ``this does not look so bad in polar coordinates, one thinks it looks worse than it is'' \ts{12}{1}{48:32}--\ts{12}{1}{49:57}.

Insert this in the equation of the relative motion: on the left stands $\mu(\ddot r-r\dot\varphi^2)\,r^\alpha/r$, on the right $-k\,r^\alpha/r^3$; hence the two prefactors of $r^\alpha$ must agree, and the whole thing \emph{reduces to a single equation}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-rad1}
  \mu\,\ddot r-\mu\,r\,\dot\varphi^2\;=\;-\,\frac{k}{r^2}.
\end{equation}
So instead of the former six coupled equations we now have a single differential equation, for the polar radius $r$ \ts{12}{1}{52:23}--\ts{12}{1}{53:49}. But it still contains $\dot\varphi$; here we use again the angular momentum conservation, $L_3=r^2\dot\varphi$, a fixed (conserved) number, so $\dot\varphi=L_3/r^2$; then $r\dot\varphi^2=L_3^2/r^3$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-rad}
  \mu\,\ddot r\;-\;\frac{\mu\,L_3^2}{r^3}\;=\;-\,\frac{k}{r^2}
\end{equation}
(the lecturer first writes the $\dot\varphi^2$ with ``$L_3^2$ over $\mu^2$'' and then absorbs the factors, ``it is not the same $\mu$ on both sides''; this is the corrected form, which holds for $L_3=r^2\dot\varphi$ without a mass factor as defined above [?]). ``This is now really a \emph{single ordinary differential equation} for $r$, a frame around it''
\ts{12}{1}{54:10}--\ts{12}{1}{56:00}. One could now start to discuss the solutions qualitatively, but we go on \ts{12}{1}{56:00}.

\subsection{The trick: parametrise by the angle, and substitute $u=1/r$}\label{ssec:tb-trick}

We again need an idea, and it is again a \emph{re-parametrisation}. We have the angular momentum conservation $L_3=r^2\dot\varphi$ once more: since $L_3\neq0$ and $r^2>0$, we have $\dot\varphi\neq0$, and (taking $L_3>0$, the other sign is the same with the opposite sense of rotation) $\dot\varphi>0$ for all times: \emph{the angle grows all the time} \ts{12}{1}{56:22}--\ts{12}{1}{57:25}.
Therefore, instead of taking the time as the parameter on which $r$ depends, we let $r$ depend on the \emph{angle}: we do not ask ``at what time is the other body how far away?'', but ``at which angle position is the other body how far away?''. (It could go wrong if the system is so arranged that after one revolution one is at the same angle but at a different distance, e.g.\ a planetary orbit which does not close: ``we shall see that this does not happen''; locally one can do it anyway, as $\varphi(t)$ is strictly increasing and so invertible) \ts{12}{1}{57:25}--\ts{12}{1}{58:26}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the radius as a function of the angle, $\tilde r(\varphi)$]\label{def:tb-rtilde}
Define $\tilde r(\varphi)$ by $\tilde r(\varphi(t)):=r(t)$ (``the angle depends on the time, and I make $r$ also depend on it''; in the lecture a curly r) \ts{12}{1}{58:26}--\ts{12}{1}{58:46}.
\end{definition}
By the chain rule
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-chain}
  \dot r\;=\;\tilde r'(\varphi)\,\dot\varphi\;=\;\tilde r'\,\frac{L_3}{\tilde r^{2}},\qquad
  \ddot r\;=\;\tilde r''\,\dot\varphi\,\frac{L_3}{\tilde r^2}+\tilde r'\,L_3\,\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\Bigl(\frac1{\tilde r^2}\Bigr)
  \;=\;\tilde r''\,\frac{L_3^2}{\tilde r^4}-2\,\tilde r'\,\frac{L_3}{\tilde r^3}\,\dot r ,
\end{equation}
where the prime denotes the derivative with respect to $\varphi$, $\dot\varphi=L_3/\tilde r^2$ was inserted, and $\dot r$ is replaced again by $\tilde r'L_3/\tilde r^2$; so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-chain2}
  \ddot r\;=\;\frac{L_3^2}{\tilde r^4}\,\tilde r''\;-\;\frac{2L_3^2}{\tilde r^5}\,(\tilde r')^2 .
\end{equation}
(The lecturer computes this on the board with intermediate steps and checks by comparison: ``this looks super''; the difference between $\dot r$ and $\tilde r'$ is just the factor $L_3/\tilde r^2$) \ts{12}{1}{58:46}--\ts{12}{1}{1:03:22}.
Inserting into the radial equation \eqref{eq:tb-rad} gives the equation of motion expressed through $\tilde r(\varphi)$ instead of $r(t)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-rad-phi}
  \mu\,\frac{L_3^2}{\tilde r^4}\,\tilde r''-\mu\,\frac{2L_3^2}{\tilde r^5}\,(\tilde r')^2-\mu\,\frac{L_3^2}{\tilde r^3}\;=\;-\,\frac{k}{\tilde r^2}.
\end{equation}
``This does not sound particularly impressive at first sight, but now comes trick 17'': it is more complicated than ever, we could multiply by $\tilde r^5$ and have $\tilde r''$, $(\tilde r')^2$, constants: still no nice linear equation \ts{12}{1}{1:03:22}--\ts{12}{1}{1:05:35}.
The trick is the following, and one sees it only when one has stared long at it: there are the $1/\tilde r$'s. What happens if we make the \emph{substitution}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-u}
  u(\varphi)\;:=\;\frac1{\tilde r(\varphi)}\ ?
\end{equation}
Compute: $\tilde r'=-u'/u^2$ and $\tilde r''=-u''/u^2+2(u')^2/u^3$ (``the product rule, once more'') \ts{12}{1}{1:05:35}--\ts{12}{1}{1:07:26}. Inserting in \eqref{eq:tb-rad-phi} with $1/\tilde r=u$ (so $1/\tilde r^4=u^4$ etc.):
\[
  \mu L_3^2u^4\Bigl(-\frac{u''}{u^2}+\frac{2(u')^2}{u^3}\Bigr)-2\mu L_3^2u^5\,\frac{(u')^2}{u^4}-\mu L_3^2u^3\;=\;-k\,u^2 .
\]
The terms with $(u')^2$ cancel ($2\mu L_3^2u(u')^2-2\mu L_3^2u(u')^2=0$): only $-\mu L_3^2u^2u''-\mu L_3^2u^3=-ku^2$ remains, and dividing by $-\mu L_3^2u^2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-ode}
  \boxed{\;u''(\varphi)+u(\varphi)\;=\;\frac{k}{\mu\,L_3^2}\;}
\end{equation}
\ts{12}{1}{1:07:26}--\ts{12}{1}{1:10:16}. (The lecturer's dictation of the constants on the right-hand side is garbled in the captions: ``$\mu k/L_3^2$'', ``$L_3^4$, $\mu^2$''; I recomputed with $L_3=r^2\dot\varphi$ as defined above and with $\mu$ on the left of \eqref{eq:tb-rad}: the constant is $k/(\mu L_3^2)$ [?].)

So the whole remaining problem is reduced to the solution of a differential equation for a quantity depending only on $\varphi$: \emph{linear}, of second order, with constant coefficients, and with a constant inhomogeneity on the right-hand side: ``and now we can jubilate: we have solved the two-body problem; only what it all means is still to be seen'' \ts{12}{1}{1:10:16}--\ts{12}{1}{1:10:37}.

\section{Solution of the orbit equation}\label{sec:tb-solution}

How does one solve such an equation? A \emph{linear} differential equation with an \emph{inhomogeneity}: one first solves the homogeneous equation (all solutions), and then one needs \emph{one} special solution of the inhomogeneous equation, which one has to guess, and adds it to the general homogeneous one: ``this is a procedure that one can do''. So:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The homogeneous equation $u''+u=0$ has the general two-parameter solution $u_{\rm hom}(\varphi)=A\cos(\varphi-\varphi_0)$ with amplitude $A$ and phase $\varphi_0$ (``or $a\cos\varphi+b\sin\varphi$: differentiate twice, one gets the cosine back with a minus sign, so $u''+u=0$'') \ts{12}{1}{1:10:37}--\ts{12}{1}{1:12:41}.
  \item A special solution of the inhomogeneous equation: the constant $u_{\rm sp}(\varphi)=k/(\mu L_3^2)$ (``it does not depend on $\varphi$ at all; differentiating a constant twice gives zero, so what remains is $u_{\rm sp}=k/(\mu L_3^2)$''; it solves it) \ts{12}{1}{1:12:41}--\ts{12}{1}{1:13:44}.
\end{enumerate}
Hence the general solution of the complete equation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-usol}
  u(\varphi)\;=\;A\cos(\varphi-\varphi_0)+\frac{k}{\mu L_3^2}\ \ \Bigl(=\frac{k}{\mu L_3^2}\bigl(1+\epsilon\cos(\varphi-\varphi_0)\bigr)\Bigr),
\end{equation}
\ts{12}{1}{1:13:44}--\ts{12}{1}{1:14:25} where the second form is obtained by scaling the amplitude: $\epsilon:=A\,\mu L_3^2/k$ (``I give it the name $\varepsilon$ [the Greek epsilon of the lecture, here written $\epsilon$ so as not to confuse it with the Levi-Civita symbol], because the form pleases me better'' \ts{12}{1}{1:18:57}--\ts{12}{1}{1:19:38}). Rotating the angle origin ($\varphi_0=0$, ``a shift'') and letting $\epsilon\ge0$ (a negative $\epsilon$ is the same as a shift by $\pi$) we get with $\tilde r=1/u$ the orbit
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-orbit}
  \boxed{\;\tilde r(\varphi)\;=\;\frac{p}{1+\epsilon\cos\varphi},\qquad p:=\frac{\mu L_3^2}{k}\;}
\end{equation}
(the symbol $p$ is not used in the lecture; the lecturer plots $u$ and then its inverse).

\begin{coursenote}[On solving differential equations, harmonic oscillators and over-interpretation (as said in class)]\label{cn:tb-ode}
\textbf{(1)} ``We have solved it; but what does it mean?'' Solving differential equations is not that simple: if they are not the simplest, it can be very, very hard. And there is the question whether the equation has solutions at all: hence all the existence theorems of mathematics; one can make minimal changes to an equation which has a wonderful solution, and there is none any more. A hundred years ago a physicist studied for three semesters how to solve differential equations; today this is mentioned only in passing.
But one sees: once the laws stand there, the Newtonian law is an ordinary second-order differential equation; if one wants to know what the system does, one has to \emph{solve} it \ts{12}{1}{1:14:25}--\ts{12}{1}{1:15:29}.
\textbf{(2)} In textbooks the \emph{harmonic oscillator} and the two-body problem and so on are discussed, and one gets the impression that the harmonic oscillator is a very important system. It is indeed important, but \emph{not because it is so important, but because we happen to be able to solve it}; in the further theories as well, if one meets such a problem, one can solve it. Sometimes one even hears: ``everything which oscillates is, in the lowest order, for small deflections, harmonic'': that is not true.
Take a potential which goes like $x^4$: its Taylor expansion has no quadratic term, so not every oscillation is harmonic to lowest order; only if it is, we can do textbook examples \ts{12}{1}{1:15:29}--\ts{12}{1}{1:17:34}.
\textbf{(3)} Earlier one said: we must be able to solve all possible differential equations as far as one can; there are no general solution methods that always work. Today this has receded since there are other methods. In the lecture we do not solve many problems; this one explicitly, and one sees that it takes some effort. Newtonian mechanics reduces the mechanical problems to the solution of differential equations, and the answer to the physical question is as difficult as finding solutions of these equations.
If one meets existence and uniqueness theorems in a mathematics course, ``then you know what they are good for'': one may one day have a problem that one must solve and then one is glad of any help. ``So one might say it is all terrible; it is great that one can do it at all: this is one of the great textbook examples where it does work'' \ts{12}{1}{1:17:34}--\ts{12}{1}{1:18:57}.
\end{coursenote}

\section{The shape of the orbit: qualitative discussion}\label{sec:tb-qual}

Now the qualitative discussion of the solution. The quantity $u(\varphi)=\frac{k}{\mu L_3^2}(1+\epsilon\cos\varphi)$ is positive for the physical orbit, and $\tilde r=1/u$ is the distance of the bodies as a function of the angle. Everything depends on $\epsilon$ (the lecturer draws, below each graph of $u$ over $0,\ \pi/2,\ \pi,\ 3\pi/2,\ 2\pi$, the graph of the distance $\tilde r$) \ts{12}{1}{1:19:38}--\ts{12}{1}{1:19:59}.

\paragraph{Case $\epsilon=0$: the circle.} The function $u$ is constant, $\tilde r=\mu L_3^2/k$: independent of the angle, the bodies keep the \emph{same distance}. If one fixes one body as reference, the other is, for every angle, at this same distance: ``a very particular special case'', a circular motion of one body around the other (and the centre of mass can move on uniformly in a straight line: we are in the system where it rests) \ts{12}{1}{1:19:59}--\ts{12}{1}{1:22:25}.

\paragraph{Case $0<\epsilon<1$: the ellipse.} The amplitude of the cosine is smaller than the constant, so $u$ stays positive and does not reach the axis (for $\epsilon=1$ it touches it); a negative $\epsilon$ merely flips the curve. The distance $\tilde r=1/u$ oscillates between a \emph{smallest} distance ($\varphi=0$, $p/(1+\epsilon)$) and a \emph{largest} one ($\varphi=\pi$, $p/(1-\epsilon)$), and comes back to the smallest distance after $2\pi$: ``it closes''.
(``Later we shall see that there is a further conserved quantity, the so-called \dots\ vector'' [the captions are garbled; presumably the Laplace--Runge--Lenz vector; in the Hamiltonian part of the lecture; (not checked), [?]] \ts{12}{1}{1:25:38}.) The relative motion is an \textbf{ellipse}: this can be checked by computation, \emph{homework}: verify the equation of the ellipse. The circle is $x^2/a^2+y^2/a^2=1$; the ellipse equation is $x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2=1$ with two semi-axes (if $a=b$ one gets the circle) \ts{12}{1}{1:22:25}--\ts{12}{1}{1:28:06}.

\begin{example}[The verification of the shape (not said in class; the lecturer leaves it as homework)]\label{ex:tb-conic}
From \eqref{eq:tb-orbit}, $\tilde r(1+\epsilon\cos\varphi)=p$, with $x=\tilde r\cos\varphi$, $y=\tilde r\sin\varphi$: $\tilde r=p-\epsilon x$, hence $x^2+y^2=(p-\epsilon x)^2$, that is
\[
  (1-\epsilon^2)\,x^2+2p\epsilon\,x+y^2\;=\;p^2 .
\]
For $\epsilon=0$ this is the circle $x^2+y^2=p^2$; for $0<\epsilon<1$ the coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ are positive and completing the square gives an ellipse (centre at $x=-p\epsilon/(1-\epsilon^2)$, semi-axes $a=p/(1-\epsilon^2)$ and $b=p/\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}$), with the origin (the body that is taken as reference) at a focus; for $\epsilon=1$ the term in $x^2$ drops out: $y^2=p^2-2px$, a \emph{parabola}; for $\epsilon>1$ the coefficient of $x^2$ is negative: a \emph{hyperbola}. This is the computation behind the claims of the lecture.
\end{example}
\begin{coursenote}[History: the ellipses of the planets (as said in class, not checked)]\label{cn:tb-kepler}
``One has seen it in the sky: it looked like circles, but if one looks closely they are ellipses; Kepler and so on had seen that they are ellipses, and nobody could explain why they are ellipses.'' That this comes out of the Newtonian theory, from this gravitational law, with the planets obeying exactly the same gravitational law as the apple: ``that was the big hit, that is what ultimately founded Newton's fame'', that he could compute it \ts{12}{1}{1:28:06}--\ts{12}{1}{1:28:47}.
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{Case $\epsilon=1$: the parabola.} Now the graph of $u$ touches the zero line at $\varphi=\pi$, so $\tilde r\to\infty$ as $\varphi\to\pi$; for every $\varphi<\pi$ the distance is finite. The orbit is therefore \emph{not closed any more}: the other body comes from far away, flies by and disappears for ever. ``I claim it is a parabola'' (the lecturer draws it badly, ``quite bad with chalk today''): the qualitative picture is right, and by recomputing as in \cref{ex:tb-conic} one gets exactly the parabola \ts{12}{1}{1:28:47}--\ts{12}{1}{1:31:56}.
(\emph{Homework}: put the solution into Cartesian coordinates and show it is a parabola.)

\paragraph{Case $\epsilon>1$: the hyperbola.} The oscillation of $u$ is now larger than the constant: $u$ becomes negative for $\cos\varphi<-1/\epsilon$, where the formula would give a negative ``distance'', which is not realised. The lecturer sketches it as a stretched, shifted cosine whose inverse goes through poles; the correct reading: the orbit exists only for those angles for which $1+\epsilon\cos\varphi>0$, and at the boundary $\varphi_\infty=\arccos(-1/\epsilon)$ the distance is already \emph{infinite}.
So here, for a \emph{finite} angle, the bodies are already infinitely far away (unlike the parabola, where infinity is reached only at $\pi$): ``one is already infinitely far away for a $\varphi$ smaller than $\pi$''; the directions for $\varphi\to\pm\varphi_\infty$ are parallel to \emph{asymptotes}: ``it is not asymptotic to the axis, since this is the distance and we change the angle'' \ts{12}{1}{1:31:56}--\ts{12}{1}{1:36:58}. This is the \textbf{hyperbola} (``you know what the hyperbola equation is: like the circle equation but with a minus in it''); the hyperbola has two branches, but only one is realised here.

These are the ways in which the two-body problem can be solved: if for example one mass is much larger than the other (Earth and a satellite), one has an ellipse; one may think about what this has to do with the focal points and so on: \emph{homework}, ``for we have solved the problem, one only has to insert'' \ts{12}{1}{1:36:58}--\ts{12}{1}{1:38:22}. One has then an ellipse motion, or a parabola, or a hyperbola: the latter two for things like comets which come once and disappear for ever. The parabola is the \emph{borderline case} $\epsilon=1$: the satellite needs to cough only once, in one direction and it is an ellipse, in the other and it is a hyperbola; so the parabola is a bit of a ``theoretical object'', a concrete object is never exactly a parabola (``too susceptible to disturbances'') \ts{12}{1}{1:38:22}--\ts{12}{1}{1:39:25}.

\begin{secondary}[Conic sections and over-interpretation (as said in class)]\label{sec:tb-conics}
All four orbits are \textbf{conic sections} (Kegelschnitte): take a double cone and cut it with a plane. Perpendicular to the axis one gets a circle; tilting the plane a little, an ellipse; tilting further, until the plane is parallel to a generating line of the cone, one gets a parabola (only one cut); tilting still further the plane cuts both halves of the cone and one gets a hyperbola, with two branches (the parabola has one).
(Etymology, ``as said in the lecture and in the German school lessons'': the Greek words ellipsis = omission, ``something is missing'', parabola = equality, hyperbola = exaggeration, ``more''; the lecturer says ``ellipse less, parabola equal, hyperbola more'' \ts{12}{1}{1:39:25}--\ts{12}{1}{1:41:29}; not checked.)

Now one could ask what is the deep mystical meaning of the fact that all these planet and comet orbits arise as conic sections; ``earlier, very much earlier, people understood this very deeply, and we of course say today: it is a coincidence''. It has nothing to do with the nature of things; we do not have to look for little cones everywhere to understand it. One must always ask whether the interpretation means something or is an \emph{over-interpretation}: in physics we have a problem in the real world, we model it, throw mathematics at it, and the mathematics guarantees that it is still the same problem in a quite different form;
then we interpret the result again, and we must be careful not to over-interpret it. It would surely be an over-interpretation that cones fundamentally mean something and that the planets move so because of them; it is a coincidence: the differential equation has this form. ``Perhaps in 300 years one will say: how sweet that they wrote it with the potential equation''; and how good that they did it with cones, but not so silly with differential equations: in physics, one wants to predict the future, so one has to know how systems change in time, that is, the \emph{derivatives with respect to time}; that physical laws take the form of differential equations is anchored in the idea of physics (it can be that something quite different comes one day, but this is quite stable).
The other (crystal spheres to which stars are glued, which conic section, and so on) is somewhat ad hoc; ``we must always ask what we are doing'' \ts{12}{1}{1:41:29}--\ts{12}{1}{1:43:55}.
\end{secondary}

\section{The time dependence of the motion}\label{sec:tb-time}

``This is one of the greatest achievements of Newtonian theory: to compute these things. But we have left an important question unanswered, and there are still six minutes'' \ts{12}{1}{1:43:55}: we have not said \emph{when} the planet is \emph{where}; we only said that when it is at this angle, it is this far away. If the planets left luminous traces, we now know the traces, but not \emph{when} the body is where. How do we find this out? We have already analysed the equation completely, so the information must lie in the angular momentum conservation, $L_3=r^2\dot\varphi$, an ordinary relation between angle and time
\ts{12}{1}{1:44:15}--\ts{12}{1}{1:45:17}. Write $\dot\varphi=\mathrm d\varphi/\mathrm dt$ and rearrange (``what only physicists do, but it is correct''; one can also write it more complicatedly):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-dt}
  \mathrm dt\;=\;\frac{\tilde r^2}{L_3}\,\mathrm d\varphi\;=\;\frac{\mu^2L_3^3}{k^2}\,\frac{\mathrm d\varphi}{\bigl(1+\epsilon\cos\varphi\bigr)^2},
\end{equation}
since $\tilde r=1/u=p/(1+\epsilon\cos\varphi)$ with $p=\mu L_3^2/k$ (the lecturer's constant in front of the integral is garbled in the captions: ``$L_3^4$'' and ``$k\,\mu$'' appear; recomputed here [?]). The right-hand side depends only on $\varphi$, the left-hand side is the time: integrate on both sides, from the initial time (one can take it as $0$; this is just the freedom of origin of the time) to $t$, and from $\varphi_0$ to $\varphi(t)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tb-time}
  t\;=\;\frac{\mu^2L_3^3}{k^2}\int_{\varphi_0}^{\varphi}\frac{\mathrm d\varphi'}{\bigl(1+\epsilon\cos\varphi'\bigr)^2}.
\end{equation}
The antiderivative of this function gives the \emph{time as a function of the angle} \ts{12}{1}{1:45:17}--\ts{12}{1}{1:47:44}.

\begin{coursenote}[On the integral; a correction (not said in class)]\label{cn:tb-integral}
The lecturer says that this integral \emph{exists}, but that one cannot express it in elementary terms (``there is no antiderivative that one could just write down; one can evaluate it numerically, discuss it for small eccentricity''; he promises exercises) \ts{12}{1}{1:47:44}--\ts{12}{1}{1:49:10}. In fact the integral \eqref{eq:tb-time} can be done in closed form in elementary functions (for instance with the substitution $\tau=\tan(\varphi/2)$);
what has no elementary expression is the \emph{inverse}, the angle as a function of the time (Kepler's equation) [my own remark, not said in class, I am confident of it but did not check it against a source].
\end{coursenote}
The importance of this: if one wants to \emph{test the theory}, everything may look fine, but if one compares it with the true planetary motion and says ``the orbits agree, but when it is where does not'', then the theory is wrong; ``so one must somehow work this out, and one can; but as said, by exercises and so on'' \ts{12}{1}{1:49:10}--\ts{12}{1}{1:49:31}. ``So one gets the orbits exactly, but the exact time dependence one would have to compute with more effort; that is not so bad. This is all that is known about the two-body problem'' \ts{12}{1}{1:49:31}.

\begin{theorem}[Summary: solution of the Newtonian two-body problem]\label{thm:tb-summary}
Two particles $(\gamma_i,m_i,m_i)$ with the gravitational interaction of \cref{ch:c11} in the (inertial) chart of the flat absolute space: the centre of mass $S=(m_1x_1+m_2x_2)/(m_1+m_2)$ moves uniformly and rectilinearly; the relative coordinate $r=x_2-x_1$ obeys $\mu\ddot r^\alpha=-k\,r^\alpha/\|r\|^3$ with $\mu=m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)$ and $k=G_{\rm N}m_1m_2$; its angular momentum components $L_\alpha=\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}r^\beta\dot r^\gamma$ are conserved; for $L\neq0$ the motion is planar and in the plane the orbit is $\tilde r(\varphi)=p/(1+\epsilon\cos\varphi)$ with $p=\mu L_3^2/k$,
a conic section (circle $\epsilon=0$, ellipse $0<\epsilon<1$, parabola $\epsilon=1$, hyperbola $\epsilon>1$); the time is given by \eqref{eq:tb-time}. (The lecture gives this as the result of the computation above.)
\end{theorem}

\section*{Key concepts}
\begin{keyconcepts}
\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What does the exclusion of the self-interaction say, and why is Newton's third law then a consequence of the field equation and not an axiom?
  \item Write the two coupled equations of the two-body problem. Why do the sum and the difference help? Define the centre of mass and the relative coordinate; what does the sum equation say?
  \item Define the reduced mass and the coupling constant and write the equation of the relative motion. Why are the centre of mass and the angular momentum no vectors here?
  \item Define $L_\alpha=\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}r^\beta\dot r^\gamma$ and prove $\dot L_\alpha=0$. Why does $L_\alpha r^\alpha=0$ say that the motion is planar?
  \item How does $L_3$ look in polar coordinates, and why does it make the equation of the relative motion a single equation $\mu\ddot r-\mu L_3^2/r^3=-k/r^2$?
  \item Why can one use the angle as parameter instead of the time? Show that $u=1/\tilde r$ gives $u''+u=k/(\mu L_3^2)$.
  \item Solve this equation (homogeneous plus special solution). Which orbits arise for $\epsilon=0$, $0<\epsilon<1$, $\epsilon=1$, $\epsilon>1$?
  \item How does one obtain the time dependence? Why is the harmonic oscillator in every textbook (according to the lecturer)?
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Concepts.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Exclusion of the self-interaction; Newton's third law as a consequence (\cref{def:tb-self,thm:tb-third}, \eqref{eq:tb-selfex}).
  \item Centre of mass, relative coordinate, reduced mass (\cref{def:tb-cm,def:tb-reduced}, \eqref{eq:tb-cm}, \eqref{eq:tb-mu}, \eqref{eq:tb-rel}).
  \item Angular momentum components and planar motion (\cref{def:tb-eps,def:tb-L}, \eqref{eq:tb-L}, \eqref{eq:tb-plane}, \eqref{eq:tb-L3}).
  \item Radial equation, the angle as parameter, the substitution $u=1/\tilde r$ (\eqref{eq:tb-rad}, \cref{def:tb-rtilde}, \eqref{eq:tb-ode}).
  \item The orbit and the conic sections (\eqref{eq:tb-orbit}, \cref{ex:tb-conic,thm:tb-summary}); time dependence \eqref{eq:tb-time}.
\end{itemize}
\end{keyconcepts}
