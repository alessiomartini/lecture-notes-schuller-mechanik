\section{The idea of the absolute space}\label{sec:as-idea}

Today's lecture deals with the absolute space. If you looked closely at the definition of the Newtonian spacetime of the last lecture you will have found that the notion of the \emph{absolute time} of Newton
was grasped in this definition in a technically very precise way, and so was the Newtonian spacetime; and we have seen that we can formulate the first axiom completely with these objects, i.e.\ the absolute time and the
compatible covariant derivative \ts{9}{1}{0:04}. We also found \emph{spaces}, defined through this absolute time, and the equation of motion of a particle with no charge, written in an inertial system,
looked quite simple. But the \emph{absolute space} of which Newton speaks we have not really seen, and it is indeed not yet contained in our definition of the
Newtonian spacetime. Schuller could have added it as a further requirement at the start, but it would have distracted, since it was not needed in the development so far; so today we refine the definition so that
Newton too would have been satisfied \ts{9}{1}{1:07}.

One has to distinguish very carefully today between \emph{spacetime} and \emph{space}, and until now we have several spaces. Recall from the first lecture the paraphrase of Newton: ``the absolute space is unchangeable \dots'' (he speaks of \emph{the} absolute space, not
of \emph{a} space, and by ``unchangeable'' he means among other things unchangeable \emph{in time}), and this we want to formalise today \ts{9}{1}{1:48}. What we reached in the last lecture was almost the whole way to it; a short recap
\ts{9}{1}{2:57}.

\begin{recall}[Newtonian spacetime and the first law (\cref{ch:c08})]
A Newtonian spacetime $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A,\nabla,t)$ is a four-dimensional smooth manifold with a torsion-free covariant derivative (equivalently a linear connection) and an absolute
time $t$ with Z1 ($\mathrm dt\ne0$ everywhere) and Z2 ($\nabla\,\mathrm dt=0$, a compatibility with $\nabla$). The first law: world lines of particles with vanishing charges are autoparallels.
\cref{def:nk-spacetime,ax:nk-first}\ts{9}{1}{3:17}.
\end{recall}
We visualised it in the charts of a \emph{stratified atlas} (the zeroth chart component is the absolute time $t$, ``physically nothing is in it, it is a pure simplification of the computation''): in such a chart the spacetime foliates into the spaces
$\mathcal R_\tau$, $\tau\in\R$, the set of all events whose absolute time is $\tau$; ``I have drawn only three of them as representatives, between them lie the others'' \ts{9}{1}{4:27}. A particle is a world line (with all tangent vectors
future-pointing, so that no particle runs backwards into the past) together with a mass and charges; the first law makes the world line of a particle with vanishing charges an autoparallel \ts{9}{1}{8:18}. And its
chart representation $q^a=x^a\circ\gamma$ gave: for the zeroth component $\ddot q^0=0$, so the parameter is an affine function of the absolute time; for the spatial ones, in an inertial system,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:as-autopar}
  \ddot q^\alpha(\lambda)+\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma}\bigl(\gamma(\lambda)\bigr)\,\dot q^\beta(\lambda)\,\dot q^\gamma(\lambda)\;=\;0 ,\qquad\alpha,\beta,\gamma=1,2,3 ,
\end{equation}
where the other terms ($\Gamma^\alpha{}_{00}\dot q^0\dot q^0$ and the mixed ones) vanish in an inertial system (the non-acceleration and non-rotation conditions of \cref{def:nk-inertial}), and the ones with upper index $0$ vanish by the choice of the stratified atlas
\ts{9}{1}{9:21}--\ts{9}{1}{11:49}.

\begin{coursenote}[The factor 2 and the choice $a=1$, $b=0$ (student remark)]
A student remarks that the mixed term in \eqref{eq:as-autopar} should carry a factor $2$: $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{0\gamma}\dot q^0\dot q^\gamma+\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\beta0}\dot q^\beta\dot q^0=2\Gamma^\alpha{}_{0\gamma}\dot q^0\dot q^\gamma$,
by the symmetry in the lower indices (torsion freeness); Schuller: right, a very good observation; ``I could write the term like this, but because $\Gamma$ is symmetric in the two lower indices (which we demanded) there
is a factor two''; here it does not matter since the whole mixed term vanishes in an inertial system \ts{9}{1}{14:58}. A second remark by the lecturer: one may without loss of generality set $a=1$ and $b=0$
in $t(\gamma(\lambda))=a\lambda+b$ (the affine parametrisation of \cref{thm:nk-affine}): this is only the choice of the unit and the zero of the time measurement (seconds or hours); then the dot is the derivative with respect to the absolute time and $\dot q^0=1$.
The warning: one must remember that the equation \eqref{eq:as-autopar} has the stated form only for this choice (otherwise the factors $a^2$ and $a\cdot a$ appear, which can be divided out since the right-hand side is $0$; ``only when there is a force on the right they cannot be thrown out any more'') \ts{9}{1}{16:04}.
\end{coursenote}

\subsection{Many spaces, but not yet \emph{the} absolute space}

Now see what \eqref{eq:as-autopar} says. In an inertial system it looks as if we could restrict ourselves to the purely spatial components of the curve: we do not need the spacetime curve, we can solve the autoparallel equation in space
\ts{9}{1}{11:49}. That is almost right, except for one decisive detail: the $\Gamma$'s depend on the point at which the curve passes, they form a \emph{field}; one must know where on the manifold they are evaluated, i.e.\ along the curve
\ts{9}{1}{12:10}. The $\dot q^\alpha$ are not the yellow tangent vectors of the picture (these have components $\dot q^a$, $a=0,\dots,3$): they are the \emph{projections} of the tangent vector onto the space, $\alpha=1,2,3$ \ts{9}{1}{12:31}.
When one solves the equation one does not stay in one of the spaces: moving along the curve one must take the $\Gamma$ in one space, then in the next, then in the next, and nobody guarantees, by our
definition so far, that $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma}$ does not \emph{depend on the space} through which the particle passes: the particle exists all the time and so passes through different spaces, because ``time ticks'' \ts{9}{1}{13:35}.
So in the picture as developed last time everything works wonderfully and one can calculate with it, but there is no talk of \emph{the} absolute space, and one that is moreover unchangeable: one may think of the spaces each with its
$\Gamma$, but for my particle this does not play a role: looking at one space I would have to look at a time-dependent $\Gamma$. We have many spaces, but not the absolute space \ts{9}{1}{14:16}.

What we will do in this lecture is to put one more additional condition on a Newtonian spacetime; for terminological clarity it is then called a \emph{Newtonian spacetime with absolute space} \ts{9}{1}{17:27}. We shall find that
only a Newtonian spacetime which admits a \textbf{global inertial system} can be such: recall what that was, an atlas in whose every chart the $\Gamma$'s vanish (the non-acceleration and non-rotation conditions); it need not exist at all (there is none in the
presence of an inhomogeneous gravitational field). But we shall find that \emph{only if} such a global inertial system exists is there also an absolute space; hence the title: ``the absolute space, a very useful inertial-system fiction'': very useful, but a fiction that
one can realise only in an inertial system \ts{9}{1}{17:49}. That brings us back to the space known from the first axiom, and that is why all Newtonian axioms in the standard formulation come with the proviso ``in an inertial system''
\ts{9}{1}{18:51}.

\begin{secondary}[Why all this effort if one comes back to the absolute space? (as said in class)]
One may ask: ``for heaven's sake, if we come back to the inertial system anyway, why did we have to take so much trouble, only to return to the absolute space today?'' Today it will be shown pictorially how one can squeeze all these spaces together
like a concertina and discuss everything in a single space, as one has presumably always imagined it, doing Newtonian mechanics in space instead of in spacetime. The answer is clear: \emph{first}, there is not always an inertial system, and then one must still be able to do
mechanics, where what is done today cannot be applied. \emph{Second}, one may not want to look at the world from an inertial system at all: as a child one wanted, by all means, to look at the world from a non-inertial system, and the parents had to buy one ticket after another to ride the carousel once more
(``it is a great pleasure to look at the world from a non-inertial system; as a child one knows it''). One cannot build a theory, the model theory for theoretical physics, on a fiction that first is not always realisable and second is not always wanted; therefore the assumptions leading to it are presented
step by step, so that this is clear once and for all \ts{9}{1}{19:12}.
\end{secondary}

\section{The spatial spacetime metric}\label{sec:as-metric}

To study the absolute space we introduce a geometric object with a very appealing name: a \emph{spatial spacetime metric} (r\"aumliche Raumzeit-Metrik) \ts{9}{1}{20:54}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: spatial spacetime metric (r\"aumliche Raumzeit-Metrik)]\label{def:as-G}
Let $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A,\nabla,t)$ be a Newtonian spacetime and, as always from now on, $\mathcal A$ a stratified atlas (``I shall mention it less and less, so that our friends the formulae become simpler'').
A \textbf{spatial spacetime metric} is a $(0,2)$ tensor field $G$ \emph{on the spacetime} (the four-dimensional $M$; today one must watch like a lynx whether an object lives on the spacetime, on one of the spaces or later on the absolute space)
whose component functions in every chart $(U,x)\in\mathcal A$ have the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:as-G}
  G_{(x)\,ab}\;=\;g_{(x)\,\alpha\beta}\;\delta^\alpha{}_a\;\delta^\beta{}_b ,\qquad a,b=0,\dots,3 ,
\end{equation}
where the $g_{(x)\,\alpha\beta}$, $\alpha,\beta=1,2,3$, are smooth functions on the chart domain $U$ such that at every point of $U$ the $3\times3$ matrix $(g_{\alpha\beta})$ is \textbf{symmetric}, $g_{\alpha\beta}=g_{\beta\alpha}$, and \textbf{positive definite}
(equivalently: invertible) \ts{9}{1}{21:50}.
\end{definition}
(The summation convention is still understood: ``one must now almost say that we still sum over the repeated Greek indices, although we had agreed it only over indices that run over the whole manifold; we agree that also if the indices run only from $1$ to $3$''
\ts{9}{1}{24:42}. What are the $g_{\alpha\beta}$? ``Almost arbitrary, but not quite'': smooth functions on the chart domain, giving at each point of the domain a positive definite matrix \ts{9}{1}{25:24}. The positive definiteness is nothing but the condition
that the thing is invertible, together with positivity \ts{9}{1}{26:09}.)
So we have here the components of a spacetime $(0,2)$ tensor field, but built from fewer components, and to get spacetime indices anyway we attach the deltas; this is obviously chart dependent, since each chart decides which direction is the $0$-direction and which are the $1,2,3$
directions; but if it looks like this in every chart, one calls it a spatial spacetime metric \ts{9}{1}{26:51}.

\begin{intuition}[What $G$ does: it sees only the spatial part of a spacetime vector]\label{int:as-G}
Take two vector fields $X,Y$ on the spacetime. In a chart $X=X^m\,\partial/\partial x^m=X^0\,\partial/\partial x^0+X^\alpha\,\partial/\partial x^\alpha$, with the summation convention split up, and similarly $Y$: every vector at a point
(which lies in a unique space $\mathcal R_{t(p)}$) decomposes into a \emph{spatial} component $X_{\rm spatial}=X^\alpha\partial_\alpha$ (the part lying in the space) and a ``temporal'' one, $X^0\partial_0$
(what stands out of the space into spacetime); the temporal one is not a length but the component $X^0$ times the basis vector \ts{9}{1}{27:54}. If now one inserts both into $G$, by \eqref{eq:as-G},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:as-G-projection}
  G(X,Y)\;=\;G_{ab}X^aY^b\;=\;g_{\alpha\beta}\,X^\alpha Y^\beta\;=\;g\bigl(X_{\rm spatial},Y_{\rm spatial}\bigr),
\end{equation}
since the deltas pick only the spatial index values \ts{9}{1}{30:40}. So $G$ is \emph{blind to the time component} of a vector and sees only the projected part on the space; ``if you have a spatial spacetime metric you need not care about first projecting and then computing in space, the spatial spacetime metric does it for you''
\ts{9}{1}{32:43}. For two vectors whose projections onto the space are $X_{\rm spatial}$ and $Y_{\rm spatial}$, it returns the inner product (``an inner product, as you know them'') of the \emph{projections}; but since $g_{\alpha\beta}$ may be different at every
point of spacetime, it may a priori define a different inner product on every space $\mathcal R_\tau$ \ts{9}{1}{33:24}.
\end{intuition}
The key sentence: a single spatial spacetime metric $G$ is one mathematical object on spacetime which contains the information of a purely spatial metric on one space, possibly another purely spatial metric on another space, and so on: it holds the whole concertina of
purely spatial inner products, which are ``inner products as you know them, not on spacetime but on each single space'' \ts{9}{1}{34:46}. In particular, one cannot compute with it the angle between two future-pointing vectors of spacetime: they get projected at once into the corresponding
subspace and only then does one form an inner product \ts{9}{1}{35:28}. We do \emph{not} have a metric on spacetime, only a spatial spacetime metric, with content an inner product on each space. Why? It is defined so; and the question is what to do with it; for now it is a purely technical
auxiliary construct, ``you will soon see why it is wonderful'' \ts{9}{1}{36:10}.

\begin{coursenote}[Why $G$ is not a metric on spacetime]
Terminology can be invented as one likes; ``it is always bad: a spatial spacetime metric is not a spacetime metric''. A metric on spacetime would have to be non-degenerate on the whole space. But take a purely temporal vector $X=X^0\partial_0$ in a chart and insert it twice into $G$:
the projection onto the space is $0$, and one gets $0$ although $X\ne0$. A metric demands (positive definiteness) that a non-zero vector inserted twice give a non-zero result; this fails here. So $G$ gives a metric \emph{on the space} but not on spacetime
\ts{9}{1}{38:08}.
\end{coursenote}

\section{Newtonian spacetime with absolute space}\label{sec:as-nrz}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: Newtonian spacetime with absolute space]\label{def:as-nrz-as}
A \textbf{Newtonian spacetime with absolute space} is a sextuple $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A,\nabla,t,G)$ such that
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A,\nabla,t)$ is a Newtonian spacetime (so a quintuple of the type we already listed; ``a particularly demanding member of the family of Newtonian spacetimes, which has to fulfil extra conditions'') \ts{9}{1}{36:57};
  \item $G$ is a spatial spacetime metric, and
  \item (\textbf{axiom A}, the compatibility with $\nabla$, ``for the absolute space as we had Z1 and Z2 for the absolute time'') $\nabla G=0$: the covariant derivative of $G$ in every direction vanishes, $\nabla_XG=0$ for all vector fields $X$
  \ts{9}{1}{39:30}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
What does the compact notation mean? One may insert any vector field $X$; $G$ is a $(0,2)$ tensor field, which stays a $(0,2)$ tensor field after covariant differentiation, but it is such a one that the covariant derivative in any direction at any point of the manifold always gives $0$ (``$G$ is not changed along the direction of
the vector field, by the covariant derivative'') \ts{9}{1}{39:50}. This axiom links the object $G$ with the object $\nabla$: ``whenever one has a tuple of several objects one wants one object to speak to the other'' \ts{9}{1}{40:11}. In the lecture it is made precise, once, as: the covariant derivative of
tensor fields was always defined in a chart \ts{9}{1}{40:31}.
The remark made several times and which one cannot make often enough: a spacetime $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A,\nabla,t)$ does \emph{not necessarily} admit such a spatial spacetime metric. Why not, the following computation will make clear \ts{9}{1}{40:52}.

\begin{coursenote}[A student asks what ``does not change along'' means]
A student asks what ``not changing along the direction'' means: there are other kinds of derivative, for instance one that says that something does not change under parallel transport or ``invariantly''. Schuller: here it means that the \emph{covariant derivative} vanishes
(the background information on other derivatives is given only to say that one should not mix them) \ts{9}{1}{42:07}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{aside}[Why define it before understanding it: the joke of mathematics]
How does one arrive at such a condition? First, write it down: the nicest thing about it is that it is crystal clear what it means because every single object is crystal clear defined. If one defines the notion this way one \emph{cannot argue} about what a Newtonian spacetime with absolute space is;
one could argue whether it is \emph{the} absolute space Newton meant (we come back to this), but technically ``we can form notions and imagine something in them, but if you do mathematics and physics for a long time you become very modest''; even in so highly formalised systems, when you want to compute something new, you have of course an intuition (without intuition one does not get far),
but it happens often that one sits with a colleague, one says ``it must be so, I know, it is completely clear, look, the vector is this and that'', and depending on the complication one computes a whole day, and at the very end someone laughs: the one who laughs is the one who was wrong
\ts{9}{1}{42:28}. ``Like a good joke'': one is told something and one thinks in one direction, then one recognises that the story can be interpreted completely differently; that is the punch line; one laughs at oneself for having been led astray; ``it is exactly like that in mathematics''. In a caf\'e this is
spectacular: it is suddenly clear, by the exact mathematical definitions agreed upon, that it must be as the other said, although one's perfect, strongly trained intuition said otherwise. ``No idea about the absolute space decides this case, the definition does, and that is the whole point of having a theory;
without a theory nobody ever laughs'' \ts{9}{1}{43:51}.
\end{aside}

\subsection{Evaluating the condition $\nabla G=0$ in a chart}\label{ssec:as-eval}

Now we must find out what the condition really means and how it fits with our intuition, above all with the spaces \ts{9}{1}{45:15}. We choose a chart of a stratified atlas, so that our intuition looks like the pictures, and evaluate the condition in a chart
\ts{9}{1}{45:46}. The condition holds for the covariant derivative in the direction of an arbitrary vector field, hence in particular for the direction of the $m$-th basis vector field $\partial/\partial x^m$ induced by the chart; the result is a $(0,2)$ spacetime tensor field and has to vanish
\ts{9}{1}{46:27}. For the components $a,b$, by the rule for a $(0,2)$ field (a minus for every lower index, the \emph{last} index of $\Gamma$ goes with the direction of differentiation; ``this you now all know in your sleep'', that is not so; ``take care'') \ts{9}{1}{47:08}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:as-nablaG}
  0=(\nabla_{\partial_m}G)_{ab}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^m}G_{ab}\;-\;\Gamma^s{}_{a\,m}\,G_{sb}\;-\;\Gamma^s{}_{b\,m}\,G_{as}
\end{equation}
(``one must replace one index of $G$ at a time, keep all others, the other index of $\Gamma$ goes with the direction $m$, and $s$ is summed'') \ts{9}{1}{47:50}. Insert $G_{ab}=g_{\alpha\beta}\delta^\alpha{}_a\delta^\beta{}_b$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:as-nablaG2}
  0=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^m}\bigl(g_{\alpha\beta}\bigr)\delta^\alpha{}_a\delta^\beta{}_b
  -\Gamma^s{}_{a\,m}\,g_{\sigma\beta}\,\delta^\sigma{}_s\,\delta^\beta{}_b
  -\Gamma^s{}_{b\,m}\,g_{\alpha\sigma}\,\delta^\alpha{}_a\,\delta^\sigma{}_s
\end{equation}
\ts{9}{1}{49:13}. ``Here we learn nothing yet, so we go on''; ``a pause to look at what has been written is always super: you can compute for pages if you just rush on, but if you look at the line after each one you simplify at once''. The derivative acts on the product but the deltas are only
zeros and ones, so they can be pulled out \ts{9}{1}{50:15}.

How many equations are these? $a,b$ run from $0$ to $3$ and the pair is symmetric; the lecture counts (loosely) ``in four dimensions six possibilities times four directions, which makes 24 equations'' (see the coursenote); we write them out by cases \ts{9}{1}{51:16}.

\begin{coursenote}[Counting the equations]
The lecture says ``24 equations'' (apparently $4$ directions $m$ times $6$ independent components $g_{\alpha\beta}$). Counting all pairs $(a,b)$ of spacetime indices one has $4\times10=40$; of these the ones with $a=b=0$ are trivial and the others reduce to the
cases below. So the count of the lecture (6 per direction) refers to the non-trivial $\alpha\beta$-block of \eqref{eq:as-nablaG}; [?].
\end{coursenote}

Case by case, using that the stratified atlas already gives $\Gamma^0{}_{ab}=0$ (\cref{ch:c08}, \eqref{eq:nk-Z2-comp}) \ts{9}{1}{51:58}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Direction $m=0$, $(a,b)=(0,0)$.} Every term has a $\delta^\alpha{}_0$ or $\delta^\beta{}_0$ with Greek indices $1$ to $3$, hence is $0$: the
        equation has no content, ``trivially satisfied'' \ts{9}{1}{54:25}.
  \item \emph{Direction $m=0$, $(a,b)=(0,\beta)$.} The first term and the last (since $a=0$) vanish; what remains is
        $-\Gamma^s{}_{00}\,g_{\sigma\beta}\delta^\sigma{}_s=-\Gamma^\alpha{}_{00}\,g_{\alpha\beta}=0$ (with $s$ restricted to $1,2,3$ by the $\delta$). Since $g_{\alpha\beta}$ is positive definite, hence invertible, this is
        equivalent to
        \[
          \Gamma^\alpha{}_{00}=0\qquad(\alpha=1,2,3),
        \]
        a real condition, ``the left-hand side is not necessarily zero but if I want this condition for the spacetime with absolute space, this has to vanish'' \ts{9}{1}{55:26}--\ts{9}{1}{57:08}.
  \item \emph{Direction $m=0$, $(a,b)=(\alpha,\beta)$.} Here quite a bit remains: with $m=0$, $a=\alpha$, $b=\beta$,
        \[
          \frac{\partial}{\partial x^0}\,g_{\alpha\beta}-\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\alpha\,0}\,g_{\sigma\beta}-\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\beta\,0}\,g_{\alpha\sigma}=0 .
        \]
        \ts{9}{1}{57:29}
  \item \emph{Direction $m=\mu\in\{1,2,3\}$, $(a,b)=(0,\beta)$.} Then $-\Gamma^\sigma{}_{0\,\mu}\,g_{\sigma\beta}=0$, again equivalent, by the positive definiteness of $g$, to
        \[
          \Gamma^\alpha{}_{0\mu}\;=\;\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu0}\;=\;0\qquad(\alpha,\mu=1,2,3)
        \]
        (the equality by torsion freeness) \ts{9}{1}{59:54}--\ts{9}{1}{1:01:20}. (For $a=b=0$ one finds again a non-condition, ``nothing happens since $a$, $b$ are $0$'' \ts{9}{1}{59:13}.)
  \item \emph{Direction $m=\mu$, $(a,b)=(\alpha,\beta)$.} The last remaining condition, which we shall evaluate after the break:
        \[
          \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\,g_{\alpha\beta}-\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\alpha\,\mu}\,g_{\sigma\beta}-\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\beta\,\mu}\,g_{\alpha\sigma}=0 .
        \]
        \ts{9}{1}{1:03:24}
\end{enumerate}
Now combine. From 2 and 4, the $\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\alpha0}$ in condition 3 vanish, so condition 3 becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:as-time-indep}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial x^0}\,g_{\alpha\beta}=0,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\frac{\partial g_{\alpha\beta}}{\partial t}=0
\end{equation}
(in a stratified chart $x^0$ is the absolute time) \ts{9}{1}{1:01:40}--\ts{9}{1}{1:02:01}: ``what do we see here? The spatial components, which until now could have differed from space to space, \emph{do not change} if I go to another space: they do not change in the absolute time. So
we have already found that, if this condition holds, we have an \emph{unchangeable metric on each space}, the same on every space'' \ts{9}{1}{1:02:22}.

And the two conditions $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{00}=0$ and $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{0\mu}=0$ together: ``what kind of condition is that? We called it the \textbf{inertial system}'', the non-acceleration and non-rotation conditions of \cref{def:nk-inertial} (with $\Gamma^0{}_{ab}=0$, $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{00}=\Gamma^\alpha{}_{0\mu}=0$)
\ts{9}{1}{1:02:43}. So we have learnt there is a time-independent spatial metric \emph{and} this is the beautiful idea of Newton, that space and its geometry are unchangeable in time; but this is possible \emph{only in an inertial system}: if one has a spacetime whose covariant derivative is such that one cannot find any charts in which $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{00}$ and $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{0\mu}$
vanish at the same time, this condition can never be fulfilled \ts{9}{1}{1:03:04}.
\begin{coursenote}[On the logic: $G$ forces the chart to be inertial]
Written precisely: the axiom $\nabla G=0$, evaluated in a chart of the stratified atlas in which $G$ has the form \eqref{eq:as-G}, implies the inertial conditions $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{00}=\Gamma^\alpha{}_{0\mu}=0$ in that chart. So the charts in which $G$ has the form \eqref{eq:as-G}
\emph{are} the charts of a global inertial system, and a Newtonian spacetime with absolute space exists only if the spacetime admits such an atlas. The lecture states the implication in this direction (``only if there is a global inertial system is there an absolute space'', \ts{9}{1}{1:06:39}).
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{The last condition, solved for $\Gamma$.} After the break the lecturer returns to the last condition (5): it can be satisfied only if the spatial $\Gamma$'s are given through $g$ and its derivatives, in the form in which it has appeared twice already on the problem sheets \ts{9}{1}{1:05:09}.
(A correction by a student in the first bracket on the board, ``a $g_{\mu\nu}$ stands there'', after the break \ts{9}{1}{1:05:56}.)
Condition 5 reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:as-last}
  \partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta}\;=\;\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\alpha\mu}\,g_{\sigma\beta}+\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\beta\mu}\,g_{\alpha\sigma},\qquad\alpha,\beta,\mu=1,2,3 .
\end{equation}
It looks unwieldy, but there is a trick, ``one must either know it or try for a week'' \ts{9}{1}{1:13:06}. The equation is true for all index values, so we may \emph{rename the free indices}: no new equation arises, just the same one in a new dress (Schuller makes a point
on the difference between ``the same'' and ``equal'': in the canteen, ``I would like the same dish as my predecessor'' versus ``the equal dish''; here we have three times the \emph{same} equation which is not \emph{equal}, so ``I may add them'')
\ts{9}{1}{1:13:27}. Write the equation \eqref{eq:as-last} with the index triple $(\mu,\alpha,\beta)$ permuted cyclically:
\begin{align*}
  \partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta}&=\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\alpha\mu}\,g_{\sigma\beta}+\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\beta\mu}\,g_{\alpha\sigma},\\
  \partial_\alpha g_{\beta\mu}&=\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\beta\alpha}\,g_{\sigma\mu}+\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\mu\alpha}\,g_{\beta\sigma},\\
  \partial_\beta g_{\mu\alpha}&=\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\mu\beta}\,g_{\sigma\alpha}+\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\alpha\beta}\,g_{\mu\sigma}
\end{align*}
\ts{9}{1}{1:13:48}. Now add the second and third and \emph{subtract} the first (``before I add I multiply the first equation by $-1$; everything is still $=0$ so it stays correct'') \ts{9}{1}{1:16:20}. Before computing blindly, one looks at which terms look alike \ts{9}{1}{1:17:29}:
using the symmetry of $g$ and the torsion freeness $\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\alpha\beta}=\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\beta\alpha}$ (``we are allowed to exchange the two lower indices'') the terms cancel pairwise (spelled out in the coursenote below), and what survives is twice one and the same term (``there is two times the same, so I put a $2$ in front''):
\[
  \partial_\alpha g_{\beta\mu}+\partial_\beta g_{\mu\alpha}-\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta}\;=\;2\,\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\alpha\beta}\,g_{\sigma\mu}
\]
\ts{9}{1}{1:21:04}. This is nice: the $\Gamma$ occurs only once, and we can solve for it.
\begin{coursenote}[The cancellation, spelled out]
Set $\Gamma_{\beta\alpha\mu}:=g_{\beta\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\alpha\mu}$ (symmetric in the last two indices). The three equations read
$\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta}=\Gamma_{\beta\alpha\mu}+\Gamma_{\alpha\beta\mu}$, $\partial_\alpha g_{\beta\mu}=\Gamma_{\mu\beta\alpha}+\Gamma_{\beta\mu\alpha}$,
$\partial_\beta g_{\mu\alpha}=\Gamma_{\alpha\mu\beta}+\Gamma_{\mu\alpha\beta}$. In the combination (second $+$ third $-$ first) the terms $\Gamma_{\beta\mu\alpha}-\Gamma_{\beta\alpha\mu}$ and $\Gamma_{\alpha\mu\beta}-\Gamma_{\alpha\beta\mu}$ cancel by the symmetry in the
last two indices, and $\Gamma_{\mu\beta\alpha}+\Gamma_{\mu\alpha\beta}=2\Gamma_{\mu\alpha\beta}$ remains. (Added; not said in class: the lecture does this on the board with the same renamings.)
\end{coursenote}
To get rid of $g_{\sigma\mu}$ on the right: $g_{\alpha\beta}$ is positive definite, so there is an inverse $g^{\alpha\beta}$, with two indices up, such that $g^{\mu\lambda}g_{\lambda\nu}=\delta^\mu{}_\nu$ (as on the problem sheet): a positive definite matrix has an inverse matrix; it is again symmetric
(the inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric, finite-dimensional) \ts{9}{1}{1:22:27}. Multiplying both sides with $g^{\lambda\mu}$ and summing over $\mu$ gives
\begin{theorem}[The spatial connection coefficients are the Christoffel symbols of $g$]\label{thm:as-christoffel}
In a Newtonian spacetime with absolute space, in a chart of the stratified atlas, the purely spatial components of the linear connection are determined uniquely by the spatial metric:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:as-christoffel}
  \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\alpha\beta}\;=\;\frac12\,g^{\lambda\mu}\Bigl(\frac{\partial g_{\mu\beta}}{\partial x^\alpha}+\frac{\partial g_{\alpha\mu}}{\partial x^\beta}-\frac{\partial g_{\alpha\beta}}{\partial x^\mu}\Bigr),\qquad\alpha,\beta,\lambda=1,2,3
\end{equation}
\ts{9}{1}{1:23:10}--\ts{9}{1}{1:25:52}.
\end{theorem}
It is worth learning this formula by heart \ts{9}{1}{1:26:22}. The compatibility condition (that the spatial spacetime metric is covariantly constant), as defined in the definition, demands a unique relation between the components of the absolute space ($g_{\alpha\beta}$ and its inverse, already known as a
time-independent metric on every space) and the purely spatial components of the linear connection of the spacetime; they can be computed in a Newtonian spacetime with absolute space purely from $g_{\mu\nu}$ \ts{9}{1}{1:26:42}.

\begin{coursenote}[The formula \eqref{eq:as-christoffel} and the transcript]
In the transcript the formula is dictated with garbled indices; it is reconstructed here by the derivation above, which gives the usual Christoffel symbols of $g$. The remark ``the last bracket has a minus sign, which one can see by looking
at it'' is missing in the audio. [?]
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{Counting the connection coefficients.} The lecture then lists all $\Gamma^a{}_{bc}$ of the four-dimensional spacetime and checks that the list is complete: $\Gamma^0{}_{bc}$ (10 components, symmetric in $b,c\in\{0,\dots,3\}$), $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{00}$ (3), $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{0\beta}$ (counted with the symmetry:
$3\times3=9$) and $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma}$ ($3\times6=18$); in total $10+3+9+18=40=4\times10$, ``so all those we want are there, in the way we want them'' \ts{9}{1}{1:30:27}. (The lecturer miscounts twice on the board, double-counts the mixed ones, and finds the error himself: ``it was a case in which everything was right and the check was wrong; there are such too'' \ts{9}{1}{1:34:54}.)
Which are non-zero? The $\Gamma^0{}_{bc}$ vanish because we chose the stratified atlas (``only for this the computations were so transparent; one could do it in a non-stratified atlas, but it is summer semester, for such things one needs a cold winter day''), the $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{00}$ and $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{0\beta}$ because
we are in an inertial system (``the whole thing works only in the inertial system''), and the last 18 are \eqref{eq:as-christoffel}, \emph{completely determined by the components of the spatial spacetime metric} \ts{9}{1}{1:35:14}.

\subsection{Interim conclusions}

\begin{theorem}[What a Newtonian spacetime with absolute space implies]\label{thm:as-summary}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A Newtonian spacetime with absolute space (the sextuple, ``the one to which Newton philosophically would have agreed'') can exist only if the underlying spacetime admits a \textbf{global inertial system} \ts{9}{1}{1:06:16}.
  \item Even then, a spatial spacetime metric $G$ is defined only with respect to such an inertial system: one must actually go into the inertial system and make the projection there \ts{9}{1}{1:09:05}.
  \item In it, the components $g_{\alpha\beta}$ are independent of the absolute time \eqref{eq:as-time-indep}, and are, up to the Christoffel formula \eqref{eq:as-christoffel}, the whole spatial part of the connection \ts{9}{1}{1:11:14}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
Comments. Books sometimes say that one of the assumptions of Newtonian theory is the existence of a global inertial system; ``fine, if one assumes it, but one should at least know what is assumed''. Schuller had argued since the first lecture (and will show next time) that in a homogeneous gravitational field one can write the
gravitational force as a force, but then the first axiom loses its domain of validity, since gravitation acts on all bodies, so that no force-free bodies exist: ``if you really take everything seriously and compute in a modern way, you cannot say there is always a global inertial system; that in some computations it is assumed, all well and good, but
one must know what one assumes'' \ts{9}{1}{1:07:00}.

\begin{secondary}[The carousel (non-inertial systems) and Coriolis forces]
Suppose we had no gravitation but a carousel and you step onto it: one cannot say ``I describe the world in the coordinates as my carousel gives them to me, but the world is still metric, the space is still metric and the same metric stays'': no, in these coordinates a lot more information must go in, and
``actually it is a big catastrophe, you must not do it''; one goes to the spacetime formalism and everything runs smoothly. In books one finds the carousel computed with a trick, with extra terms that are then interpreted; ``actually you may not compute what is done there, since there the metric becomes time dependent, whereas our metric is not time
dependent, and Newton speaks of the absolute \emph{unchangeable} space; in one way or another one gets away from this, here we put it the other way round, and in my opinion it is the clearest way'' \ts{9}{1}{1:09:47}. Moreover, from the inertial system one can transform by a spacetime transformation to a rotating subsystem; then
the $\Gamma$'s (above all the second and the third line of the list) are no longer zero: ``this goes on a problem sheet'': for a rigid rotation with respect to our spatial metric, $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{00}$ and $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{0\beta}$ give the \emph{centrifugal} and the \emph{Coriolis} acceleration, ``you have heard of them and now you can compute them technically''
\ts{9}{1}{1:37:42}.
\end{secondary}

\section{The absolute space}\label{sec:as-absolute}

So we arrive at the main notion, the \textbf{absolute space} \ts{9}{1}{1:28:07}. Until now we did not have it: we only had a Newtonian spacetime \emph{with absolute space}, and the absolute space itself follows only from it \ts{9}{1}{1:28:40}.

\emph{Setting.} Take a global inertial system, with $(M,\mathcal O,\mathcal A,\nabla,t,G)$ a Newtonian spacetime with absolute space, $G_{ab}=g_{\alpha\beta}\delta^\alpha{}_a\delta^\beta{}_b$ \ts{9}{1}{1:29:02}; the components of the linear connection in the charts of the inertial system are then those listed
above. The space $\mathcal R_\tau=\{p\in M\mid t(p)=\tau\}$ is a subset of $M$, so it inherits structure \ts{9}{1}{1:39:29}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the \textbf{subspace topology} $\mathcal O|_{\mathcal R_\tau}$ (a subset of spacetime; ``we have seen how, on a problem sheet''), making it a topological manifold in its own right \ts{9}{1}{1:40:37};
  \item the \textbf{subspace atlas}: from the atlas of the inertial system, for each chart $(U,x)$ the chart $(U\cap\mathcal R_\tau,\,x^1,x^2,x^3)$, i.e.\ restricted to the space and keeping only the three spatial component maps (``this one does not do explicitly, but it is clear it works'')
        \ts{9}{1}{1:41:18}.
\end{enumerate}
Then $(\mathcal R_\tau,\mathcal O|_{\mathcal R_\tau},\mathcal A|_{\mathcal R_\tau})$ is a smooth three-dimensional manifold, for every $\tau$, ``a smoothly parametrised family of three-dimensional manifolds, all lying in your spacetime, which you had imagined all along; this is only the technical definition''
\ts{9}{1}{1:42:20}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the spatial metric $g$ on a space]\label{def:as-g}
On the smooth manifold $\mathcal R_\tau$ define the $(0,2)$ tensor field $g$ by its component functions in the charts above,
$g_{\alpha\beta}$ (the same functions as in \eqref{eq:as-G}). The charts must be those of a \emph{stratified inertial} atlas. $g$ is then a symmetric, positive definite $(0,2)$ tensor field, now \emph{on the space} and not on spacetime. It is called the \textbf{spatial metric}
(r\"aumliche Metrik), \ts{9}{1}{1:43:24}.
\end{definition}
The construction is possible only in an inertial system, as the lecture stresses \ts{9}{1}{1:44:06}.

\begin{theorem}[The spatial metric does not depend on $\tau$; the absolute space]\label{thm:as-absolute}
In a Newtonian spacetime with absolute space the spatial metric $g$ of \cref{def:as-g} is \emph{the same on every space} $\mathcal R_\tau$: it does not depend on $\tau$. Hence all the spaces $\mathcal R_\tau$ may be identified with one single space, equipped with one metric $g$; this is the
\textbf{absolute space} \ts{9}{1}{1:45:09}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
It is what we have just computed: the condition $\nabla G=0$ gives \eqref{eq:as-time-indep}, $\partial g_{\alpha\beta}/\partial t=0$, in the charts of the inertial system \ts{9}{1}{1:45:30}. \qedhere
\end{proof}
Hence the symbol $\tau$ plays no role: ``I can identify all these spaces'' \ts{9}{1}{1:46:11}. Consequently, the absolute space of Newton is, in our formalism, the one space $(\mathcal R,g)$, obtained by identifying all the $\mathcal R_\tau$; ``and now it is a statement
that this is the absolute space that Newton meant''. (As said in class; whether it is \emph{exactly} what Newton meant is left open, as at the start of the section on the definition: ``one could argue about it'' \ts{9}{1}{42:49}.)

\paragraph{Projection of world lines.} Take a curve $\gamma$ that is an autoparallel through this spacetime (a particle with vanishing charges, as shown in the previous lecture) \ts{9}{1}{1:46:32}. In an inertial system one can then, instead of $\gamma$, consider the \emph{spatial curve}
$\gamma_{\rm spatial}$ defined by: take the chart of the inertial system, go backwards to the space $\mathcal R_\tau$ that was chosen, and take only the spatial coordinates of the curve, $\gamma_{\rm spatial}(\lambda)=(q^1(\lambda),q^2(\lambda),q^3(\lambda))$: ``it is the projection along the coordinates of this inertial system'';
this gives a projected curve in the space, and one would say this is the \emph{trajectory} (Bahnkurve) of the particle in space \ts{9}{1}{1:46:53}. One can do it into any other space, and it looks exactly the same \ts{9}{1}{1:47:34}. So in an inertial system one can really make this projection: push everything that happens in the single spaces down into one
space of one's choice, and look only at trajectories instead of the time-resolved world lines (``instead of the world lines''). The one space one chooses, $\tau$, can without loss of generality be $\tau=0$: ``push everything into one space, in an inertial system, and if one has a spatial spacetime metric, push everything down; and this is the
absolute dream under the stated conditions'' \ts{9}{1}{1:48:16}.
At this point we are fully back: from now on one can open the textbooks and everything one finds there will be understood \ts{9}{1}{1:48:37}.

\begin{coursenote}[A summary of how the objects fit together]
Spacetime $M$ (4-dimensional) $\supset$ spaces $\mathcal R_\tau$ (3-dimensional, given by the level sets of $t$) $\leftarrow$ absolute space $(\mathcal R,g)$ (all $\mathcal R_\tau$ identified, with the one time-independent metric $g$, available only in an inertial system) $\leftarrow$ nothing else: a 4-dimensional metric on $M$ does not occur.
$G$ lives on $M$, $g$ on the space; $\nabla G=0$ ties $G$ to $\nabla$; the Christoffel symbols of $g$ are the spatial $\Gamma$'s. (Added; not said in class.)
\end{coursenote}
