\section{Plan of the lecture and recall of the last lecture}\label{sec:var-plan}

Good morning, welcome to the make-up lecture (``Ausweichvorlesung''), and thanks for the walk. Today is lecture 14, \textbf{calculus of variations and the Lagrangian of a particle}. In the first part of the lecture we do the calculus of variations, a purely mathematical part; in the second part we identify the Lagrangian that belongs to a particle in Newtonian absolute space. How it goes for many particles, for interactions and for constraints is for the next lecture \ts{14}{1}{0:00}.

\begin{recall}[Tangent bundle, Lagrangian, action (\cref{ch:c13})]\label{rec:var-last}
Last time we constructed, from a smooth manifold $M$, the \emph{tangent bundle} $TM$ (the disjoint union of all tangent spaces, \cref{def:lag-tb}), with the projection $\pi\colon TM\to M$. A chart $(U,x)$ of $M$ induces a chart $\xi_x$ of $TM$ (\cref{def:lag-xi}); in this way $TM$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $2d$, with physical interpretation as the \emph{velocity phase space}. Later a second phase space will appear, the cotangent bundle, the basis of the Hamiltonian formulation. On $TM$ any smooth function is called a \emph{Lagrangian}, $L\colon TM\to\R$ (\cref{def:lag-L}): very many possible ones, and we shall see which are interesting. The \emph{action} is the functional $S[\gamma]=\int_a^b\mathrm d\lambda\,L(\tilde\gamma(\lambda))$ on the smooth curves $\gamma\colon[a,b]\to M$ from $p=\gamma(a)$ to $q=\gamma(b)$ (\cref{def:lag-action}), where $\tilde\gamma(\lambda)\in T_{\gamma(\lambda)}M$ is the tangent vector of the curve at $\gamma(\lambda)$, the \emph{lift} (\cref{def:lag-lift}); so $\tilde\gamma$ is a curve in the tangent bundle. The square brackets are the tradition for ``a functional''.
\ts{14}{1}{0:42}--\ts{14}{1}{7:12}.
\end{recall}

Up to now these are, in the lecturer's words, ``more or less vague mathematical constructions, physically completely void of content''; in the first part of today's lecture this stays so. Why spend one and a half lectures on it without physical content? The answer: this technique of the calculus of variations, with Lagrangians and actions, has a meaning \emph{far beyond point mechanics}. Today the whole Standard Model of elementary particle physics and the theory of gravitation are formulated by actions, only not for point particles but by actions generalised to field theory; and it is ``no great magic'' \ts{14}{1}{7:12}.

\begin{aside}[The ``principle of action'' (as said in class, opinion of the lecturer)]\label{as:var-principle}
One often speaks of the \emph{action principle} as a very deep principle. The lecturer's opinion: there is nothing especially deep in it; it is one \emph{possible formulation} of a theory which has advantages. Whether there is knowledge of nature in it, ``one must be very careful''; but it is very important \emph{within the theory}. That is why he presents it first in larger generality, and specialises to the concrete context only afterwards \ts{14}{1}{7:54}.
\end{aside}

Today: first we investigate the \textbf{stationary curves} of such a functional and derive the \textbf{Euler--Lagrange equations (Euler--Lagrange-Gleichungen)}, an essential topic of mechanics: a criterion, in the form of \emph{differential equations}, for the stationarity of the action value. What does ``stationary'' mean? In quotation marks ``wiggling'' at the curve, which must be written down mathematically exactly. Imagine that to each curve $\gamma$ belongs a number $S[\gamma]$; one wiggles a little at the curve and examines whether this value changes; if it does not change to first order, for very small wiggling, the curve is called stationary. Why this interests us I say after the precise definition \ts{14}{1}{8:35}--\ts{14}{1}{10:03}.
The second thing we do is to identify a very specific Lagrangian, for a particle that couples to conservative interaction fields. The first part is purely mathematical, the second is physical, and there we make contact with Newtonian theory \ts{14}{1}{10:03}--\ts{14}{1}{10:46}. ``Are there questions about the background from last time? Okay, good'' \ts{14}{1}{10:46}.

\section{Variations of a curve}\label{sec:var-variation}

``Variation'' is the posh word for ``wiggling'', it is also the technical term, and we define it exactly \ts{14}{1}{11:43}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: variation of a curve (Variation einer Kurve)]\label{def:var-variation}
Let $\gamma\colon[a,b]\to M$ be a smooth curve in the base manifold $M$. A map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-H}
  H\colon[a,b]\times(-1,1)\to M,\qquad(\lambda,\varepsilon)\mapsto H(\lambda,\varepsilon),
\end{equation}
with two real arguments, is called a \textbf{variation of $\gamma$} if
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $H$ is a smooth map (the lecturer: ``a very important condition, we shall use it technically later'');
  \item $H(\lambda,0)=\gamma(\lambda)$ for all $\lambda\in[a,b]$: the curve we started from is recovered at the second argument $0$;
  \item $H(a,\varepsilon)=\gamma(a)$ and $H(b,\varepsilon)=\gamma(b)$ for all $\varepsilon\in(-1,1)$: the initial point and the end point (for comparison with the functional, the points $p$ and $q$) are recovered for \emph{every} value of the second argument.
\end{enumerate}
(The lecturer writes a big $\Gamma$ for $H$, ``this has nothing to do with the small $\gamma$''; here the letter $H$ is used so as not to clash with the Christoffel symbols below.) \ts{14}{1}{12:03}--\ts{14}{1}{14:50}.
\end{definition}

\emph{Picture.} Each variation $H$ defines a \textbf{family of curves}: for each $\varepsilon\in(-1,1)$ the \emph{family member}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-member}
  \gamma_\varepsilon\colon[a,b]\to M,\qquad\gamma_\varepsilon(\lambda):=H(\lambda,\varepsilon)
\end{equation}
is a curve from $[a,b]$ to $M$, smooth because $H$ is, and with $\gamma_\varepsilon(a)=\gamma(a)$, $\gamma_\varepsilon(b)=\gamma(b)$ (``it is hardly worth writing down, but it is a different formal object''). Draw two points $p$ and $q$ and a curve $\gamma$ from one to the other: a variation is a whole family of further curves that all begin at the same point and end at the same point; for example the member $\gamma_{0.7}$ on one side, $\gamma_{-1/2}$ on the other, and in the middle the curve $\gamma_0=\gamma$, which is condition 2. A continuum of other curves lies in between \ts{14}{1}{14:50}--\ts{14}{1}{17:38}.

Variations are \emph{not unique}: for one curve there are very many possible variations, there are many ways to fan out curves around it. A variation is a particular wiggling at the given curve, and how one wiggles is coded in the specific form of $H$: a variation which deviates very much quickly at the beginning, another which deviates little at the start and very much towards the end, and so on. Technically one likes to write the variation by the capital letter; intuitively it is ``a whole family of curves'' \ts{14}{1}{17:38}--\ts{14}{1}{18:20}.

\section{Stationary curves}\label{sec:var-stationary}

Now we look at \textbf{stationary curves}. The question behind it is a very simple one: how does the value $S[\gamma]$ change if one wiggles at $\gamma$? Precisely: fix an action $S$ and a variation $H$ of $\gamma$, and consider the function of one real variable
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-s}
  s\colon(-1,1)\to\R,\qquad s(\varepsilon):=S[\gamma_\varepsilon].
\end{equation}
Then $s(0)=S[\gamma]$ is the action on the original curve, and $s(\varepsilon)$ tells how $S$ changes if $\gamma$ is wiggled in the way described by $H$ \ts{14}{1}{18:45}--\ts{14}{1}{20:13}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: stationary curve (stationäre Kurve)]\label{def:var-stationary}
A curve $\gamma\in\mathcal C^\infty_{p,q}$ is called \textbf{stationary with respect to the action $S$} if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-stationary}
  s'(0)\;=\;0\qquad\text{for every variation }H\text{ of }\gamma ,
\end{equation}
that is, if the value of $S$ does not change \emph{to first order} when one deviates from $\gamma$ in any way, with the end points held fixed \ts{14}{1}{20:13}--\ts{14}{1}{22:25}.
\end{definition}
(The lecturer first forgot the essential word and then inserted it: the condition must hold not for some particular variation but for an \emph{arbitrary} variation $H$, which enters the definition of the family of curves and hence of $s$: ``no matter how I wiggle at this curve, no matter in which direction and in which form, but the end points I keep fixed'' \ts{14}{1}{21:43}.)

\begin{coursenote}[Stationarity is only necessary for a minimum (as said in class)]\label{cn:var-necessary}
Stationarity is a \emph{necessary} condition, for instance for $S[\gamma]$ to be minimal compared to the neighbouring curves (``in quotation marks''): if no matter how one wiggles $S[\gamma]$ was minimal, then $s$ has a minimum at $0$, hence $s'(0)=0$; a \emph{sufficient} condition is then, for example, $s'(0)=0$ together with $s''(0)>0$. These are the familiar criteria: $s'(0)=0$ and $s''(0)>0$ say that the parameter $\varepsilon=0$ is a local minimum, here a local minimum among all the neighbouring curves to be compared. So we have transformed the stationarity of a curve, via the variation, ``in a trice'' into a kindergarten problem: the necessary condition for a local minimum of a function of one variable. Mathematically there is no secret, neither in what a variation is nor in what it means \ts{14}{1}{21:21}--\ts{14}{1}{24:10}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{coursenote}[The usual notation $\delta S=0$ (as said in class)]\label{cn:var-delta}
The usual notation, ``but not really practised by us'': where we demand $s'(0)=0$ one writes $\delta S=0$, ``the variation of the action shall vanish''. There are 1001 ways to define this $\delta S$; if one pushes someone to say what exactly is meant, in the end he writes down the condition \eqref{eq:var-stationary}, ``the only one that is clear''. Whenever you see the symbol $\delta S$, replace it by \eqref{eq:var-stationary} with these definitions, and you are mathematically on the completely clear side, since all that stands there is understood \ts{14}{1}{24:10}--\ts{14}{1}{25:34}.
\end{coursenote}

The condition \eqref{eq:var-stationary} is nicely formulated, but it sounds terribly hard: how on earth should one, for a given action $S$ with a concrete Lagrangian, try out \emph{all conceivable variations} to find that $s'(0)=0$ holds for each? It is well defined and clear why we want it, but we cannot calculate it. What we need is a \emph{handy, quickly evaluable criterion} for stationarity, provided you grant that stationarity is interesting at all; for that I put you off to the second part of today's lecture, where we see why it matters in mechanics \ts{14}{1}{25:34}--\ts{14}{1}{27:04}.

\section{The Euler--Lagrange equations}\label{sec:var-el}

The handy criterion is a reformulation of the condition ``$s'(0)=0$ for all variations'', whose hard part is ``all variations''. We shall see that it is expressed in \emph{differential equations}; these are not differential equations for the Lagrangian (which we think as given) but \emph{differential equations for the curve}, expressed through the Lagrangian \ts{14}{1}{27:26}--\ts{14}{1}{28:54}. We derive it on the blackboard.

\subsection{The action in a chart}\label{ssec:var-chart}

By definition $s(\varepsilon)=S[\gamma_\varepsilon]=\int_a^b\mathrm d\lambda\,L\bigl(\tilde\gamma_\varepsilon(\lambda)\bigr)$, the lift of the member $\gamma_\varepsilon$. We could go on abstractly, without chart; ``I do not want to impose that on you, the enthusiasts can do it as homework'' \ts{14}{1}{29:56}--\ts{14}{1}{30:37}. So we pass to a chart $(U,x)$ of the base manifold, with the small restriction that the \emph{whole variation lies within the chart domain} $U$ (it could be generalised, but with this everything is transparent) \ts{14}{1}{30:37}--\ts{14}{1}{31:18}. As seen last time (\eqref{eq:lag-Schart}),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-schart}
  s(\varepsilon)\;=\;\int_a^b\mathrm d\lambda\ L_{(x)}\Bigl(Q^1(\lambda,\varepsilon),\dots,Q^d(\lambda,\varepsilon),\ \frac{\partial Q^1}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda,\varepsilon),\dots,\frac{\partial Q^d}{\partial\lambda}(\lambda,\varepsilon)\Bigr),
\end{equation}
where $L_{(x)}=L\circ\xi_x^{-1}$ is the chart representation of the Lagrangian (\cref{def:lag-Lchart}) and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-Q}
  Q^i(\lambda,\varepsilon)\;:=\;x^i\bigl(H(\lambda,\varepsilon)\bigr),\qquad i=1,\dots,d,
\end{equation}
are the components of the variation in the chart (the lecturer calls them $Q^i$ with a big $Q$, ``the $i$-th component of the variation''). They depend on $\lambda$ \emph{and} on $\varepsilon$ without our having to say so explicitly, and they are smooth functions of two real variables, because $H$ is smooth (this is the technical use of condition 1). For $\varepsilon=0$ they reduce to the chart representation of the original curve,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-q}
  q^i(\lambda)\;:=\;Q^i(\lambda,0)\;=\;x^i\bigl(\gamma(\lambda)\bigr).
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:var-schart} the $2d$ arguments of $L_{(x)}$ are exactly the $\xi_x$-components of the lift of the member $\gamma_\varepsilon$: the first $d$ are the coordinates of the point where the member is, the second $d$ the components of its velocity (the lecturer writes this out for $d=3$ with the arguments $Q^1,Q^2,Q^3$ and then $\dot Q^1,\dots$, the dot being the derivative with respect to the first argument, and notes that $L_{(x)}$ itself does not depend on the curve: it is just the chart representation of the Lagrangian on the base manifold) \ts{14}{1}{31:18}--\ts{14}{1}{34:03}. Now we have a very concrete object that can be integrated as soon as somebody gives the Lagrangian; which Lagrangians interest us is irrelevant here, ``for every Lagrangian what we do works, that is the point'' \ts{14}{1}{34:03}.

\subsection{Differentiating under the integral}\label{ssec:var-diff}

Now we must work hard. We want the condition $s'(0)=0$. It is a standard formal problem, but an important one: the derivative has to be taken with respect to $\varepsilon$ \emph{before} setting $\varepsilon=0$, so that the $\varepsilon$ must be kept in the arguments: in the lecturer's notation one defines the shorthand for the arguments $(Q(\lambda,\varepsilon),\partial_\lambda Q(\lambda,\varepsilon))$, integrates over $\lambda$ with the entry $\varepsilon$ open, and only then differentiates with respect to $\varepsilon$ and evaluates at $\varepsilon=0$ \ts{14}{1}{34:24}--\ts{14}{1}{39:17}.

First the derivative must be pulled into the integral. In general one may \emph{not} interchange integral and derivative: both are limits (the difference quotient, the Riemann integral as a limit of upper and lower sums) and limits can be interchanged only under special circumstances, for instance uniform convergence (``does this ring a bell? ah yes, one needs that''). We assume that the functions are nice enough, under a whole cloud of assumptions (continuity, uniform continuity, \dots) such that it works, only to make contact with your mathematical knowledge; if one really had to check it, one would have to show the uniform convergence \ts{14}{1}{39:17}--\ts{14}{1}{41:03}.

\begin{coursenote}[A student: why may the derivative be pulled in? (as said in class)]\label{cn:var-pullin}
A student (the caption is garbled [?]) asks about exchanging evaluation at $\varepsilon=0$ and the integral (the lecturer's answer: the integral is a limit of finite sums, and for a finite sum one may certainly differentiate and evaluate termwise; the question is about the limit, and the condition for exchanging is continuity: the integral must depend continuously on $\varepsilon$, which holds if the integrand does). If everything is smooth I see no reason why it should not depend continuously on $\varepsilon$; one would have to show it very rigorously, but ``I believe that it always works here, on account of the assumptions on the functions'' \ts{14}{1}{50:49}--\ts{14}{1}{52:32}.
\end{coursenote}

Under these assumptions
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-ds}
  s'(0)\;=\;\int_a^b\mathrm d\lambda\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\Bigl[L_{(x)}\bigl(Q^1,\dots,Q^d,\partial_\lambda Q^1,\dots,\partial_\lambda Q^d\bigr)\Bigr]_{\varepsilon=0}.
\end{equation}
The integrand is a composition of the outer function $L_{(x)}\colon\R^{2d}\to\R$ and the $2d$ inner functions $Q^m,\ \partial_\lambda Q^m$. So the \textbf{multidimensional chain rule} is needed: differentiate the outer function \emph{partially} with respect to its $M$-th argument, $M=1,\dots,2d$, and multiply with the derivative of the $M$-th inner function with respect to $\varepsilon$; sum over $M$. The sum over $M=1,\dots,2d$ splits into the first $d$ terms and the last $d$ terms ($M=m$ and $M=d+m$ with $m=1,\dots,d$) \ts{14}{1}{41:03}--\ts{14}{1}{46:17}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-chain}
  s'(0)\;=\;\int_a^b\mathrm d\lambda\ \Bigl[\,\bigl(\partial_mL_{(x)}\bigr)\ \frac{\partial Q^m}{\partial\varepsilon}\;+\;\bigl(\partial_{d+m}L_{(x)}\bigr)\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\frac{\partial Q^m}{\partial\lambda}\,\Bigr]_{\varepsilon=0},
\end{equation}
with the summation convention over $m=1,\dots,d$, and where in the brackets $\partial_mL_{(x)}$ and $\partial_{d+m}L_{(x)}$ are evaluated at the arguments $(Q(\lambda,0),\partial_\lambda Q(\lambda,0))=\xi_x(\tilde\gamma(\lambda))$, since $\varepsilon=0$ gives the original curve. The lecturer insists on the logic of evaluation: first one differentiates the function $L_{(x)}$ with respect to its $M$-th argument (a function on $\R^{2d}$, nothing to do with any curve: ``first differentiate, then insert the curve''); then one inserts the lift of the curve; for the inner functions: first the $m$-th component of $H$, then differentiate with respect to $\lambda$, then with respect to $\varepsilon$, then fix specific values; each derivative of a function is again a function that can be differentiated again. The same logic as when one writes $f(x)=x^2$, $f'(x)=2x$ \ts{14}{1}{46:39}--\ts{14}{1}{50:08}.

\begin{coursenote}[A student: index $d+m$ (as said in class)]\label{cn:var-index}
A student (garbled in the captions [?]) asks about the term $\partial_{d+m}$ and the summation convention (``are these two facts about up and down?''); the lecturer: think the sum $\sum_{m=1}^d$ in front, and the derivative is with respect to the $(d+m)$-th entry, which is exactly the entry that holds the $m$-th velocity component, and which is then differentiated again, ``exactly right'' \ts{14}{1}{50:08}.
\end{coursenote}

\subsection{Interchanging the derivatives and integrating by parts}\label{ssec:var-parts}

In \eqref{eq:var-chain} we see the mixed second derivatives $\partial_\varepsilon\partial_\lambda Q^m$. By condition 1, the $Q^m$ are \emph{smooth} functions of two variables, hence in particular twice continuously differentiable, and then the \textbf{theorem of Schwarz} applies: the order of the two derivatives can be interchanged,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-schwarz}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\frac{\partial Q^m}{\partial\lambda}\;=\;\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\frac{\partial Q^m}{\partial\varepsilon}.
\end{equation}
(The lecturer: ``only and just from this follows it'', a little ``Schwarz'' in the margin.) \ts{14}{1}{57:13}--\ts{14}{1}{59:21}.

\begin{coursenote}[``The derivative and the variation commute'' (as said in class)]\label{cn:var-commute}
In the textbooks one says things like ``the derivative and the variation commute'' or ``if there is a variation of the derivative then one may equally take the derivative of the variation''; what is that supposed to mean? If one pushes the author further and further to say what exactly is meant, one reaches precisely the definition of a variation and does what we did: this is the Schwarz interchange \eqref{eq:var-schwarz}, and nothing else \ts{14}{1}{58:39}.
\end{coursenote}

It is important that the \emph{last} derivative is now $\partial_\lambda$: under the $\lambda$-integral we can do an \textbf{integration by parts} on the second summand, putting the $\lambda$-derivative onto the other factor. Introduce the shorthand
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-eta}
  \eta^m(\lambda)\;:=\;\frac{\partial Q^m}{\partial\varepsilon}(\lambda,0),\qquad m=1,\dots,d,
\end{equation}
the \emph{infinitesimal displacement} of the curve in the chart (not an extra object: it is the derivative at $\varepsilon=0$ of the $m$-th component of the variation, and it is the only place where the arbitrary variation $H$ still enters). Then, writing $L_{(x)}$ evaluated on $\xi_x\circ\tilde\gamma$ (i.e.\ at $(q(\lambda),\dot q(\lambda))$),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-parts}
  s'(0)\;=\;\int_a^b\mathrm d\lambda\ \eta^m\,\partial_mL_{(x)}\;+\;\int_a^b\mathrm d\lambda\ \partial_{d+m}L_{(x)}\,\frac{\mathrm d\eta^m}{\mathrm d\lambda}.
\end{equation}
The second integral is integrated by parts: the lecturer keeps the first summand, ``pulls the dot over'' by a partial integration, which produces a minus sign, and collects the \emph{boundary term}, which one writes with square brackets, ``also with square brackets, which must be taken, as you know, by inserting first $b$ and then $a$ and subtracting the second from the first'' \ts{14}{1}{59:42}--\ts{14}{1}{1:02:50}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-parts2}
  s'(0)\;=\;\int_a^b\mathrm d\lambda\ \eta^m\Bigl[\partial_mL_{(x)}-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\Bigl(\partial_{d+m}L_{(x)}\Bigr)\Bigr]\;+\;\Bigl[\,\eta^m\,\partial_{d+m}L_{(x)}\Bigr]_{\lambda=a}^{\lambda=b}.
\end{equation}
In the bracket the Lagrangian derivatives are evaluated at $\xi_x\circ\tilde\gamma(\lambda)$ and the $\lambda$-derivative is taken \emph{after} that insertion: ``this partial derivative knows nothing of $\lambda$; first I must insert, and then I may differentiate'' \ts{14}{1}{1:08:46}.

The \textbf{boundary term} vanishes, and this is where condition 3 of the definition of a variation enters. Evaluating at $\lambda=b$ we get $\eta^m(b)=\partial_\varepsilon Q^m(b,0)$; but $H(b,\varepsilon)=\gamma(b)$ for all $\varepsilon$ (the end point), so $Q^m(b,\varepsilon)$ does not depend on $\varepsilon$ at all, and its derivative with respect to $\varepsilon$ is zero: $\eta^m(b)=0$. Exactly the same holds at $\lambda=a$: $\eta^m(a)=0$. So the whole boundary term drops out \ts{14}{1}{1:03:14}--\ts{14}{1}{1:05:14}.

\subsection{The Euler--Lagrange equations}\label{ssec:var-theorem}

So the intermediate result is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-inter}
  0\;\overset!=\;s'(0)\;=\;\int_a^b\mathrm d\lambda\ \eta^m(\lambda)\Bigl[\partial_mL_{(x)}-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\bigl(\partial_{d+m}L_{(x)}\bigr)\Bigr]_{\xi_x\circ\tilde\gamma(\lambda)}.
\end{equation}
Look at what is now in it: the bracket contains \emph{only the curve $\gamma$} around which we vary, and \emph{only} the factor $\eta^m$ contains the arbitrary variation. This is exactly what we wanted: the problem was that we would have to try out all variations while interested only in the curve $\gamma$; now the criterion has been split so that the bracket depends only on $\gamma$ \ts{14}{1}{1:05:14}--\ts{14}{1}{1:06:59}. The condition \eqref{eq:var-inter} must be true for \emph{every} variation, hence for every function $\eta^m$ that arises as the derivative of a variation; it can hold for all of them only if the bracket vanishes (``a variation might deviate quickly at the beginning, one at the end, and so on; if the integral is to be zero whatever the choice, the bracket must be zero''):

\begin{theorem}[Euler--Lagrange equations (Euler--Lagrange-Gleichungen)]\label{thm:var-el}
Let $L\colon TM\to\R$ be a Lagrangian and $\gamma\colon[a,b]\to M$ a smooth curve whose image lies in the domain of a chart $(U,x)$. Then $\gamma$ is stationary with respect to the action $S$ of $L$ (\cref{def:var-stationary}) if and only if, for $m=1,\dots,d$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-el}
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\Bigl(\partial_{d+m}L_{(x)}\bigl(\xi_x\circ\tilde\gamma(\lambda)\bigr)\Bigr)\;-\;\partial_mL_{(x)}\bigl(\xi_x\circ\tilde\gamma(\lambda)\bigr)\;=\;0 .
\end{equation}
In the notation of the textbooks (see \cref{cn:lag-notation}, not used here) this is $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q^m}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^m}=0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
``If'' (the Euler--Lagrange equations imply stationarity): by the computation above, $s'(0)$ for the variation $H$ is given by \eqref{eq:var-inter}, with $\eta^m=\partial_\varepsilon Q^m(\cdot,0)$ (differentiation under the integral assumed, \cref{cn:var-pullin}). If the bracket in \eqref{eq:var-inter} vanishes identically, then $s'(0)=0$ for every variation. ``Only if'' (stationarity implies the equations; the lecturer's argument): $s'(0)=0$ must hold for every variation, i.e.\ for arbitrary $\eta$; so the bracket is $0$. For completeness, the step ``if the integral vanishes for all $\eta$ then the bracket vanishes'' is the \emph{fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations} (not proved in class): if the continuous function $f_m:=\partial_mL_{(x)}-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\partial_{d+m}L_{(x)}$ were different from zero at an interior point $\lambda_0$ for some $m$, say $f_m(\lambda_0)>0$, it would be $>0$ on a neighbourhood $[\lambda_0-\delta,\lambda_0+\delta]\subset(a,b)$. Choose the variation $H$ with $x^i\circ H(\lambda,\varepsilon)=q^i(\lambda)+\varepsilon\,\delta^i_m\,\phi(\lambda)$, with $\phi\ge0$ smooth, $\phi>0$ at $\lambda_0$, $\phi=0$ outside that neighbourhood (so the end points stay fixed and, for the $|\varepsilon|$ small, the curves stay in $U$; one rescales $\varepsilon$ to $(-1,1)$). Then $\eta^i=\delta^i_m\phi$ and $s'(0)=\int f_m\phi\,\mathrm d\lambda>0$, a contradiction [my addition, not said in class]. Hence all $f_m$ vanish at the interior points, and by continuity on all of $[a,b]$.
\end{proof}

The lecturer summarises: the \textbf{stationarity condition for the curve $\gamma$} (in a chart) is the equation \eqref{eq:var-el}, evaluated at the lifted curve, with the first partial derivative evaluated first, then the curve inserted, and only then the derivative with respect to $\lambda$. You find this equation, in the traditional form, on the cover page of Fließbach's book: ``$\delta S=0$'' and underneath the Euler--Lagrange equation \ts{14}{1}{1:08:46}--\ts{14}{1}{1:10:08}. There one writes it with problems of notation: (1) the notation of the derivatives (\cref{cn:lag-notation}); (2) it appears there always in a chart; here also we have the derivative in a chart, but it holds for \emph{every} chart, which is what we showed by the derivation \ts{14}{1}{1:10:08}--\ts{14}{1}{1:10:30}.

\begin{coursenote}[The Euler--Lagrange equations are ordinary differential equations for the curve (as said in class)]\label{cn:var-ode}
These are \emph{not differential equations for $L$}: $L$ is given. If you plug in a concrete $L$, they become, for a concrete $L$, \emph{ordinary differential equations of second order for the curve $\gamma$}. These are the Euler--Lagrange equations, ``derived very cleanly here''. The derivation also shows what ``the equations have the same form in all coordinate systems'' means (the lecturer says later): he never understood what ``same form'' should mean in the textbooks (``form is defined by form''); in our case it is simple: the equation is the same in every chart, because we derived it using an \emph{arbitrary} chart, so it looks like this in every chart, ``this is manifest, a lot more work on the way, but no verification any more'' \ts{14}{1}{1:10:50}--\ts{14}{1}{1:11:35}, \ts{14}{1}{1:21:53}--\ts{14}{1}{1:22:54}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{history}[Euler, Lagrange and the calculus of variations]\label{hist:var-el}
The equation is named after Leonhard Euler and Joseph-Louis Lagrange. Euler's book \emph{Methodus inveniendi lineas curvas maximi minimive proprietate gaudentes} appeared in 1744, and Lagrange developed the $\delta$-method of variations from 1755 on; Lagrange's \emph{M\'ecanique analytique} (1788) builds mechanics on it. The lecturer does not say any of this; these are additions of mine, not said in class.\\
\emph{References}: L.\ Euler, \emph{Methodus inveniendi lineas curvas \dots} (Lausanne \& Geneva, 1744); J.-L.\ Lagrange, \emph{M\'ecanique analytique} (Paris, 1788).
\end{history}

\section{The Lagrangian of an uncharged particle}\label{sec:var-free}

So now we have an action, and we know what the stationary curves of an arbitrary Lagrangian look like. The big question is what on earth this has to do with physics, and that is the fourth part of the lecture: the \textbf{Lagrangian of an uncharged particle}. The title now has our hard physics in it: we defined what a particle in spacetime is (a world line with an inertial mass and charges), and we know what an \emph{uncharged} system is: all charges are zero. That is the first important case, ``and actually the only important one, the rest is decoration'', to which we return \ts{14}{1}{1:11:56}--\ts{14}{1}{1:12:49}.

\subsection{Restriction to an inertial system and to absolute space}\label{ssec:var-restrict}

From now on we specialise: we take the world line of the particle and consider its \emph{projection} to absolute space, in a stratified atlas. This is possible only in an inertial system, and it is the whole basis of what follows. ``Nothing of what we do here can be transferred without more ado to a spacetime picture'': the Lagrange formalism and the Hamilton formalism can be lifted to spacetime in relativity theory, but in classical mechanics they are, for various reasons, forced to remain purely in \emph{absolute space}; that is, we can really only do it in an inertial system \ts{14}{1}{1:12:49}--\ts{14}{1}{1:13:52}.

So: consider the particle $(\gamma,m,c_1,\dots,c_L)$ with all charges zero, $c_i=0$ (the inertial mass $m$ stays); the projection of the world line $\gamma$ is a curve in absolute space $\mathcal R$ (``I use $\gamma$ again, the curve of the variation calculus: the one we think of as a curve in absolute space''), where $\mathcal R$ is a three-dimensional manifold with an atlas and, in particular, with a Riemannian metric $g$. We have seen (\cref{ch:c09}) that we can only project, and lose no information, in an inertial system; the inertial system is a notion of spacetime and whether it exists depends on the spacetime, but if it exists we can define absolute space and that is where the music plays \ts{14}{1}{1:13:52}--\ts{14}{1}{1:15:36}. So the curve $\gamma$ that we considered all the time in the calculus of variations is a curve in absolute space: the usual space picture of a curve in mechanics.

\begin{coursenote}[The Lagrange formalism and non-inertial systems (as said in class)]\label{cn:var-noninertial}
Sometimes the Lagrange formalism is used also by transforming \emph{out of} the inertial system; ``technically and conceptually highly problematic'': one can do it and play with the formulas, they are not wrong, but it is no longer clear what the geometry is, and so on. ``We do not make such nonsense: if we need something that is not an inertial system, we go to the spacetime formalism.'' So from now on always a three-dimensional space, which may carry a non-trivial Riemannian metric, but that is where we live \ts{14}{1}{1:15:36}--\ts{14}{1}{1:16:40}.
\end{coursenote}

\subsection{The strategy: find $L$ whose stationarity condition is Newton's equation}\label{ssec:var-strategy}

The idea: find a Lagrangian, living on the velocity phase space $T\mathcal R$ of absolute space, \emph{a concrete one}, such that the stationarity condition $s'(0)=0$ for all variations, equivalent to the Euler--Lagrange equations, is exactly \textbf{Newton's equation of motion for this particle} \ts{14}{1}{1:16:40}--\ts{14}{1}{1:17:44}. We saw that the Euler--Lagrange equations are ordinary differential equations of second order for the curve; maybe we find a function on the velocity phase space such that the variation of the action built with it gives the equations of motion \ts{14}{1}{1:18:04}.

``Then you can say: wow, we have worked hard to get back what we had before; right.'' But as promised, the point is that \emph{actions can be added} when one has more complex systems, which with the equations of motion is not so simple; this is the trick, and it leads to a reformulation of mechanics (next time). Here: I claim what $L$ is, and then we show that it works \ts{14}{1}{1:18:25}--\ts{14}{1}{1:19:06}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the Lagrangian of a free uncharged particle]\label{def:var-Lfree}
Let $(\mathcal R,g)$ be absolute space with its Riemannian metric $g$ and $m$ the inertial mass of the particle. The \textbf{Lagrangian of the uncharged particle} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-Lfree}
  L\colon T\mathcal R\to\R,\qquad L(X)\;:=\;\tfrac12\,m\,g_{\pi(X)}(X,X),
\end{equation}
for $X\in T\mathcal R$: take the base point $\pi(X)$, where $X$ lives as a tangent vector, apply the metric of absolute space at that point (it can change from point to point) with the vector $X$ inserted twice, and multiply by $m/2$ \ts{14}{1}{1:19:06}--\ts{14}{1}{1:19:47}.
\end{definition}
This is a smooth function $T\mathcal R\to\R$, hence a Lagrangian: the metric $g_{\pi(X)}$ is a $(0,2)$-tensor at the point $\pi(X)$, eating two tangent vectors at that point to produce a number. Does it have a name in physics? Yes: it is the \textbf{kinetic energy} $T=\tfrac12 mv^2$, with $v$ the velocity: if you want to make a speed from a velocity vector, you need a metric to take its length, ``there it is'' (``need for speed''); so $L=T$ at this point of the velocity phase space \ts{14}{1}{1:19:47}--\ts{14}{1}{1:21:09}.

\subsection{Verification in a chart}\label{ssec:var-verify}

Now we must show that this really gives the equation of motion of an uncharged particle. We do not carry out the variation again, ``we are not crazy'', but go to a chart and write down the Euler--Lagrange equations \eqref{eq:var-el}, valid in any chart $(U,x)$ of the base manifold $\mathcal R$, with the $2d$-chart $\xi_x$ on the bundle (here $d=3$ and the indices of space are written with Greek letters, as agreed) \ts{14}{1}{1:21:09}--\ts{14}{1}{1:23:56}. (``You have to be able to write the Euler--Lagrange equations down from memory, in the oral exam; some write them multiplied by $-1$; just so that you can do it in your sleep.'' \ts{14}{1}{1:23:16})

To evaluate we need the chart representation of the Lagrangian. For numbers $(\alpha^1,\dots,\alpha^3,\beta^1,\dots,\beta^3)\in\xi_x(T U)\subseteq\R^6$, which have nothing whatsoever to do with a curve (``first differentiate, then insert the curve''), the point $\xi_x^{-1}(\alpha,\beta)$ is the tangent vector at the point with coordinates $\alpha$ whose components are $\beta$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-Lchart}
  L_{(x)}(\alpha,\beta)\;=\;\tfrac12\,m\,g_{\rho\sigma}(\alpha)\,\beta^\rho\beta^\sigma,\qquad g_{\rho\sigma}(\alpha):=g\Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\rho},\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\sigma}\Bigr)\circ x^{-1}(\alpha),
\end{equation}
(``this is just the definition of $\xi_x$, as in the book'' \ts{14}{1}{1:24:20}--\ts{14}{1}{1:27:30}). Evaluating along the curve means inserting $\alpha^\mu=q^\mu(\lambda)=x^\mu(\gamma(\lambda))$ and $\beta^\mu=\dot q^\mu(\lambda)$, the components of the tangent vector, abbreviated $q\equiv(q^1,q^2,q^3)$ \ts{14}{1}{1:27:50}--\ts{14}{1}{1:30:39}.

\emph{First term of the Euler--Lagrange equation.} We need the partial derivative of $L_{(x)}$ with respect to the $(d+\mu)$-th argument, i.e.\ with respect to $\beta^\mu$, $\mu=1,2,3$. The $\beta$ occurs twice, so a product rule is needed (the lecturer does it ``by hand'': first the first factor, then the second, giving two terms $\delta^\rho_\mu\beta^\sigma$ and $\beta^\rho\delta^\sigma_\mu$); renaming the dummy index in the second term and using the symmetry of the metric, the two terms are equal and the factor $\tfrac12$ cancels \ts{14}{1}{1:34:13}--\ts{14}{1}{1:38:22}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-dbeta}
  \partial_{d+\mu}L_{(x)}(\alpha,\beta)\;=\;m\,g_{\mu\sigma}(\alpha)\,\beta^\sigma .
\end{equation}
Only now insert the curve, $\partial_{d+\mu}L_{(x)}\bigl(\xi_x\circ\tilde\gamma(\lambda)\bigr)=m\,g_{\mu\sigma}(q(\lambda))\,\dot q^\sigma(\lambda)$, and \emph{then} differentiate with respect to $\lambda$ (``that ugly $\partial_{d+\mu}$ does not interest us; what interests us is the derivative of this object with respect to $\lambda$''): a product rule, and for $g_{\mu\sigma}(q(\lambda))$ a chain rule, because $\lambda$ is hidden in $q$; the second factor gets a dot more:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-term1}
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\,\partial_{d+\mu}L_{(x)}\bigl(\xi_x\circ\tilde\gamma\bigr)\;=\;m\,\frac{\partial g_{\mu\sigma}}{\partial\alpha^\rho}\bigl(q\bigr)\,\dot q^\rho\dot q^\sigma\;+\;m\,g_{\mu\sigma}(q)\,\ddot q^\sigma .
\end{equation}
\ts{14}{1}{1:38:22}--\ts{14}{1}{1:42:17}. (The lecturer comments: ``afterwards you leave out all the decorations which we write now so that we know what objects we have, and you do such a calculation in three lines; but this is the complete, absolutely precise version, where at every point one can say which object it is and where it comes from, and only with this information can questions be settled.'' \ts{14}{1}{1:41:36}.)

\emph{Second term.} The derivative with respect to the $\mu$-th argument $\alpha^\mu$ only touches the metric components:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-term2}
  \partial_\mu L_{(x)}\bigl(\xi_x\circ\tilde\gamma\bigr)\;=\;\tfrac12\,m\,\frac{\partial g_{\rho\sigma}}{\partial\alpha^\mu}\bigl(q\bigr)\,\dot q^\rho\dot q^\sigma .
\end{equation}
\ts{14}{1}{1:42:37}--\ts{14}{1}{1:44:51}.

\begin{coursenote}[Student corrections of the indices (as said in class)]\label{cn:var-slips}
During this computation the lecturer mixes the indices $\rho$ and $\sigma$; ``I did that really wrong'', ``it was only a bad sigma'', and the students correct them; and later (in \eqref{eq:var-christoffel}) the lecturer forgets to close a bracket, a student points it out and he notes ``it's true, forgotten'', then the result is right. Here these slips are corrected silently \ts{14}{1}{1:43:27}, \ts{14}{1}{1:48:44}.
\end{coursenote}

So the Euler--Lagrange equation \eqref{eq:var-el} for the uncharged particle reads, for $\mu=1,2,3$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-elfree}
  m\,g_{\mu\sigma}\,\ddot q^\sigma\;+\;m\,\frac{\partial g_{\mu\sigma}}{\partial x^\rho}\,\dot q^\rho\dot q^\sigma\;-\;\frac12\,m\,\frac{\partial g_{\rho\sigma}}{\partial x^\mu}\,\dot q^\rho\dot q^\sigma\;=\;0,
\end{equation}
all $g$'s being evaluated at $q(\lambda)$. This is beautiful, because it is equivalent to something familiar: we only reorder the terms and multiply both sides by $g^{\nu\mu}(q(\lambda))$, the inverse of the metric (an equivalence transformation); it is allowed to divide by $m$. The first term gives $g^{\nu\mu}g_{\mu\sigma}=\delta^\nu_\sigma$, hence $\ddot q^\nu$. In the second term the product $\dot q^\rho\dot q^\sigma$ is symmetric under $\rho\leftrightarrow\sigma$, so we may replace $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\sigma}$ by its symmetrisation $\tfrac12(\partial_\rho g_{\mu\sigma}+\partial_\sigma g_{\mu\rho})$ (``nobody may forbid me to replace this function by a symmetrisation'': one writes the term twice with a factor $\tfrac12$, exchanges $\rho$ and $\sigma$ in the second); one gets
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-christoffel}
  \ddot q^\nu\;+\;\Gamma^\nu{}_{\rho\sigma}\,\dot q^\rho\dot q^\sigma\;=\;0,\qquad\Gamma^\nu{}_{\rho\sigma}\;=\;\frac12\,g^{\nu\mu}\Bigl(\frac{\partial g_{\mu\sigma}}{\partial x^\rho}+\frac{\partial g_{\rho\mu}}{\partial x^\sigma}-\frac{\partial g_{\rho\sigma}}{\partial x^\mu}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
\ts{14}{1}{1:44:51}--\ts{14}{1}{1:48:44}. One recognises in $\Gamma^\nu{}_{\rho\sigma}$ the \textbf{Christoffel symbols} of the metric $g$, which we already know (\cref{eq:as-christoffel}, \cref{thm:as-christoffel}), and \eqref{eq:var-christoffel} is the equation for the spatial components in a chart of a curve of a particle which has no charge: it is nothing else than the \emph{autoparallel equation in space}, in addition affinely parametrised, and this is the \emph{rectilinear uniform motion} in absolute space: the first axiom of Newton (in an inertial system, as stated in the title of lecture 9, and there are no interaction terms) \ts{14}{1}{1:48:44}--\ts{14}{1}{1:50:31}; compare \eqref{eq:as-autopar}.

\begin{theorem}[The stationary curves of $L=\tfrac12mg(X,X)$ are the free motions]\label{thm:var-free}
A curve $\gamma$ in absolute space is stationary for the action of the Lagrangian \eqref{eq:var-Lfree} if and only if, in every chart, it satisfies the autoparallel equation $\ddot q^\nu+\Gamma^\nu{}_{\rho\sigma}\dot q^\rho\dot q^\sigma=0$ of the Levi-Civita connection of $g$ (``rectilinear uniform motion''), that is, if it is the world line of a free particle of the first Newtonian axiom.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{thm:var-el}, stationarity is equivalent to \eqref{eq:var-el}; for \eqref{eq:var-Lchart} this is \eqref{eq:var-elfree}, equivalent to \eqref{eq:var-christoffel} (multiplication by the invertible matrix $g^{\nu\mu}/m$), as computed above. \qedhere
\end{proof}

\begin{coursenote}[Lecture caveats: the transcript, and charts of the curve (my remark, not said in class)]\label{cn:var-caveat}
The lecturer's statement that this holds only ``in the inertial system'' refers to the setting: absolute space $\mathcal R$ exists only if an inertial system is chosen, as in \cref{ch:c09}. The computation is valid in \emph{each} chart of $\mathcal R$; for a curve not contained in a single chart one patches by overlapping charts, which \cref{thm:var-el} does not need to state. [?]
\end{coursenote}

\subsection{What we have achieved: the role of the kinetic energy}\label{ssec:var-achieved}

What did we achieve? We showed that if one takes as the Lagrangian the \emph{kinetic energy}, builds the action, and finds the \emph{stationary points} of the action (not the minimal ones), one gets the equation of motion of the free particle \ts{14}{1}{1:50:31}--\ts{14}{1}{1:51:15}.

\begin{coursenote}[It is not a ``principle of minimal action'' (as said in class)]\label{cn:var-minimal}
In the books one often finds the \emph{principle of minimal action}. It is not a principle but a \emph{construction principle}; it sounds as if it were very deep; it is just so made, the Lagrangian is so constructed that the right thing comes out. Mysteriously it is the kinetic energy, and mysteriously one should look for minima; none of this is mysterious: one must find \emph{all stationary points}, not only the minima. Secondly, the kinetic energy is important because the variation gives the equation of motion of the free particle \ts{14}{1}{1:51:15}--\ts{14}{1}{1:51:57}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{coursenote}[Why not a square root? (as said in class)]\label{cn:var-sqrt}
If you ever wondered why the kinetic energy is not $\tfrac12m|v|$, i.e.\ why there is no square root, ``why $v^2$'': take the root, $\sqrt{g(X,X)}$, and vary (you have done that on the problem sheet): what comes out is the \emph{length functional}, and the variation gives the straight lines (geodesics), but these are \emph{arbitrarily parametrised}: they are straight but not uniform, the equation says nothing about when we are where, and the parametrisation is not fixed (``at the beginning fast, then slow, although we run along the straight line''). Newton's first axiom says \emph{uniform} (``gleichf\"ormig''): it excludes the root and says that you must take the kinetic energy without the root. ``These are very deep connections; why the kinetic energy looks as it looks is in the first Newtonian axiom, but one has to come to it first'' \ts{14}{1}{1:51:57}--\ts{14}{1}{1:53:20}.
\end{coursenote}

\section{Coupling to conservative interaction fields: $L=T-V$}\label{sec:var-coupled}

What now if the particle couples to an interaction field? Then the right-hand side of the equation would not be zero. The question: can we \emph{add} something to this Lagrangian, such that it is still a Lagrangian (still a function from the velocity phase space to the reals) and the modified Euler--Lagrange equations have, on the right-hand side, the interaction \ts{14}{1}{1:53:41}--\ts{14}{1}{1:54:23}? Suppose we think up a modification, with $\Phi$ a smooth function on the base manifold (absolute space), and $c$ the charge of the particle:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-Lcoupled}
  L(X)\;=\;\tfrac12\,m\,g_{\pi(X)}(X,X)\;-\;c\,\Phi\bigl(\pi(X)\bigr).
\end{equation}
This is no interaction \emph{field}, since a scalar is no vector field; but we had special interaction fields which come from a scalar, with an index raised, as the negative gradient: the \emph{conservative} fields (\cref{def:nd-conservative}). So maybe we have a chance if $W^\beta=-g^{\beta\gamma}\partial_\gamma\Phi$ is such a conservative field with potential $\Phi$ (here $\Phi$ is a function on absolute space; the particle's potential energy is $V=c\,\Phi$, \cref{def:nd-potential}) \ts{14}{1}{1:54:23}--\ts{14}{1}{1:55:04}.

It is not hard to evaluate the Euler--Lagrange equations of this $L$: they are linear in $L$, so one may compute them for each summand of the Lagrangian separately, take care to keep the order and the minus sign, and add. The kinetic part gives what we know; the new part gives in the Euler--Lagrange equation \eqref{eq:var-el}: $\partial_{d+\mu}$ of $-c\Phi$ is zero (the potential does not depend on the velocity $\beta$), and $-\partial_\mu(-c\Phi)=+c\,\partial_\mu\Phi$. Hence, collecting the terms,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-elcoupled}
  m\bigl(\ddot q^\nu+\Gamma^\nu{}_{\rho\sigma}\dot q^\rho\dot q^\sigma\bigr)\;=\;-\,c\,g^{\nu\mu}\,\partial_\mu\Phi\bigl(q(\lambda)\bigr)\;=\;c\,W^\nu\bigl(q(\lambda)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where the interaction field $W$ is, for a conservative vector field given by the scalar potential, $-$ the gradient with the index raised; this is exactly the right-hand side of the second Newtonian axiom (\cref{ax:nd-second}) for a single interaction field: ``for a charge that couples to such a field we have the interaction on the right-hand side'' \ts{14}{1}{1:55:04}--\ts{14}{1}{1:56:53}.

If one wants \emph{several} different interactions with charges $c_1,\dots,c_L$ and potentials $\Phi_1,\dots,\Phi_L$, the Lagrangian is just the sum,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-Lgeneral}
  L(X)\;=\;\tfrac12\,m\,g_{\pi(X)}(X,X)\;-\;\bigl(c_1\Phi_1+\dots+c_L\Phi_L\bigr)\bigl(\pi(X)\bigr),
\end{equation}
since the Euler--Lagrange equations are linear. One calls $V:=c_1\Phi_1+\dots+c_L\Phi_L$ the \textbf{total potential}; then one gets the usual formula of the books, $L=T-V$ \ts{14}{1}{1:56:53}--\ts{14}{1}{1:57:56}.

\begin{coursenote}[Sign of the potential and the field (as said in class, [?])]\label{cn:var-sign}
The lecturer writes the Lagrangian with $-c\,\Phi$ and says that with the field $W$ defined as \emph{minus} the gradient the term on the right is ``$+c\,W$''; in the transcript the signs are blurred (he says first ``$-c\,g^{\alpha\beta}\partial_\beta\Phi$'' and then ``plus $c\,W^\beta$ because $W$ is defined as minus the gradient''); the computation above is consistent with this. The relation of $\Phi$ to the potential energy of \cref{def:nd-potential} is only up to this sign convention [?].
\end{coursenote}

\begin{aside}[$L=T-V$: not ``mostly'' but always (as said in class)]\label{as:var-TV}
You will find in almost all books the statement that the Lagrangian is ``mostly'' $T-V$: it is a most useful piece of information that it is mostly so, if one is not told when it is not; and it is not even true, it is \emph{always} so. How does one come to say ``mostly''? One usually writes $T$ as $\tfrac12m(\dot q_1^2+\dot q_2^2+\dot q_3^2)$ and calls this the kinetic energy; but then the metric of flat space has been put in, and not only that: the metric in coordinates in which it has the form $\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1)$. In polar coordinates the metric of flat space looks different, and the expression would acquire factors $r^2$ and so on; one would say ``in these other systems there are extra terms'', but technically there are no extra terms: $T$ is the object $\tfrac12mg(X,X)$ and it has different forms in different coordinates. So it always looks like this, if one defines the object properly; it holds even in curved space, so $L=T-V$ for a point particle in Newtonian spacetime is always true, not only mostly \ts{14}{1}{1:57:56}--\ts{14}{1}{2:01:23}.
\end{aside}

\begin{coursenote}[Leaving the inertial system, and the rotating frame (as said in class)]\label{cn:var-rotating}
If one transforms \emph{out of} the inertial system, formally somewhat problematic but one can do many things, then one can drop the potential energy and get extra terms, which are, e.g., no longer quadratic in $\dot q$; and one says ``wow, but now we have really left the system''. Right: if you leave the inertial system it no longer holds; the main problem is that the fiction of metric geometry on your space has gone. This is exercise 3 of next week's problem sheet, where you transform into a rotating reference system; there you will see it very explicitly. Remember the title of an earlier lecture, \emph{the absolute space is a useful inertial-system fiction}: then one would have to leave metric geometry and go to \emph{Finsler geometry}, ``which I did not want to impose on you any more'', but in the inertial system it always has this form and in all coordinates and for all systems, ``this is much clearer than what usually stands there'' \ts{14}{1}{2:01:23}--\ts{14}{1}{2:02:46}.
\end{coursenote}

\section*{Key concepts}
\begin{keyconcepts}
\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define a variation of a curve $\gamma\colon[a,b]\to M$ (three conditions). What is the family of curves $\gamma_\varepsilon$ and why is a variation not unique?
  \item Define when a curve is stationary with respect to $S$. Why is this a necessary condition for a minimum, and what is meant by $\delta S=0$?
  \item Derive $s'(0)$ in a chart: where does the multidimensional chain rule enter, where Schwarz's theorem, where the integration by parts, and why does the boundary term vanish?
  \item State the Euler--Lagrange equations in the form used here (with $\partial_m$ and $\partial_{d+m}$ of $L_{(x)}$), and say in what order one differentiates and inserts the curve.
  \item What kind of equation is it for a concrete $L$? Why does it have the same form in every chart?
  \item Write the Lagrangian of an uncharged particle in absolute space and its chart representation. Show that its Euler--Lagrange equation is the autoparallel equation with the Christoffel symbols.
  \item What is added to the Lagrangian for a charge coupled to a conservative interaction field, and why can several interactions be added?
  \item Why is the square root of the kinetic energy not used? Why is it wrong to say ``principle of minimal action''?
\end{enumerate}
