\section{The stage: the phase space $T^*\mathcal Z$}\label{sec:hq-phase}

``Yes, okay, today somewhat more relaxed than usual, but that does no harm.'' Hamilton's equations are a \emph{reformulation of the Lagrange equations} and form the dynamical core of the Hamiltonian formulation which we began last time \ts{19}{1}{0:09}, when the Legendre transformation was explained, taken out of its context, as a transformation that keeps the information of a function but turns it into another function, replacing variables by the derivatives of the function with respect to them (\cref{ch:c18}). Technically it will be recognisable only in the abstract today, ``but where you recognise it you say: aha, that does not fall from heaven, it is a Legendre transformation'' \ts{19}{1}{0:30}. First comes the scene on which the Hamiltonian formulation takes place: the phase space.

Let $\mathcal Z$ be a smooth manifold with a topology and, above all, an atlas $\mathcal A_{\mathcal Z}$. ``The notation shall remind you that it describes the accessible configurations of a system'' (\cref{def:zw-access}): we no longer care about absolute space or the configuration space of several particles built from it, but start from the level of ``a system with a certain set of accessible configurations'' \ts{19}{1}{1:16}. In the trivial case $\mathcal Z$ is absolute space, or $N$ copies of it, $\mathcal Z=\mathcal Q$, the configuration manifold without constraints; ``these are all special cases; $\mathcal Z$ is the general object'' \ts{19}{1}{2:07}.

Besides the velocity phase space $T\mathcal Z$ (with its atlas built from that of $\mathcal Z$ by the $\xi$-charts, \cref{def:lag-xi}) we construct now ``the real one'' (or momentum phase space, or canonical phase space; we call it simply the \emph{phase space}, with the other one always called velocity phase space) \ts{19}{1}{2:48}. It is again a smooth manifold, but instead of the tangent bundle it is the \emph{cotangent bundle}, which the students already met in the exercises.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the cotangent bundle $T^*\mathcal Z$ and the phase space (Phasenraum)]\label{def:hq-cotangent}
As a set, $T^*\mathcal Z:=\bigsqcup_{\zeta\in\mathcal Z}T^*_\zeta\mathcal Z$, the disjoint union over all points $\zeta\in\mathcal Z$ of the cotangent spaces at $\zeta$ (\cref{sec:ts-cotangent}). The \textbf{projection} $\pi_{T^*\mathcal Z}\colon T^*\mathcal Z\to\mathcal Z$ assigns to $\varphi\in T^*\mathcal Z$ the unique point $\zeta$ with $\varphi\in T^*_\zeta\mathcal Z$ (unique because the union is disjoint; the lecturer writes the bundle in the index to distinguish it from the projection $\pi_{T\mathcal Z}$ of the tangent bundle, ``there is no problem to tell them apart, but at the beginning I write it'') \ts{19}{1}{3:50}.
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[A letter that was used twice (as said in class)]\label{cn:hq-letters}
The lecturer first writes $z$ both for a point and for a chart map and notices it (``now I have exaggerated a bit with the $z$'s''); he promises to change the point to another letter and ends by writing a $p$ for the point, then says the students may do as they like. In these notes: points of $\mathcal Z$ are $\zeta$, the chart is $z=(z^1,\dots,z^f)$, $f=\dim\mathcal Z$ (\cref{cn:pc-f}), and $p_a$ is reserved for the momentum components. ``Does everyone feel so warm? Perhaps somebody can try to do something about the windows'' \ts{19}{1}{6:38}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the induced chart $\xi^*_z$ on $T^*U$ and the atlas of $T^*\mathcal Z$]\label{def:hq-xistar}
The bundle is not enough: we need an atlas, constructed ``exactly analogously to the velocity phase space''. For every chart $(U,z)$ of the atlas of $\mathcal Z$ define $\xi^*_z\colon T^*U\to\R^{2f}$, for $\varphi\in T^*U$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hq-xistar}
  \xi^*_z(\varphi)\;:=\;\Bigl(z^1\bigl(\pi_{T^*\mathcal Z}(\varphi)\bigr),\dots,z^f\bigl(\pi_{T^*\mathcal Z}(\varphi)\bigr),\ \varphi\Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial z^1}\Big|_{\pi(\varphi)}\Bigr),\dots,\varphi\Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial z^f}\Big|_{\pi(\varphi)}\Bigr)\Bigr).
\end{equation}
The first $f$ components are the coordinates of the base point (``we use again the coordinate functions $z^1,\dots,z^f$ for the foot point of our object $\varphi$'', composed with $\pi_{T^*\mathcal Z}$); the last $f$ components are not the components of a vector but of a \emph{covector}: one applies the covector to the basis of the tangent space at the foot point induced by the chart $z$ \ts{19}{1}{6:17}. The \textbf{atlas of $T^*\mathcal Z$} is $\mathcal A_{T^*\mathcal Z}:=\{(T^*U,\xi^*_z)\mid(U,z)\in\mathcal A_{\mathcal Z}\}$. The manifold $T^*\mathcal Z$ with this atlas (one can also construct a topology) is called the \textbf{phase space over the configuration space $\mathcal Z$}; ``mathematically speaking it is the cotangent bundle'' \ts{19}{1}{17:43}.
\end{definition}
(In the lecture the whole thing is introduced as ``construction of an atlas for $T^*\mathcal Z$'' before the definition of the atlas; the order is condensed here.) \ts{19}{1}{7:19}

\begin{remark}[Three remarks]\label{rem:hq-three}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The chart $\xi^*_z$ is in full analogy to the chart $\xi_z\colon T U\to\R^{2f}$ of the velocity phase space (\cref{def:lag-xi}); only the way the last $f$ components are extracted is new, and ``if you think a bit, it must be so; there is no other possibility'' \ts{19}{1}{10:06}. ``I even gave this as an exercise: to think how it goes for $T^*\mathcal Z$ or $T^*\mathcal Q$.'' \ts{19}{1}{17:22}
  \item \emph{Notation.} One often writes (for $a=1,\dots,f$) $q^a:=\xi^{*a}_z\colon T^*U\to\R$ for the $a$-th component function of the first half and $p_a:=\xi^{*\,f+a}_z\colon T^*U\to\R$ for that of the second half. Careful: these are \emph{functions on $T^*U$}, ``short notations for the coordinate functions of this coordinate map''. The same letters $q^a$ and $v^a$ are used for the component functions of the chart $\xi_z$ of the velocity phase space: $q^a:=\xi_z^a$, $v^a:=\xi_z^{f+a}$ on $TU$ (``then everything is fine''). It is ``actually very bad style to give the same name to maps that live on completely different spaces'', but there is no danger: the first $f$ components just send the object to its foot point and then apply the chart function, $q^a=z^a\circ\pi_{T^*\mathcal Z}$ on $T^*U$ and $q^a=z^a\circ\pi_{T\mathcal Z}$ on $TU$; ``you always see which map is meant by the object that it eats'' (a $\varphi$ or a vector $X$) \ts{19}{1}{10:49}--\ts{19}{1}{15:18}.
  \item \emph{Components.} A vector $X\in T\mathcal Z$ lies in $T_{\pi(X)}\mathcal Z$ and can be expanded, $X=X^m\,\partial/\partial z^m|_{\pi(X)}$; a covector $\varphi\in T^*\mathcal Z$ lies in $T^*_{\pi(\varphi)}\mathcal Z$ and is expanded as $\varphi=\varphi_m\,\mathrm dz^m|_{\pi(\varphi)}$. (Which $\pi$ is meant, ``you see immediately'', by what it eats.) ``This is just a reminder'' \ts{19}{1}{15:39}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}
``That was a small tour de force, but actually nothing happened'' \ts{19}{1}{17:02}.

\section{The musical isomorphism}\label{sec:hq-musical}

The second section: the \textbf{musical isomorphism} (musikalischer Isomorphismus), which one can form whenever one has a Lagrangian \ts{19}{1}{20:16}. A Lagrangian $L\colon T\mathcal Z\to\R$ lives on the velocity phase space as before and \emph{induces} a map, ``and emphasis: not necessarily linear'' (it can be linear, but it is not in general)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hq-flat-sig}
  \flat_L\colon T\mathcal Z\longrightarrow T^*\mathcal Z,
\end{equation}
read ``flat''. (Joke on the name: ``Who of you makes music? What is this sign called? In English that is a flat, and the other is a sharp; in German a \emph{Kreuz}. That would be trivial: so from now on it is called flat.'' The name came from a research student's e-mail the evening before \ts{19}{1}{20:37}--\ts{19}{1}{21:20}.)

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the map $\flat_L$ (the ``Wums'')]\label{def:hq-flat}
For a Lagrangian $L$ and $X\in T\mathcal Z$ let $\flat_L(X)\in T^*_{\pi(X)}\mathcal Z$ be the covector at the foot point of $X$ whose components with respect to a chart $z$ around $\pi(X)$ are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hq-flat}
  \bigl(\flat_L(X)\bigr)_a\;:=\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi_z^{f+a}}(X)\;=\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^a}(X),\qquad a=1,\dots,f,
\end{equation}
that is, the derivative of the Lagrangian with respect to the $(f+a)$-th chart component (the $a$-th velocity component) evaluated at $X$ \ts{19}{1}{21:41}. (The lecturer writes this as ``$\flat_L(X)=\partial L/\partial\xi_z^{f+a}(X)$, with $v^a$ for $\xi^{f+a}$, and if I am pedantic at the point $\pi(X)$''.)
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[``The Wums'' (as said in class)]\label{cn:hq-wums}
This is the object of the \emph{problem sheet 9, problem 1} (not among the materials), where ``it was called the Wums'': there it was shown that it is a covector. It is a covector at the same foot point where the element of the tangent bundle lives, $\flat_L(X)\in T^*_{\pi(X)}\mathcal Z$ (``you wonder: above I have a $T^*$ and here I throw an $X$ into $\pi$: so it is''). And which ``Wums'' belongs to a point of the velocity phase space is fixed by the Lagrangian: \emph{it regulates what the momentum is for a position and a velocity} \ts{19}{1}{22:43}--\ts{19}{1}{24:25}.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the musical isomorphism; regular Lagrangian (reguläre Lagrange-Funktion)]\label{def:hq-regular}
If the map $\flat_L\colon T\mathcal Z\to T^*\mathcal Z$ is \emph{invertible}, i.e.\ there is $\flat_L^{-1}\colon T^*\mathcal Z\to T\mathcal Z$, then $\flat_L$ is called the \textbf{musical isomorphism} and the Lagrangian $L$ is called \textbf{regular}. Since $\flat_L$ is built from $L$ alone, its invertibility depends only on $L$ \ts{19}{1}{24:25}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[One particle in $\R^3$ without constraints]\label{ex:hq-flat}
Let $\mathcal Z=\R^3$ with the chart $z=\mathrm{id}_{\R^3}$ (one chart covers everything), and
\[
  L(X)\;=\;\tfrac12\,m\,g_{\pi(X)}(X,X)\;-\;V\bigl(\pi(X)\bigr),
\]
the usual Lagrangian ($g$ the metric field, the potential $V$ depends only on the foot point, not on the velocity). Then, ``and that this is so you prove on problem sheet 11'',
\[
  \flat_L(X)\;=\;m\,g_{\pi(X)}(X,\,\cdot\,),
\]
the second slot left open. Why is this a covector? It can eat a $Y\in T_{\pi(X)}\mathcal Z$ at the same foot point and spits out a real number; if we put $Y_1+Y_2$ in, the sum of the numbers comes out, because the tensor field is linear in both entries (in components $g_{ab}X^aY^b$). So it is a linear map $T_{\pi(X)}\mathcal Z\to\R$, i.e.\ an element of $T^*_{\pi(X)}\mathcal Z$ \ts{19}{1}{25:51}--\ts{19}{1}{30:04}. And the inverse (computed on the same sheet) is, for $\varphi\in T^*\mathcal Z$,
\[
  \flat_L^{-1}(\varphi)\;=\;\frac1m\,g^{-1}_{\pi(\varphi)}(\varphi,\,\cdot\,),
\]
with the inverse metric (defined on one of the problem sheets); one leaves an entry open, in which now a covector can be put, and the dual of a covector is again a vector, lying in $T_{\pi(\varphi)}\mathcal Z$ \ts{19}{1}{30:44}. ``If this seems suspect you can also accept it at this point and learn it; it counts as derived, together with the problem sheet'' \ts{19}{1}{31:24}.
\end{example}
(A tensor field is a multilinear map from several copies of vector spaces and covector spaces; the lecturer checks with the audience that this was said; the answer is ``no'' and then ``yes, the components can be pulled apart linearly'' \ts{19}{1}{28:58}.) To summarise: one can map every element of the velocity phase space to an element of the phase space, ``but not just so: only for a given Lagrangian'' \ts{19}{1}{31:45}.

\begin{remark}[Why ``musical''?]\label{rem:hq-music}
In components, write $X=X^m\,\partial/\partial q^m|_{\pi(X)}$. The $a$-th component of $\flat_L(X)$ is $\partial L/\partial v^a(X)$ \eqref{eq:hq-flat}. Note the position of the indices: in the original object the index was \emph{up} ($X^a$), the component of the result carries the index \emph{down}. ``A vector becomes a covector''; the flat \emph{pulls the index down} (as shown by a finger in the position of the index), ``and this is exactly what happens in music: a half-tone lowering, as in the notation of the notes'' \ts{19}{1}{32:06}. Sometimes in the literature, ``and this is by exception not a name which I invented'', the inverse is written as a \emph{sharp} (the sharp pulls the index up): ``the flat pulls it down and the cross pulls it up. But we do not write the cross; we write flat inverse, so that we know what we are dealing with.'' The map allows us to send vectors to covectors, \emph{mediated by the dynamics} \ts{19}{1}{34:13}.
\end{remark}

\section{The Hamilton function}\label{sec:hq-hamilton}

Third section: the Hamilton function (Hamilton-Funktion) \ts{19}{1}{34:57}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the Hamilton function $H_L$]\label{def:hq-hamilton}
Let $L\colon T\mathcal Z\to\R$ be a regular Lagrangian (so $\flat_L$ is invertible). The \textbf{Hamilton function} of $L$ is the function on the phase space
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hq-H}
  H_L\colon T^*\mathcal Z\to\R,\qquad H_L(\varphi)\;:=\;\varphi\bigl(\flat_L^{-1}(\varphi)\bigr)\;-\;L\bigl(\flat_L^{-1}(\varphi)\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
In words: $\varphi$ is a covector at some point; $\flat_L^{-1}(\varphi)$ is a vector at the \emph{same} foot point, in $T_{\pi(\varphi)}\mathcal Z$, so the covector can act on it, and a real number comes out; from it one subtracts the Lagrangian evaluated at the vector, an element of the velocity phase space: ``the Lagrangian would like an element of the velocity phase space; I fed in one from the real phase space; so I must make one of it, and this I can only do with the inverse of the musical isomorphism'' \ts{19}{1}{35:21}.

Where does the Lagrangian enter this definition? ``Here, here and once again, explicitly, here'': in $\flat_L^{-1}$ (twice) and in $L$ itself; so the whole right-hand side depends on the dynamics of the particle. And since we are already on the space $\mathcal Z$ of all accessible configurations, ``everything is in it: the constraints, the interaction between the particles, how many particles you have'': it is all in the Lagrangian and in $\mathcal Z$. Strictly one should say that $H$ depends on $L$, and mark it: \emph{the Hamilton function of the Lagrangian $L$} \ts{19}{1}{37:29}--\ts{19}{1}{38:55}.

\begin{example}[The Hamilton function of the particle: $T+V$]\label{ex:hq-ham}
For one particle without constraints (\cref{ex:hq-flat}) one computes, ``once and for all'' (and again as an exercise on sheet 11),
\[
  H_L(\varphi)\;=\;\frac1{2m}\,g^{-1}_{\pi(\varphi)}(\varphi,\varphi)\;+\;V\bigl(\pi(\varphi)\bigr).
\]
This is often written as $T+V$: the kinetic energy for certain momenta and the potential at the foot points. In contrast to the Lagrangian it is $T+V$, not $T-V$. ``But you see something else: it is a \emph{different} $T$ and a different $V$? Not really: it is the $T$ and the $V$ expressed in $\varphi$ instead of in $X$.'' The kinetic energy can be given as $\tfrac12mv^2$ or, if $p$ is the momentum, as $p^2/(2m)$: two different expressions, one using the velocity and one the momentum, and, thinking in terms of velocity and momentum phase space, really different maps. ``This is philosophical, but if one says $L=T-V$ and $H=T+V$, then yes, but the $T$ is formed differently than before.'' The mnemonic ``$T+V$'' stays; one has to express everything through the momenta \ts{19}{1}{39:40}--\ts{19}{1}{42:29}.
\end{example}
(Compare the one-variable computation $\tfrac12mv^2\mapsto p^2/(2m)$ of \cref{ex:lt-kin}; the passage from $-V$ to $+V$ is the sign convention of \cref{ch:c18}.)

\begin{remark}[Non-regular Lagrangians: the Dirac--Bergmann formalism]\label{rem:hq-dirac}
We assumed that $L$ is regular. One can also define a Hamilton function for non-regular Lagrangians, ``but it is not quite so simple; this would not deter me, but the four weeks that fell out do'': this is the so-called \emph{Dirac--Bergmann formalism} (Dirac and later Bergmann). It may happen that one is given a Lagrangian and tries to compute a Hamilton function by the above scheme: ``if you can write down some kind of inverse at all'', the Hamilton function would typically be, for instance, $0$ in such cases; ``that cannot be: no information is in it if it is the constant $0$'', and this is a rather sure sign that one has found only a \emph{partial} inverse of $\flat$ \ts{19}{1}{42:49}--\ts{19}{1}{44:15}.
In the exam: one must always check whether $L$ is regular; he could ask ``is the following Lagrangian regular or not?'' and one decides with the definition. He would not lead anybody astray by asking for the Hamilton function of a non-regular one (``perhaps I would, now that I have told you''); in that case one may stop and write: \emph{Hamilton function not readily definable, since the Lagrangian is not regular}. If one meets it later: look up Dirac--Bergmann; it plays a large role in relativity and in the quantisation of gravity \ts{19}{1}{44:36}--\ts{19}{1}{45:16}.
\end{remark}

\section{The Hamilton function in a chart}\label{sec:hq-chart}

Next, the Hamilton function in a chart \ts{19}{1}{45:38}. There are two relevant diagrams: ``if you say one cannot remember how all these functions are composed, you are right, I cannot either; but one can always construct the diagrams and read everything from them'' \ts{19}{1}{46:00}.

\begin{nfigure}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,node distance=3.4cm,every node/.style={font=\small}]
  \node (TZ) at (0,2.6) {$TU$};
  \node (TsZ) at (5.2,2.6) {$T^*U$};
  \node (R2a) at (0,0) {$\R^{2f}$};
  \node (R2b) at (5.2,0) {$\R^{2f}$};
  \node (R) at (8.6,1.3) {$\R$};
  \draw[->] (TsZ) -- node[above] {$\flat_L^{-1}$} (TZ);
  \draw[->] (TZ) -- node[left] {$\xi_z$} (R2a);
  \draw[->] (TsZ) -- node[right] {$\xi^*_z$} (R2b);
  \draw[->] (R2b) -- node[below] {$\xi_z\circ\flat_L^{-1}\circ(\xi^*_z)^{-1}\ :\ (\alpha,\rho)\mapsto(\alpha,\beta(\alpha,\rho))$} (R2a);
  \draw[->] (TsZ) -- node[above right] {$H_L$} (R);
  \draw[->] (R2b) -- node[below right] {$H_z$} (R);
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption*{The two diagrams of the lecture, drawn together. Both charts $\xi_z$ and $\xi^*_z$ come from the same chart $z$ of $\mathcal Z$ (lower right: the chart expression $H_z:=H_L\circ(\xi^*_z)^{-1}$).}
\end{nfigure}

We had a velocity phase space $T\mathcal Z$ and the true phase space $T^*\mathcal Z$, mediated by $\flat_L$, which depends on the Lagrangian; on $T\mathcal Z$ the chart $\xi_z$ constructed from a chart $z$ of $\mathcal Z$ (``we have done this all lectures long''), and today we constructed from the same chart another chart $\xi^*_z$ of $T^*\mathcal Z$, also into $\R^{2f}$. Since the Hamilton function uses only the inverse $\flat_L^{-1}$ (``it is the sharp map that matters, and so it interests us''), the diagram is drawn with the arrow in that direction. ``This is what goes on abstractly, and it is chart-independent; but it is always interesting to push it down into charts'' \ts{19}{1}{46:20}. The map on charts is $\xi_z\circ\flat_L^{-1}\circ(\xi^*_z)^{-1}$: it eats $2f$ real numbers, which we call $\alpha^1,\dots,\alpha^f$ and $\rho_1,\dots,\rho_f$ (the second half not $\beta$, ``that we would rather do in the other space; pure notation''); the collections are often abbreviated simply $\alpha$ and $\rho$ \ts{19}{1}{47:43}. We give the $2f$-tuple that comes out the name: the first $f$ entries are $\alpha$ again, ``for that is exactly what happens'': $\flat_L$ makes out of a vector in a fibre a covector over the \emph{same} foot point, so the foot point coordinates are untouched; all the nontrivial content is in the last $f$ entries, which we call
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hq-beta}
  \xi_z\circ\flat_L^{-1}\circ(\xi^*_z)^{-1}(\alpha,\rho)\;=:\;\bigl(\alpha,\ \beta(\alpha,\rho)\bigr),
\end{equation}
with $\beta=(\beta^1,\dots,\beta^f)$ functions of the $2f$ numbers. ``In practice, again this $\beta$ is exactly what you have to compute'' \ts{19}{1}{49:08}--\ts{19}{1}{52:12}.

\begin{theorem}[The Hamilton function in a chart is a Legendre transform]\label{thm:hq-chart}
Let $L$ be a regular Lagrangian, $(U,z)$ a chart of $\mathcal Z$, $L_z:=L\circ\xi_z^{-1}\colon\R^{2f}\supseteq\xi_z(TU)\to\R$ the chart representation of $L$, and $H_z:=H_L\circ(\xi^*_z)^{-1}$ the chart representation of $H_L$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hq-Hz}
  H_z(\alpha,\rho)\;=\;\rho_a\,\beta^a(\alpha,\rho)\;-\;L_z\bigl(\alpha,\beta(\alpha,\rho)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is defined by \eqref{eq:hq-beta}. Since in the chart \eqref{eq:hq-flat} reads $\rho_a=\partial_{f+a}L_z(\alpha,\beta)$, the right-hand side is the Legendre transform of $L_z$ in the last $f$ variables (\cref{def:lt-hamchart}, \eqref{eq:lt-H}), the first $f$ ones untouched \ts{19}{1}{52:12}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Insert $\varphi=(\xi^*_z)^{-1}(\alpha,\rho)$ into \eqref{eq:hq-H} (the lecturer: ``I do this particularly explicitly'') \ts{19}{1}{53:18}:
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  H_z(\alpha,\rho)&=\Bigl((\xi^*_z)^{-1}(\alpha,\rho)\Bigr)\Bigl(\Phi(\alpha,\rho)\Bigr)\;-\;L\Bigl(\Phi(\alpha,\rho)\Bigr),\\
  \Phi&:=\flat_L^{-1}\circ(\xi^*_z)^{-1}.
  \end{aligned}
\]
First term: a covector is applied to a vector, so in components the covector components, $p_a$ of the object $(\xi^*_z)^{-1}(\alpha,\rho)$, which by \eqref{eq:hq-xistar} are $\rho_a$, are contracted with the vector components, which are the $v^a=\xi_z^{f+a}$ of $\flat_L^{-1}\circ(\xi^*_z)^{-1}(\alpha,\rho)$; by definition of $\beta$ these are $\beta^a(\alpha,\rho)$: the first term is $\rho_a\beta^a(\alpha,\rho)$ \ts{19}{1}{55:24}. Second term: the ``small trick'': $L=L_z\circ\xi_z$ (that is $L_z=L\circ\xi_z^{-1}$), so
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  L\bigl(\Phi(\alpha,\rho)\bigr)&=L_z\circ(\xi_z\circ\Phi)(\alpha,\rho)\\
  &=L_z\bigl(\alpha,\beta(\alpha,\rho)\bigr)
  \end{aligned}
\]
(the lecturer inserts $\xi_z^{-1}\circ\xi_z=\mathrm{id}$ and uses associativity of the composition) \ts{19}{1}{56:47}--\ts{19}{1}{59:34}. This gives \eqref{eq:hq-Hz}. The last statement: by \eqref{eq:hq-flat}, $\flat_L(X)$ has the components $\rho_a=\partial_{f+a}L_z$, so $\beta$ is the inverse of this map, i.e.\ exactly what step 2 of the Legendre transformation does \ts{19}{1}{1:00:57}. \qedhere
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[Slips corrected on the board (as said in class)]\label{cn:hq-slips}
The students correct several index slips (the $a$ of the covector components versus $f+a$; the lecturer notes that his standard error is $f+a$ instead of $a$ and the other way round); in the formulas of these notes all arguments are written out correctly. ``In the book often $q$ and $p$ stand for $\alpha$ and $\rho$, but that is misleading'': $\alpha$ and $\rho$ are real numbers, $q^a$ and $p_a$ are functions on the phase space \ts{19}{1}{1:00:18}.
\end{coursenote}

So: ``you get the Hamilton function in coordinates, for these $2f$ numbers $\alpha$ and $\rho$; but you must compute this $\beta$, and $\beta$ has to do with the inversion of the musical isomorphism'' \ts{19}{1}{1:00:38}. And ``now it should ring a bell, and it rings if I strike the bell: this is the \emph{Legendre transformation}'': one takes the Lagrangian in some chart; the first $f$ entries are left totally untouched, the last $f$ are replaced via the inverse of the map $\flat$, which is the derivative of the Lagrangian with respect to these last components, i.e.\ a bona fide Legendre transformation \ts{19}{1}{1:01:00}--\ts{19}{1}{1:02:24}. Why that way? ``Because we defined the Hamilton function as $\varphi(\flat^{-1}\varphi)-L(\flat^{-1}\varphi)$, and it was the only possibility to construct the Hamilton function: we saw this in the abstract formalism without coordinates'' (as said in class; the uniqueness claim is not proved). Legendre transformations turn up in many contexts, ``and here it has turned up: it does not fall from heaven, it is structurally necessary'' \ts{19}{1}{1:02:44}.

\begin{coursenote}[What you really have to do (as said in class)]\label{cn:hq-practice}
If a concrete Lagrangian is given in a coordinate system, then: (1) compute $\flat_L$ in the chart (for a single particle in absolute space it is already written in \cref{ex:hq-flat}, one only translates it to coordinates), (2) invert it, which gives $\beta(\alpha,\rho)$, (3) insert in \eqref{eq:hq-Hz}. ``These are exactly the steps of the Legendre transformation: a map which has to do with the derivative of the original function with respect to some components; one must invert it (remember: that $p$ had to be inverted, solved), now in components, and that is the $\beta$; and then you get this formula: it is all the same. This is derived cleanly, and if you follow it no question should remain: it is gap-free.'' \ts{19}{1}{1:03:25}--\ts{19}{1}{1:05:08}
\end{coursenote}

\section{Lifting a curve to the phase space}\label{sec:hq-lift}

Recapitulation: what have these points to do with the trajectory of a particle, or any curve? ``Nothing at all.'' We took the whole velocity phase space and built a whole momentum phase space; we assumed that there is a Lagrangian, a function on the velocity phase space, assigning a real number to every point; from it we built the function $H$ on the phase space, again assigning a real number to every point. ``More has not happened so far''; in particular no derivatives along curves \ts{19}{1}{1:05:30}--\ts{19}{1}{1:06:33}. To connect these purely mathematical definitions with mechanics we look again at curves in the configuration space, which describe the configurations of a system; in Lagrangian mechanics one lifted the curve from $\mathcal Z$ to the velocity phase space; ``and now we must lift the curve to the momentum phase space too, and then we can go on'' \ts{19}{1}{1:06:53}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the lift of a curve to the phase space $T^*\mathcal Z$]\label{def:hq-lift}
Let $\sigma\colon I\to\mathcal Z$ be a smooth curve (``we always want smooth''), and $\tilde\sigma\colon I\to T\mathcal Z$ its lift to the velocity phase space (\cref{def:lag-lift}; the lecturer calls it $X_\sigma$). The \textbf{lift of $\sigma$ to $T^*\mathcal Z$} is the curve
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hq-lift}
  \varphi_\sigma\colon I\to T^*\mathcal Z,\qquad \varphi_\sigma\;:=\;\flat_L\circ\tilde\sigma .
\end{equation}
Take the curve, lift it from the configuration manifold to the velocity phase space, and map it over to the phase space with $\flat_L$; ``this is already everything; how else should it go?'' \ts{19}{1}{1:07:13}--\ts{19}{1}{1:10:10}
\end{definition}
(The lift depends on $L$ via $\flat_L$; the lecturer's question ``how do we get from $T\mathcal Z$ to $T^*\mathcal Z$?'' is answered by a student: ``with flat''.)

\begin{example}[The configuration manifold of the double pendulum (as said in class)]\label{ex:hq-torus}
The lecturer draws the situation: a configuration manifold $\mathcal Z$, for instance ``the surface of a car tyre'', which is $S^1\times S^1$, and this is the configuration space of the double pendulum (``two angles, each of which can run from $0$ to $2\pi$ if the pendulum can make a full turn''). Over it the velocity phase space cannot be drawn: it is four-dimensional, at each point one would attach the tangent space (here two-dimensional), and the collection of these is the tangent bundle; likewise the cotangent bundle, with at every point the two-dimensional \emph{co}tangent space (``in principle all of them are two-dimensional tangent spaces, only they are not tangent but cotangent spaces; I have no better picture''). Between them $\flat_L$, over both the projections to $\mathcal Z$; the Lagrangian lives on the velocity phase space and assigns to each point a real number, the Hamilton function on the phase space \ts{19}{1}{1:10:35}--\ts{19}{1}{1:13:26}. (In the lecture the picture first draws the Lagrangian on the phase space; the lecturer notices that the picture would better duplicate nothing: both lifts live over the same $\mathcal Z$.)

What has been done so far: ask whether a given curve in $\mathcal Z$ is a solution of the Euler--Lagrange equation, i.e.\ the motion of a particle with this Lagrangian; for this one \emph{lifts} the curve to the velocity phase space (``at every point the corresponding tangent vector is attached''), and it is the lift that must satisfy the Euler--Lagrange equations \ts{19}{1}{1:13:48}.
\end{example}
Now the natural question: ``Donnerwetter: if the Euler--Lagrange equation can be expressed by the Lagrangian, and $H$ is a Legendre transform of $L$ by means of $\flat^{-1}$, could one not also express the Euler--Lagrange equation by $H$, but now by the lift to the phase space instead of the lift to the velocity phase space?'' This is exactly the aim of the next section \ts{19}{1}{1:14:30}--\ts{19}{1}{1:15:11}.

\section{Why leave the velocity phase space?}\label{sec:hq-why}

Before the computation, a word of motivation: ``you can say, good heavens, now you have explained for ages how the Euler--Lagrange equations work, it is nice that one can go to another type of phase space; but why on earth do I want it, if the Lagrangian and the velocity phase space give a perfect framework even for practical problems?'' \ts{19}{1}{1:16:08}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The momentum phase space has \emph{fantastic properties}, which can be translated back by $\flat$; but properties that one has directly on $T^*\mathcal Z$ are \emph{independent of the choice of the Hamilton function}: they come only from how the configuration space looks, and one can put any Hamilton function on top and understand the dynamics of the particle. These properties are the same for all systems with the same phase space. Going back to $T\mathcal Z$ with $\flat$ they depend on $L$, and so on $T\mathcal Z$ they are mixed up. One says that here the \emph{dynamics and the kinematics are decoupled in a particularly drastic way}, while on the velocity phase space it is all somewhat blended. Even the Euler--Lagrange equation is not that simple: derivatives with respect to the higher components, then $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$, minus the derivative with respect to the lower components, equals zero: ``a funny split into the higher and the lower components'' ; a similar split will show in the Hamiltonian formulation, but quite differently \ts{19}{1}{1:16:49}--\ts{19}{1}{1:18:53}.
  \item \emph{Statistical physics.} A large vessel contains a gas with very many particles ($10^{23}$). Suppose one understands the particles microscopically exactly: one knows the Lagrangian of all and can set up the Euler--Lagrange equations for $10^{23}$ particles (``you just add an index; no problem''). To get a concrete solution one needs not only the equations but \emph{initial conditions}: a starting point in the velocity phase space: three position and three velocity data per particle, $6\cdot10^{23}$ numbers. ``How do you want to measure them? Do you want to go into this gas? You have no chance.'' So the problem with a gas is not only that the equations of motion are complicated (they are), but that even if one could solve them, one does not have the initial conditions: one has a huge ignorance of them, and one must do \emph{statistics} (theoretical physics 4). Statistics means counting: ``10 of 11 students are stupid''; one must be able to count the ensemble: ``so and so many of the initial conditions have this and that energy''. But this is a continuous space: how does one count in a continuous space? ``In litres: how much cola is that? Half a litre'' (``10 of 11 litres of cola taste terrible''). One needs a \emph{volume measure}: one could invent one, but here comes the point: on the \emph{phase space}, in contrast to the velocity phase space, there is a canonical, natural volume measure, already present by construction. It is this that must be used to do statistics. \ts{19}{1}{1:19:13}--\ts{19}{1}{1:22:19}
  \item \emph{Quantum mechanics.} One could start with the velocity phase space, but to do it \emph{always right} (by whichever quantisation method, path integral, whatever), one has to start on the phase space. ``You can solve the classical problems well on the velocity phase space, but if you want to go on theoretically, which in many cases is very practical, you have to go to the upper right in the diagram. That is why we reformulate the Lagrangian into the Hamilton function (done), and now we also write down Hamilton's equation.'' \ts{19}{1}{1:22:40}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Hamilton's equations}\label{sec:hq-hamiltoneq}

\textbf{Aim:} express the Euler--Lagrange equations not by $L$ but by $H$ \ts{19}{1}{1:15:47}. Let $\sigma\colon I\to\mathcal Z$ be a smooth curve, $\tilde\sigma$ its lift to $T\mathcal Z$ and $\varphi_\sigma=\flat_L\circ\tilde\sigma$ its lift to $T^*\mathcal Z$ (\cref{def:hq-lift}); fix a chart $z$ and use the component functions $q^a=z^a\circ\pi_{T^*\mathcal Z}$ and $p_a=\xi^{*\,f+a}_z$ (\cref{rem:hq-three}). By construction, along the lift,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hq-qp}
  q^a\bigl(\varphi_\sigma(t)\bigr)=z^a\bigl(\sigma(t)\bigr),\qquad p_a\bigl(\varphi_\sigma(t)\bigr)=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^a}\bigl(\tilde\sigma(t)\bigr),
\end{equation}
the second because $\varphi_\sigma=\flat_L\circ\tilde\sigma$ and \eqref{eq:hq-flat}. The partial derivatives of $H$ are those of the chart representation: for $\varphi\in T^*U$
\[
  \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^a}(\varphi):=(\partial_aH_z)\bigl(\xi^*_z(\varphi)\bigr),\qquad \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_a}(\varphi):=(\partial_{f+a}H_z)\bigl(\xi^*_z(\varphi)\bigr)
\]
(``the derivative with respect to the $a$-th component of the function $H\circ(\xi^*_z)^{-1}$, evaluated at $\xi^*_z$ of the object; that is the $H_z$, the representation of $H$ in the chart'') \ts{19}{1}{1:23:32}--\ts{19}{1}{1:24:55}. Note $\xi^*_z(\varphi_\sigma(t))=\bigl(q(\varphi_\sigma(t)),p(\varphi_\sigma(t))\bigr)$: ``a $2f$-tuple; in the notation $q$ and $p$ these are the $q$ of $\varphi_\sigma$ and the $p$ of $\varphi_\sigma$; I translate notations into each other; why two notations? So that you still recognise something in the notation afterwards: Newton's law is $A=B\cdot C$, true also if $A$ is force, $B$ mass and $C$ acceleration, but nobody can remember it; therefore I translate everything into the notation you find in the book.'' \ts{19}{1}{1:25:15}

\begin{theorem}[Hamilton's equations (Hamiltonsche Bewegungsgleichungen)]\label{thm:hq-hamilton}
Let $L$ be a regular Lagrangian with Hamilton function $H=H_L$, and let $\sigma$ be a smooth curve in $\mathcal Z$ such that its lift $\tilde\sigma$ satisfies the Euler--Lagrange equations,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hq-EL}
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^a}\bigl(\tilde\sigma(t)\bigr)\;=\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^a}\bigl(\tilde\sigma(t)\bigr)\qquad(a=1,\dots,f)
\end{equation}
(\cref{thm:var-el}, \eqref{eq:var-el}; $\partial L/\partial q^a:=\partial_aL_z\circ\xi_z$). Then along the lift $\varphi_\sigma$ to the phase space
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hq-hamilton}
  \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^a}\bigl(\varphi_\sigma(t)\bigr)\;=\;-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,p_a\bigl(\varphi_\sigma(t)\bigr),\qquad
  \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_a}\bigl(\varphi_\sigma(t)\bigr)\;=\;+\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,q^a\bigl(\varphi_\sigma(t)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Written in the usual short notation, along the motion in the phase space:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hq-hamilton-short}
  \dot q^a\;=\;\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_a},\qquad\dot p_a\;=\;-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^a}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(The smoothness of $\beta$, which is needed to differentiate, is assumed; it is not discussed in the lecture.)\\
\emph{Calculation 1: $\partial H/\partial q^a$} \ts{19}{1}{1:23:32}. By \eqref{eq:hq-Hz} with $\partial_a$ the derivative with respect to the $a$-th entry (an $\alpha^a$) and the product and chain rules,
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \partial_aH_z(\alpha,\rho)&=\rho_m\,\partial_a\beta^m(\alpha,\rho)\;-\;\partial_aL_z\bigl(\alpha,\beta(\alpha,\rho)\bigr)\\
  &\quad-\;\partial_{f+m}L_z\bigl(\alpha,\beta(\alpha,\rho)\bigr)\,\partial_a\beta^m(\alpha,\rho),
  \end{aligned}
\]
summed over $m$ (the first term has no $\alpha$ in $\rho_m$; the lecturer renames the summation index $m$ ``so as to get no index collision''; the first and the last term come from the $\rho_m\beta^m$ and from the dependence of the last $f$ arguments of $L_z$ on $\alpha$) \ts{19}{1}{1:26:40}--\ts{19}{1}{1:29:25}. Now evaluate at $(\alpha,\rho)=\xi^*_z(\varphi_\sigma(t))=\bigl(z(\sigma(t)),p(\varphi_\sigma(t))\bigr)$. At this point, $\beta(\alpha,\rho)$ is by \eqref{eq:hq-beta} the last half of $\xi_z\circ\flat_L^{-1}(\varphi_\sigma(t))=\xi_z(\tilde\sigma(t))$, so $(\alpha,\beta(\alpha,\rho))=\xi_z(\tilde\sigma(t))$ (``you push the curve down to the coordinates for the phase space, but with the $\beta$ you go back with flat inverse; if you think what really stands there, you find in two, three lines, which I leave as an exercise, that this is $\xi_z\circ\tilde\sigma$''), and $\partial_{f+m}L_z(\alpha,\beta)=\partial L/\partial v^m(\tilde\sigma(t))=p_m(\varphi_\sigma(t))$ by \eqref{eq:hq-qp}, which is $\rho_m$. Hence the first and the third term cancel (``the lecturer notices that the $\rho$ must not stay: one has to put $p\circ\varphi_\sigma$ into it''), and what remains is
\[
  \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^a}\bigl(\varphi_\sigma(t)\bigr)\;=\;-\partial_aL_z\bigl(\xi_z(\tilde\sigma(t))\bigr)\;=\;-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^a}\bigl(\tilde\sigma(t)\bigr),
\]
\ts{19}{1}{1:29:46}--\ts{19}{1}{1:33:26}. (``Here with $L$ it would not have been right, here stands $L_z$.'') Now use the assumption that $\sigma$ solves the Euler--Lagrange equations \eqref{eq:hq-EL} \ts{19}{1}{1:33:26}: the right-hand side is $-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^a}(\tilde\sigma(t))$ (``the minus is right: the Euler--Lagrange equations say that the one minus the other is zero, so the one equals the other, the minus stays''), and by \eqref{eq:hq-qp} this is $-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}p_a(\varphi_\sigma(t))$. This is the first equation \ts{19}{1}{1:34:32}.\\
\emph{Calculation 2: $\partial H/\partial p_a$} \ts{19}{1}{1:35:38}. Differentiate \eqref{eq:hq-Hz} with respect to $\rho_a$ (entry number $f+a$ of $H_z$):
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \partial_{f+a}H_z(\alpha,\rho)&=\beta^a(\alpha,\rho)\;+\;\rho_m\,\frac{\partial\beta^m}{\partial\rho_a}(\alpha,\rho)\\
  &\quad-\;\partial_{f+m}L_z\bigl(\alpha,\beta(\alpha,\rho)\bigr)\,\frac{\partial\beta^m}{\partial\rho_a}(\alpha,\rho)
  \end{aligned}
\]
(product rule for the first term; for the second the multidimensional chain rule: $\rho$ enters only through the last $f$ arguments) \ts{19}{1}{1:36:40}--\ts{19}{1}{1:39:07}. Evaluating at $\xi^*_z(\varphi_\sigma(t))$ as before, the last two terms cancel again, since $\partial_{f+m}L_z(\xi_z(\tilde\sigma(t)))=p_m(\varphi_\sigma(t))=\rho_m$ (``again this whole Kladderadatsch is connected with the lift to the velocity phase space: it is $p_m$ along $\varphi_\sigma$; this $m$ goes with that $m$, and the last two expressions cancel'') \ts{19}{1}{1:39:28}--\ts{19}{1}{1:40:32}. What remains is $\beta^a(\alpha,\rho)$, which at this point is the $(f+a)$-th component of $\xi_z(\tilde\sigma(t))$, i.e.\ the $a$-th component of the velocity of the curve in the chart $z$: by the definition of the tangent vector of a curve this is $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(z^a\circ\sigma)(t)$, which by \eqref{eq:hq-qp} is $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}q^a(\varphi_\sigma(t))$ \ts{19}{1}{1:40:57}--\ts{19}{1}{1:41:59}. This is the second equation. \qedhere
\end{proof}
So: ``if you differentiate $H$ with respect to the first $f$ components along the curve that gives the motion of the system in the phase space, there comes out minus $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$ of the last $f$ components of the lifted curve; and if you differentiate $H$ with respect to the $p$'s you get something quite similar, but now a plus $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$, and not of $p_a$, but of $q^a$'' \ts{19}{1}{1:41:59}--\ts{19}{1}{1:43:01}.

\begin{coursenote}[The proof is only an exercise in the same calculation (as said in class)]\label{cn:hq-exercise}
``We have only used what we already know; it is an exercise in the same calculation again and again'' \ts{19}{1}{1:35:12}. The step ``$\beta$ evaluated along $\varphi_\sigma$ is $\xi_z\circ\tilde\sigma$'' is left to the students as an exercise (``two, three lines'') \ts{19}{1}{1:30:06}. The converse direction (that curves solving \eqref{eq:hq-hamilton} have lifts solving the Euler--Lagrange equations) is not discussed in the lecture.
\end{coursenote}

\section{The crossed structure: a glimpse of symplectic geometry}\label{sec:hq-symplectic}

Two remarks on \eqref{eq:hq-hamilton} before the end of the hour, ``the time is over'': if one differentiates $H$ with respect to the $q$'s, the $p$'s stand on the other side, and if one differentiates with respect to the $p$'s, the $q$'s stand: \emph{crosswise}. And the other remarkable thing is the \emph{relative minus sign}. ``This crossing in connection with the relative minus sign between these equations defines what is called the \emph{symplectic geometry} on the phase space $T^*\mathcal Z$'' \ts{19}{1}{1:43:22}--\ts{19}{1}{1:44:11}. And it is this symplectic geometry that carries a \emph{natural volume measure}: ``I spoke earlier, alluding to statistical physics, of the necessity of counting; on continuous spaces one counts in litres'' \ts{19}{1}{1:44:33}.

Next time (announced):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Given a Hamilton function (one writes it down or constructs it: stare at the system, form the Lagrangian, do the Legendre transformation), draw its \emph{level lines} on the phase space; ``then, by the interchanging and the relative minus sign, I give you a wonderful geometric interpretation of how to understand what the system does, the motion of the system, by just looking at the picture''. This was promised \ts{19}{1}{1:44:53}.
  \item The second consequence leads to the \emph{Poisson bracket formalism}. Multiplied by $\mathrm i/\hbar$, the Poisson bracket translates directly into the \emph{commutator} of quantum mechanics, and this was one of the original ideas, still followed today in quantisation, to make from a classical system the corresponding quantum system. ``This is then theoretical physics 3. For this all is important'' \ts{19}{1}{1:45:34}.
\end{enumerate}
