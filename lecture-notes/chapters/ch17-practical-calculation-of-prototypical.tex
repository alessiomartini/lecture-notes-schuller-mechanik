\section{The typical exam problems, and the plan}\label{sec:pc-plan}

``Good morning, today comes the promised lecture with the examples'', and with it the answer to the question what typical exam problems are \ts{17}{1}{0:04}. There is a whole panopticon of possible tasks. Typically we are somewhere in a \emph{homogeneous gravitational field}: not something fundamental, but a situation in which we model some interaction by a potential. And then there are such small things as:

The \emph{thread pendulum} (Fadenpendel) is really a rod with a massless stick, since a thread would allow the mass to come closer, but it always stays at a distance; one could consider the \emph{double pendulum}; then the \emph{dumbbell} (Hantel): two masses at a constant distance, possibly with unequal masses, which is an excellent classical model for a two-atom molecule; an even better model of such a molecule is the one with a \emph{spring} in between, so that the two masses can swing against each other with some spring force, which one can model too; and the second last, ``I think the Atwood machine'' (the captions are garbled): if the mass $m_1$ is larger than $m_2$ it rushes downwards, but the feeling is that it is somehow diminished by the force exerted by the other mass, and so on \ts{17}{1}{0:25}--\ts{17}{1}{1:47}. All of these are systems \emph{with constraints}: not free particles, but, if it is one particle (the pendulum), held on some submanifold of configurations; but also for two and more particles these are typical problems.

\begin{coursenote}[The exam, ``Aufgabe 2'' (as said in class)]\label{cn:pc-exam}
In the exam you will probably get, as problem 2, after having mastered problem 1 (the mathematical formalism) ``with bravura'', something like this: a picture, and a little guidance. But if you hold something of yourself as a physicist you say: stop, the picture is enough for me, I need no further information, because the information comes today: \emph{an algorithm in five steps}, which has to be gone through. In principle everything was there last time, but there is a difference between ``in principle it is there'' and knowing exactly how to deal with it \ts{17}{1}{1:47}--\ts{17}{1}{2:49}.
\end{coursenote}

The plan: perform the algorithm once more, with a bit of theoretical background but only the essence, and treat in each of the five steps as example the small thread pendulum. When this has been done once in detail and one has seen that the formalism is quite clear, the \emph{double pendulum} will be done ``hoppla hopp'' (one-two-three), and one may imagine everything that is left out. ``This is also what the experienced classical-mechanics student does: he only sees such a system and says: I've got it'' \ts{17}{1}{2:49}--\ts{17}{1}{3:30}.

\section{The algorithm in five steps}\label{sec:pc-algorithm}

\textbf{Task} (always): \emph{set up the equations of motion for the system shown} \ts{17}{1}{3:30}--\ts{17}{1}{3:54}. The algorithm, each step illustrated by the simplest non-trivial example, the pendulum \ts{17}{1}{3:54}--\ts{17}{1}{4:38}:

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the five-step algorithm]\label{def:pc-algorithm}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Implement the constraints}: model the accessible configurations $\mathcal Z\subseteq\mathcal Q$ and the embedding $h$ (in charts: $\omega\circ h\circ\tilde\omega^{-1}$), \cref{def:zw-access,def:zw-h}.
  \item \emph{Compute the push-forward} $h_*\colon T\mathcal Z\to T\mathcal Q$ (in charts), \cref{thm:zw-components}.
  \item \emph{Write the Lagrangian $L$ of the system without constraints}, on $T\mathcal Q$.
  \item \emph{Compute the Lagrangian with constraints}, $L_{\mathcal Z}=L\circ h_*$, \eqref{eq:zw-LZ}.
  \item \emph{Write the Euler--Lagrange equations} for the curve in $\mathcal Z$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
(The steps are named by Schuller as he goes: ``step 1: implement the constraints'', ``step 2: calculation of the push-forward'', ``step 3: Lagrangian for the unconstrained system'', ``step 4: calculation of the Lagrangian with constraints'', ``step 5: Euler--Lagrange equations''.) What has to be \emph{explicitly modelled} he writes on the board in green \ts{17}{1}{7:12}--\ts{17}{1}{7:34}; in these notes: the pieces marked ``to write in the exam'' are the formulas in the displayed lines of each step.

\begin{coursenote}[Notation of the pendulum example]\label{cn:pc-f}
Here $f$ stands for the \emph{dimension of the manifold of accessible configurations} ($f=\dim\mathcal Z$; the plane pendulum has $f=1$, the double pendulum $f=2$). In \cref{ch:c16} this number was called $n$ and $f$ was the number of constraints; $D=\dim\mathcal Q=3N$ is the dimension of the unconstrained configuration space (\cref{cn:zw-f}). The pendulum has $D=3$, hence $2$ constraints. The chart of $\mathcal Z$ is called $\tilde\omega$, the chart of $\mathcal Q$ is $\omega$ (as in \cref{ch:c16}); Schuller says ``klein omega'' and ``gro{\ss}es Omega''.
\end{coursenote}

\section{Step 1: implement the constraints}\label{sec:pc-step1}

\subsection{In general}\label{ssec:pc-step1-gen}

\begin{recall}[Constraints (\cref{ch:c16})]\label{rec:pc-step1}
There is a configuration space $\mathcal Q$ for the system \emph{without} constraints, and in it the set of accessible configurations $\mathcal Z$ (a smooth submanifold); how it lies in $\mathcal Q$ is modelled by an injective smooth map $h\colon\mathcal Z\to\mathcal Q$. To write it concretely one needs charts: $\omega$ on $\mathcal Q$ and $\tilde\omega$ on $\mathcal Z$, and in general \emph{many} charts: then $\omega\circ h\circ\tilde\omega^{-1}\colon\R^f\to\R^{D}$, the map of numbers one has to model and write down: it depends on $f$ numbers, here called $\alpha^1,\dots,\alpha^f$ \ts{17}{1}{4:38}--\ts{17}{1}{8:18}.
\end{recall}

\begin{coursenote}[A correction from the audience (as said in class)]\label{cn:pc-inv}
Writing the map one first says that it ``goes from $\R^f$ to $\R^D$'' and writes the chart in the wrong order; the audience says ``it is the other way round'', and Schuller: ``of course, we go backwards here, thank you very much'': in the composition $\omega\circ h\circ\tilde\omega^{-1}$ one first undoes the chart of $\mathcal Z$ \ts{17}{1}{7:55}--\ts{17}{1}{8:40}.
\end{coursenote}

\subsection{The pendulum}\label{ssec:pc-step1-pend}

For the thread pendulum: the large space is $\mathcal Q=\R^3$ (one particle in three dimensions), which can be covered by the single chart $\omega=\mathrm{id}$, mapping $(\alpha^1,\alpha^2,\alpha^3)\mapsto(\alpha^1,\alpha^2,\alpha^3)$; the accessible configurations are a circle, $\mathcal Z=S^1$. But the circle cannot be covered by one chart; Schuller takes two (the circle minus the north pole, and the circle minus the south pole): $\tilde\omega_1$ maps $S^1\setminus\{\text{north pole}\}$ to the angle in $(-\pi,\pi)$, and $\tilde\omega_2$ maps $S^1\setminus\{\text{south pole}\}$ to the angle in $(0,2\pi)$. ``Both charts assign to the point $p$ the angle of the picture; the \emph{range} of $\tilde\omega_1$ and $\tilde\omega_2$ is different, while on the overlap they are the same map; usually we had charts with the same domain and different maps, now it is the other way round: a bit degenerate, if it confuses you, you can test yourself'' \ts{17}{1}{8:40}--\ts{17}{1}{12:46}.

The pendulum is hung on a wall: a nail in the wall is the suspension point, and the pendulum swings in front of the wall, in the $xz$-plane (``one could also take the whole sphere as accessible set, but then by a conservation law the motion stays in the plane of the initial push''). We choose the origin of $\R^3$ at the nail. Then, the embedding in the first chart:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-h}
  \omega\circ h\circ\tilde\omega_1^{-1}(\alpha)\;=\;\bigl(l\sin\alpha,\ 0,\ -l\cos\alpha\bigr),\qquad\alpha\in(-\pi,\pi),
\end{equation}
and in the second chart in exactly the same way (the angle is the same), only with $\alpha\in(0,2\pi)$ \ts{17}{1}{12:46}--\ts{17}{1}{15:16}. (The ``wall'' remark is Schuller's: first he says ``I hang the pendulum on the wall'', then the embedding.)

\begin{coursenote}[Why all this fuss with two charts? (as said in class)]\label{cn:pc-twocharts}
Of course in an application one would say: the point at the top is a nuisance, let $\alpha$ run from $-\pi$ to $\pi$ \emph{inclusive}, and then all angles of the circle are there. That works \emph{here}; but for a rather complicated system one can no longer survey where all the charts break down. That is why it is best to do it in the simple example, with all the subtlety: when one has a huge or complex problem one then does it automatically right, by following the algorithm of the general theory. ``The claim is that this stands in every textbook, and that it is often forgotten; in simple examples it is no great harm'' \ts{17}{1}{15:16}--\ts{17}{1}{16:39}.
\end{coursenote}

The lesson: this map one has to \emph{write down}. When one is more experienced, one looks at the pendulum and the first thing one writes is: it is a particle in absolute space, so the configuration space is $\R^3$ and the accessible one is an $S^1$; on it one chooses coordinates, and then one builds this embedding. If one writes it for every chart one is careful; but one need not write the whole ``rattletrap'' as long as one \emph{could}: ``if you knew what has to be written, you need not write it any more; if you never knew, it would be extremely dangerous simply to say: one does it so and so'' \ts{17}{1}{16:39}--\ts{17}{1}{18:14}.

``That was the first step, and also the last step in which you have to \emph{think}'': you model the system, everything after is just computing. By the way, this whole procedure, choosing such an embedding and coordinates on $S^1$, is in the textbooks under the keyword \emph{choice of generalised coordinates} (Wahl generalisierter Koordinaten); but there is more in it than the choice of coordinates: the embedding is in it, and that is usually swept under the table \ts{17}{1}{18:14}--\ts{17}{1}{18:56}.

\section{Step 2: the push-forward}\label{sec:pc-step2}

\begin{recall}[The push-forward (\cref{ch:c16})]\label{rec:pc-step2}
$h_*\colon T\mathcal Z\to T\mathcal Q$ takes $X\in T_p\mathcal Z$ to a vector in $T_{h(p)}\mathcal Q$, defined by $(h_*X)f=X(f\circ h)$ (\cref{def:zw-pushforward}); in charts, \cref{thm:zw-components}. Concretely one has charts $\xi_{\tilde\omega}$ and $\xi_\omega$ of the velocity phase spaces into $\R^{2f}$ and $\R^{2D}$ (``the induced charts''), and what is computed is
\[
  \xi_\omega\circ h_*\circ\xi_{\tilde\omega}^{-1}\colon\R^{2f}\to\R^{2D} \ts{17}{1}{19:17}.
\]
\end{recall}

Its components are: the first $D$ entries are those of $\omega\circ h\circ\tilde\omega^{-1}(\alpha)$ (the foot point is mapped by $h$), and the further $D$ entries are the components $(h_*X)^J=\partial_j(\omega^J\circ h\circ\tilde\omega^{-1})(\alpha)\,\beta^j$ of the push-forward of the vector with components $\beta^j$ at $\alpha$ (\cref{thm:zw-components}) \ts{17}{1}{21:01}--\ts{17}{1}{25:08}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-pushchart}
  \begin{aligned}\xi_\omega\circ h_*\circ\xi_{\tilde\omega}^{-1}(\alpha,\beta)=\Bigl(&h^1(\alpha),\dots,h^{D}(\alpha)\,;\\
  &\partial_jh^{1}(\alpha)\,\beta^j,\dots,\partial_jh^{D}(\alpha)\,\beta^j\Bigr),\end{aligned}
  \qquad h^J:=\omega^J\circ h\circ\tilde\omega^{-1}.
\end{equation}
So: ``all one wants to write is the coordinate representation of $h_*$; and that is simple, one needs only what has just been modelled: put the modelled map into the first $D$ entries, and into the last $D$ entries copy the same map, but differentiated with respect to the $j$-th variable and multiplied by $\beta^j$, and summed. Three lines in an exam'' \ts{17}{1}{25:08}--\ts{17}{1}{27:32}.

\begin{coursenote}[A slip with the index $J$ (as said in class)]\label{cn:pc-J}
In the first formulation Schuller writes a free index $J$ on the whole $2D$-tuple, then notices ``I take the $J$ away: I have written all the $2D$ components, this is already the result, a $2D$-tuple''; the large $J$ would have to run from $1$ to $2D$ \ts{17}{1}{28:20}--\ts{17}{1}{29:03}.
\end{coursenote}

For the pendulum: $f=1$, $D=3$, so $\alpha$ is one number and $\beta$ is one number; the first three entries are copied from \eqref{eq:pc-h}, and the last three are the derivative of each of the three components with respect to $\alpha$, multiplied by $\beta$ \ts{17}{1}{29:03}--\ts{17}{1}{30:26}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-pushpend}
  \xi_\omega\circ h_*\circ\xi_{\tilde\omega_1}^{-1}(\alpha,\beta)\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}l\sin\alpha\\0\\-l\cos\alpha\\l\cos\alpha\ \beta\\0\\l\sin\alpha\ \beta\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Indeed: $\partial_\alpha(l\sin\alpha)=l\cos\alpha$, $\partial_\alpha0=0$ and $\partial_\alpha(-l\cos\alpha)=l\sin\alpha$, ``then multiply by $\beta$; and there is only one $\beta$, so we are done'' \ts{17}{1}{30:26}--\ts{17}{1}{31:49}. ``Is that difficult? Not at all: this is the only thing which has to be done. If you have done it three times, you ask what the problem is''; it is the coordinates in which we do the work in order to be able to grasp the abstract objects. In principle one would do the same in the other chart; here it looks exactly the same (``only for completeness'') \ts{17}{1}{31:49}--\ts{17}{1}{32:30}.

\section{Step 3: the Lagrangian of the unconstrained system}\label{sec:pc-step3}

``Step 3 is actually hardly a real step'': we have written the constraints, but we must now throw them on a system which is not yet subject to constraints; \emph{Lagrangian for the unconstrained system} (``ungezwungen'', ``bad German''): for the pendulum, think the rod away \ts{17}{1}{32:53}--\ts{17}{1}{33:36}. The Lagrangian is a function on $T\mathcal Q$ of the system \emph{without} constraints; through a choice of chart $\omega$, mapping to $\R^{D}$ ($D=3N$), it has the chart representation $L_\omega=L\circ\xi_\omega^{-1}$ \ts{17}{1}{33:36}--\ts{17}{1}{34:38}.

\begin{recall}[The Lagrangian of $N$ particles (\cref{ch:c14,ch:c15})]\label{rec:pc-step3}
We have shown (last time but one) that what we now write reproduces the Newtonian second law as Euler--Lagrange equations, and that is our justification; it is always so for free particles. For $N$ particles one has a configuration space of dimension $3N$ and, in the chart $\omega$, with coordinates called $q^1,\dots,q^{3N}$ and velocities $v^1,\dots,v^{3N}$ (``names for the $2\cdot3N$ real numbers''), the sum of the kinetic energies of the particles and a potential of interaction, depending on all positions:
\[
  \begin{aligned}L_\omega(q,v)&=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tfrac12\,m_n\sum_{a,b=1}^{3}g_{ab}\,v^{3(n-1)+a}\,v^{3(n-1)+b}\\&\quad-V\bigl(q^1,\dots,q^{3N}\bigr),\end{aligned}
\]
the $g_{ab}$ depending in principle on the positions, but in flat space with the standard chart independent of them \ts{17}{1}{34:38}--\ts{17}{1}{38:32}.
\end{recall}
(The captions are very garbled here; the formula is reconstructed from the sum over the particle index $n$ with the three indices $3(n-1)+1,\dots,3n$ which Schuller writes, and from \cref{def:mp-Lsum}; the potential may include one-particle terms and coupling terms between the particles, $V$ ``irgend so was'' [?].)

In the example: $f=1$, $D=3$ and also $N=1$, so $D=3N=3$, our case. Hence the Lagrangian of the unconstrained system, in the chart $\omega$:
\[
  L_\omega(q^1,q^2,q^3,v^1,v^2,v^3)\;=\;\tfrac12\,m\,g_{ab}\,v^av^b-V(q).
\]
One has to show colours (``Farbe bekennen''): which metric and which potential? From the beginning we chose the coordinates so that in our flat absolute space the metric is the Euclidean one, $g_{ab}=\delta_{ab}$ (``the matrix with ones on the diagonal and zeros else; the index $a$ labels the rows and $b$ the columns; one sometimes says this is the \emph{standard metric}, an absurd notion, but all right'' \ts{17}{1}{39:14}--\ts{17}{1}{39:55}). Then the kinetic term is $\tfrac12m\bigl((v^1)^2+(v^2)^2+(v^3)^2\bigr)$. The potential is that of the homogeneous gravitational field: $V=mg\,q^3$ (the potential energy of a mass $m$ at height $h=q^3$ is $mgh$):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-Lfree}
  L_\omega(q,v)\;=\;\tfrac12\,m\bigl((v^1)^2+(v^2)^2+(v^3)^2\bigr)\;-\;m\,g\,q^3 .
\end{equation}

\begin{coursenote}[Where does $V=mgq^3$ come from? (as said in class)]\label{cn:pc-mgh}
``How do I get this now? Did we really derive this?'' No: it is a typical case where one says: it couples like this, and if one writes this and the equations of motion for it, they agree with what is observed. To derive it more precisely one would take the gravitational law (\cref{ch:c11}) at the surface of the Earth: one could compute the small $g$ from the mass of the Earth and Newton's gravitation theory. ``That is the general case'': here, in the general discussion, the strategy is to say what is the general case, and that one writes for particles which otherwise fly around freely by Newton's law the Lagrangian always like this; ``one can learn it by heart'' \ts{17}{1}{40:15}--\ts{17}{1}{43:01}. (Schuller also repeats the construction of the configuration space of $N$ particles ``which the substitute showed'', \cref{ch:c15}: instead of $N$ curves in absolute space one single curve in $\mathcal Q$ of dimension $3N$ \ts{17}{1}{41:37}--\ts{17}{1}{42:40}.)
\end{coursenote}

So \eqref{eq:pc-Lfree} is the next line in the exam: ``after having written the pendulum: it consists of one mass, one drops the constraint, the mass without constraint would follow this Lagrangian; this is the next line (the next green line), and the rest framed in green you only have in your head'' \ts{17}{1}{43:01}--\ts{17}{1}{43:45}.

\section{Step 4: the Lagrangian with constraints}\label{sec:pc-step4}

\begin{recall}[$L_{\mathcal Z}=L\circ h_*$ (\cref{ch:c16}, \eqref{eq:zw-LZ})]\label{rec:pc-step4}
One has $L\colon T\mathcal Q\to\R$ (step 3), and $h_*\colon T\mathcal Z\to T\mathcal Q$ (step 2): so $L\circ h_*\colon T\mathcal Z\to\R$ is a Lagrangian of the restricted system. In charts $\omega,\tilde\omega$,
\[
  L_{\mathcal Z,(\tilde\omega)}\;=\;L_{\omega}\circ\bigl(\xi_\omega\circ h_*\circ\xi_{\tilde\omega}^{-1}\bigr),
\]
and the \emph{only} thing that really has to be done is to insert \eqref{eq:pc-pushchart} into the chart representation of $L$ \ts{17}{1}{43:45}--\ts{17}{1}{48:18}.
\end{recall}
(Schuller writes this as $\xi_\omega\circ\dots$ with ``$L\circ\xi_\omega^{-1}$ gives $L_\omega$'': he notes that this ``pull-back'' is exactly the definition of $L_{\mathcal Z}$, and that the intermediate chart $\omega$ cancels out: ``actually it depends on $\omega$, but this drops out immediately'' \ts{17}{1}{46:34}--\ts{17}{1}{47:56}.)

\begin{coursenote}[A missing inverse (as said in class)]\label{cn:pc-inv2}
When the composition $\omega\circ\xi\circ\dots$ is written, an inverse chart map is missing again (``entschuldige, fehlt uns ein invers''); the audience points it out and Schuller thanks: ``correct, thank you'' \ts{17}{1}{46:13}--\ts{17}{1}{46:34}.
\end{coursenote}

$L_{\mathcal Z,(\tilde\omega)}$ depends on $2f$ real numbers $(\alpha^1,\dots,\alpha^f,\beta^1,\dots,\beta^f)$, and the prescription is: take the unconstrained $L_\omega$ and put into the $q$ and $v$ the entries of the push-forward \ts{17}{1}{48:18}--\ts{17}{1}{50:05}. For the pendulum, inserting \eqref{eq:pc-pushpend} in \eqref{eq:pc-Lfree} (the entries $q^1=l\sin\alpha$, $q^2=0$, $q^3=-l\cos\alpha$, $v^1=l\cos\alpha\,\beta$, $v^2=0$, $v^3=l\sin\alpha\,\beta$) \ts{17}{1}{50:48}--\ts{17}{1}{52:12}:
\begin{align}
  L_{\mathcal Z,(\tilde\omega)}(\alpha,\beta)
  &=\tfrac12\,m\Bigl(l^2\cos^2\!\alpha\,\beta^2+0+l^2\sin^2\!\alpha\,\beta^2\Bigr)-m\,g\,(-l\cos\alpha)\notag\\
  &=\tfrac12\,m\,l^2\,\beta^2\;+\;m\,g\,l\cos\alpha .\label{eq:pc-LZpend}
\end{align}
``Is this difficult? We are all agreed that this was only the sixth or seventh line of the calculation'' \ts{17}{1}{52:12}--\ts{17}{1}{53:58}. (The potential term: $-m\,g\,q^3$ with $q^3=-l\cos\alpha$ gives $+mgl\cos\alpha$.)

\section{Step 5: the Euler--Lagrange equations}\label{sec:pc-step5}

Now we write the Euler--Lagrange equations \ts{17}{1}{53:58}--\ts{17}{1}{54:24}, for a curve $\sigma$ on the manifold $\mathcal Z$: we do not look any longer for a curve in the large configuration space (which we have restricted), but for a curve in the small one which satisfies the constraints and makes the action stationary, since the Euler--Lagrange equations are its criterion \ts{17}{1}{54:24}--\ts{17}{1}{55:27}. For this one first needs the lift of the curve: $\sigma\colon\R\supseteq(a,b)\to\mathcal Z$ is lifted to $\tilde\sigma$ in $T\mathcal Z$, and on $\mathcal Z$ we have chosen the chart $\tilde\omega$, with the induced chart on $T\mathcal Z$; in these coordinates, with $\sigma^j(\lambda):=\tilde\omega^j(\sigma(\lambda))$ the chart components of the curve,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-lift}
  \xi_{\tilde\omega}\circ\tilde\sigma(\lambda)\;=\;\bigl(\sigma^1(\lambda),\dots,\sigma^f(\lambda),\ \dot\sigma^1(\lambda),\dots,\dot\sigma^f(\lambda)\bigr),
\end{equation}
the coordinates of the curve at the instant and, as the other $f$ entries, the derivatives with respect to the parameter: ``this is what you wrote in experimental physics 1: you modelled what your curve looks like in the configuration space, took its derivative, and the chart representation of the curve in the velocity phase space is the coordinates at the instant, with the velocities attached'' \ts{17}{1}{55:27}--\ts{17}{1}{58:51}.

\begin{coursenote}[The first curve (as said in class)]\label{cn:pc-curve}
Remark: in step 5 a \emph{curve} appears for the first time. In the whole preceding construction, in all the derivatives, one never differentiated with respect to curves and velocities, but always with respect to the $j$-th entry of a function of numbers \ts{17}{1}{58:51}--\ts{17}{1}{59:12}.
\end{coursenote}

The Euler--Lagrange equations of $L_{\mathcal Z}$ (\cref{thm:var-el}, with $\mathcal Z$ in place of $\mathcal R$) are $f$ equations, for $j=1,\dots,f$ \ts{17}{1}{59:12}--\ts{17}{1}{59:55}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-EL}
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\Bigl(\partial_{f+j}L_{\mathcal Z,(\tilde\omega)}\bigl(\xi_{\tilde\omega}\circ\tilde\sigma(\lambda)\bigr)\Bigr)-\partial_jL_{\mathcal Z,(\tilde\omega)}\bigl(\xi_{\tilde\omega}\circ\tilde\sigma(\lambda)\bigr)\;=\;0 .
\end{equation}
The clou: look at the expression $\partial_{f+j}L_{\mathcal Z,(\tilde\omega)}$ itself: there is \emph{no} dependence on $\lambda$ in it, it is only the function we wrote in step 4. One must first \emph{differentiate the function} with respect to its $(f+j)$-th entry, and \emph{then} insert the lifted curve; only then does a $\lambda$-dependence arise, and one differentiates that with respect to $\lambda$. ``In many books it says: differentiate with respect to $\dot\sigma$; this has nothing to do with the curve: first derive, then insert the lifted curve; that way round it works'' \ts{17}{1}{59:55}--\ts{17}{1}{1:04:16}, \ts{17}{1}{1:05:40}. The general part ``one need not do all of this; it is the wonderful thing'': what in the exam must be written is only the computed result, namely the derivatives $\partial_{f+j}L_{\mathcal Z}$ and $\partial_jL_{\mathcal Z}$ with $(\alpha,\beta)$ replaced by $(\sigma(\lambda),\dot\sigma(\lambda))$ \ts{17}{1}{1:00:37}--\ts{17}{1}{1:04:16}.

For the pendulum ($f=1$, so $j=1$ only), from \eqref{eq:pc-LZpend}:
\begin{align*}
  \partial_{2}L_{\mathcal Z}(\alpha,\beta)&=m\,l^2\,\beta,\ \ \text{inserted:}\ \ m\,l^2\,\dot\sigma^1(\lambda),\ \ \text{so}\ \ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\bigl(\dots\bigr)=m\,l^2\,\ddot\sigma^1(\lambda),\\
  \partial_{1}L_{\mathcal Z}(\alpha,\beta)&=-m\,g\,l\sin\alpha,\ \ \text{inserted:}\ \ -m\,g\,l\sin\sigma^1(\lambda).
\end{align*}
(the constants $m,l$ pull out of the derivative with respect to time \ts{17}{1}{1:04:59}--\ts{17}{1}{1:08:06}). The Euler--Lagrange equation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-pendEL}
  m\,l^2\,\ddot\sigma^1\;+\;m\,g\,l\,\sin\sigma^1\;=\;0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\ddot\sigma^1(\lambda)\;+\;\frac{g}{l}\,\sin\sigma^1(\lambda)\;=\;0
\end{equation}
after dividing by $m\,l$ (``you can still cancel the $m$ and divide by $l$'') \ts{17}{1}{1:08:06}--\ts{17}{1}{1:09:09}. ``If you have arrived here and have done only the green steps, you have solved the \emph{complete} problem: set up the equation of motion for this system'' \ts{17}{1}{1:09:09}--\ts{17}{1}{1:09:31}.

Remark: $\sigma$ is a curve in the coordinates we chose, $\sigma^1$ its first and only component: the angle as a function of time (the angle because we regarded the coordinate of the chart as the angle). For small angles, $\sin\sigma^1\approx\sigma^1$, and \eqref{eq:pc-pendEL} is the \emph{harmonic oscillator}, the thread pendulum in a gravitational field with thread length $l$ \ts{17}{1}{1:09:31}--\ts{17}{1}{1:10:12}.

\begin{coursenote}[Newton would also do (as said in class)]\label{cn:pc-newton}
``Of course you could also have computed this with Newtonian mechanics, forces decomposed normal and tangential to the path; you could have done all that, and maybe, if you have practised a lot, you would be as fast at the goal as I am with this method'' \ts{17}{1}{1:10:12}--\ts{17}{1}{1:10:32}. The point of the method is that it works for every system with the same five steps, and the formalism takes care of the difficulties of the forces.
\end{coursenote}

``After the break, which lasts until a bit before half past, I show you how we compute the double pendulum, and now we do only the green steps; you will see how fast it goes; this is how you execute all problems of the exercise this afternoon and in the exam'' \ts{17}{1}{1:10:32}--\ts{17}{1}{1:10:53}.

\section{The double pendulum ``hoppla hopp''}\label{sec:pc-double}

\textbf{Task:} determine the equations of motion of the system with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, rod lengths $l_1$ and $l_2$, suspended above, in the plane (the $xz$-plane) \ts{17}{1}{1:10:53}--\ts{17}{1}{1:12:23}. ``Execution of the algorithm, first step; and now I should write only in green.''

  \draw[->] (0,-4.8) -- (0,0.8) node[above] {$z$};

  \draw[dashed,gray] (a) -- ++(0,-1.7);
  \draw[thick] (a) -- (2.7,-3.4) coordinate (b); \node[right] at (1.95,-2.8) {$l_2$};
  \fill[blue!70!black] (a) circle (3pt) node[left] {$m_1$};

\subsection{Step 1}\label{ssec:pc-d1}

``What do we see? These are \emph{two} particles, so the configuration space is $\R^{3N}$ with $N=2$: $\R^6$.'' The accessible set: the first particle moves on a circle around the suspension point, and the second moves, independently of the first, on a circle around the first (the first mass may be here or there, but with respect to it the second has always a configuration $S^1$): so $\mathcal Z=S^1\times S^1$, $f=2$. Now the embedding, $\omega\circ h\circ\tilde\omega^{-1}$, with coordinates $\alpha^1,\alpha^2$ on $\mathcal Z$: the first three entries of $\R^6$ are the coordinates of the first particle, the last three those of the second \ts{17}{1}{1:12:23}--\ts{17}{1}{1:14:27}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-hdouble}
  \omega\circ h\circ\tilde\omega^{-1}(\alpha^1,\alpha^2)\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}l_1\sin\alpha^1\\0\\-l_1\cos\alpha^1\\l_1\sin\alpha^1+l_2\sin\alpha^2\\0\\-l_1\cos\alpha^1-l_2\cos\alpha^2\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The first three: exactly as for the simple pendulum; the next three: one starts where the first particle sits, and from there goes on by $l_2\sin\alpha^2,\ 0,\ -l_2\cos\alpha^2$: with respect to the first mass the second performs again a circular motion, parametrised by the angle $\alpha^2$ (``it is clear that when I insert $\alpha^1,\alpha^2$ the first three coordinates give the position of the first mass, and the last three the position of the second'') \ts{17}{1}{1:14:27}--\ts{17}{1}{1:16:13}.

\subsection{Step 2}\label{ssec:pc-d2}

The push-forward (\cref{eq:pc-pushchart}): the first six entries are \eqref{eq:pc-hdouble}; for the last six we need the derivatives of the six components with respect to the first and with respect to the second entry: ``we have six components; we differentiate them with respect to the first, then with respect to the second entry'', evaluated at $\alpha$ \ts{17}{1}{1:16:13}--\ts{17}{1}{1:17:34}. Writing the six components at once as a column vector,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-dh}
  \partial_1h\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}l_1\cos\alpha^1\\0\\l_1\sin\alpha^1\\l_1\cos\alpha^1\\0\\l_1\sin\alpha^1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
  \partial_2h\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\\l_2\cos\alpha^2\\0\\l_2\sin\alpha^2\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
so that the last six entries of the push-forward are $\partial_1h\,\beta^1+\partial_2h\,\beta^2$ \ts{17}{1}{1:17:34}--\ts{17}{1}{1:19:21}. (``Why do I need exactly these two objects? Because it is the only thing I really need of the push-forward; you can see it again by looking back at the algorithm.'' In the lecture the derivative of the fifth and sixth component with respect to $\alpha^1$ is first mis-stated with $\alpha^2$; ``there is no $\alpha^1$ in there''; ``alpha 1, thank you'' \ts{17}{1}{1:18:16}--\ts{17}{1}{1:18:38}.)

\subsection{Step 3}\label{ssec:pc-d3}

The Lagrangian of the system without constraints, in the chart $\omega$ with coordinates $q^1,\dots,q^6$ and $v^1,\dots,v^6$: two particles of different masses, both in the gravitational field; $m_1$ with $(q^1,q^2,q^3)$, $m_2$ with $(q^4,q^5,q^6)$ \ts{17}{1}{1:19:21}--\ts{17}{1}{1:20:57}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-Ldouble}
  L_\omega\;=\;\tfrac12m_1\bigl((v^1)^2+(v^2)^2+(v^3)^2\bigr)-m_1g\,q^3\;+\;\tfrac12m_2\bigl((v^4)^2+(v^5)^2+(v^6)^2\bigr)-m_2g\,q^6 .
\end{equation}
``If we did not have the pendulum rods and left the masses to their fate in the homogeneous gravitational field, this would obviously be the Lagrangian: it is child's play. We do not care how the mass $m_2$ hangs on $m_1$: the free system is simply the sum'' (\cref{def:mp-Lsum}) \ts{17}{1}{1:20:57}--\ts{17}{1}{1:22:21}.

\subsection{Step 4}\label{ssec:pc-d4}

We want $L_{\mathcal Z}$; it depends only on $\alpha^1,\alpha^2,\beta^1,\beta^2$ (``the eight generalised coordinates''; it should be four: $2f=4$; the lecturer says ``acht'' but there are four numbers). The coordinates $\omega$ do not appear any more: ``we have them all in the basket, we know what happens, we may slowly forget them; and so it is in the textbooks; but there is a difference between having forgotten what one has learned and never having learned it'' (Schuller quotes the joke that education is what is left when one has forgotten everything one has learned) \ts{17}{1}{1:22:21}--\ts{17}{1}{1:23:24}. The prescription: insert in \eqref{eq:pc-Ldouble}, for $q$, the entries \eqref{eq:pc-hdouble}, and for $v$ the entries $\partial_jh\,\beta^j$ \ts{17}{1}{1:23:24}--\ts{17}{1}{1:25:07}. The velocities of the two masses are
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  (v^1,v^2,v^3)&=\bigl(l_1\cos\alpha^1\,\beta^1,\ 0,\ l_1\sin\alpha^1\,\beta^1\bigr),\\
  (v^4,v^5,v^6)&=\bigl(l_1\cos\alpha^1\,\beta^1+l_2\cos\alpha^2\,\beta^2,\ 0,\ l_1\sin\alpha^1\,\beta^1+l_2\sin\alpha^2\,\beta^2\bigr).
  \end{aligned}
\]
Hence, for the first mass: $\tfrac12m_1\,l_1^2(\cos^2\alpha^1+\sin^2\alpha^1)(\beta^1)^2=\tfrac12m_1l_1^2(\beta^1)^2$ \ts{17}{1}{1:25:07}--\ts{17}{1}{1:27:36}; and the potential term $-m_1g\,q^3=+m_1gl_1\cos\alpha^1$. For the second mass:
\begin{align*}
    &\tfrac12m_2\Bigl[\bigl(l_1\cos\alpha^1\beta^1+l_2\cos\alpha^2\beta^2\bigr)^2+\bigl(l_1\sin\alpha^1\beta^1+l_2\sin\alpha^2\beta^2\bigr)^2\Bigr]\\
  &=\tfrac12m_2\Bigl[l_1^2(\beta^1)^2+l_2^2(\beta^2)^2+2\,l_1l_2\,\beta^1\beta^2\cos(\alpha^1-\alpha^2)\Bigr],
\end{align*}
by $\cos^2+\sin^2=1$ for the square terms and, for the mixed term, the addition theorem $\cos\alpha^1\cos\alpha^2+\sin\alpha^1\sin\alpha^2=\cos(\alpha^1-\alpha^2)$, and the potential term $-m_2g\,q^6=m_2g\,(l_1\cos\alpha^1+l_2\cos\alpha^2)$ \ts{17}{1}{1:27:36}--\ts{17}{1}{1:32:16}. The total:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-LZdouble}
  \begin{aligned}
  L_{\mathcal Z,(\tilde\omega)}(\alpha,\beta)\;=\;&\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)\,l_1^2(\beta^1)^2+\tfrac12\,m_2\,l_2^2(\beta^2)^2+m_2\,l_1l_2\,\beta^1\beta^2\cos(\alpha^1-\alpha^2)\\
  &+(m_1+m_2)\,g\,l_1\cos\alpha^1+m_2\,g\,l_2\cos\alpha^2 ,
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
``modulo nonsense, this is the Lagrangian we want'' \ts{17}{1}{1:33:20}--\ts{17}{1}{1:34:03}.

\begin{coursenote}[Slips while simplifying (as said in class)]\label{cn:pc-slips}
While writing the second component of the second mass a wrong index appears: ``$l_1\cos\alpha^2$'' where $\alpha^1$ is meant (``here must stand $\alpha^1$'') \ts{17}{1}{1:29:21}--\ts{17}{1}{1:30:05}. When the mixed term has to be simplified Schuller stops (``who can do the addition theorems? stop, stop, I cannot ... ''), a student supplies it and Schuller says ``now it is your fault'' \ts{17}{1}{1:32:16}--\ts{17}{1}{1:33:20}. That ``Lagrangians are simplified'' is a popular exercise, and it need not be done in the exam: ``nobody must really do this''.
\end{coursenote}

\subsection{Step 5}\label{ssec:pc-d5}

Now the Euler--Lagrange equations \ts{17}{1}{1:34:03}--\ts{17}{1}{1:34:26}; there are two, $j=1,2$: $f=2$. Compute the four partial derivatives of \eqref{eq:pc-LZdouble} \emph{first}, with respect to the entries $\alpha^1,\alpha^2,\beta^1,\beta^2$, and then insert $(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto(\sigma^1(\lambda),\sigma^2(\lambda),\dot\sigma^1(\lambda),\dot\sigma^2(\lambda))$; write $\Delta:=\sigma^1-\sigma^2$ for short \ts{17}{1}{1:34:26}--\ts{17}{1}{1:35:08}.
\begin{align*}
  \partial_{1}L_{\mathcal Z}&=-m_2\,l_1l_2\,\dot\sigma^1\dot\sigma^2\,\sin\Delta\;-\;(m_1+m_2)\,g\,l_1\,\sin\sigma^1 ,\\
  \partial_{2}L_{\mathcal Z}&=+m_2\,l_1l_2\,\dot\sigma^1\dot\sigma^2\,\sin\Delta\;-\;m_2\,g\,l_2\,\sin\sigma^2 ,\\
  \partial_{3}L_{\mathcal Z}&=(m_1+m_2)\,l_1^2\,\dot\sigma^1\;+\;m_2\,l_1l_2\,\dot\sigma^2\cos\Delta ,\\
  \partial_{4}L_{\mathcal Z}&=m_2\,l_2^2\,\dot\sigma^2\;+\;m_2\,l_1l_2\,\dot\sigma^1\cos\Delta .
\end{align*}
Here the inner derivative of $\cos(\alpha^1-\alpha^2)$ with respect to $\alpha^2$ is $-1$, which turns the sign of the term in $\partial_2L_{\mathcal Z}$ (``the inner derivative is $-1$, so it becomes plus'', \ts{17}{1}{1:35:08}--\ts{17}{1}{1:37:17}); the third and fourth are the derivatives with respect to $\beta^1$ and $\beta^2$ \ts{17}{1}{1:37:17}--\ts{17}{1}{1:39:10}.

\begin{coursenote}[The factor of the cross term (my check, not said in class)]\label{cn:pc-cross}
At \ts{17}{1}{1:35:29} the captions give the cross term of the first derivative as ``$2l_1l_2\beta^1\beta^2$'' times a sine; with the prefactor $\tfrac12m_2$ of \eqref{eq:pc-LZdouble} before simplification, the net coefficient is $m_2l_1l_2$, as written above (recomputed, since the spoken formula has a stray factor 2 [?]).
\end{coursenote}

It remains to differentiate the last two with respect to $\lambda$ (product and chain rule):
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\partial_3L_{\mathcal Z}&=(m_1+m_2)\,l_1^2\,\ddot\sigma^1+m_2\,l_1l_2\,\ddot\sigma^2\cos\Delta-m_2\,l_1l_2\,\dot\sigma^2\sin\Delta\,\bigl(\dot\sigma^1-\dot\sigma^2\bigr),\\
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\partial_4L_{\mathcal Z}&=m_2\,l_2^2\,\ddot\sigma^2+m_2\,l_1l_2\,\ddot\sigma^1\cos\Delta-m_2\,l_1l_2\,\dot\sigma^1\sin\Delta\,\bigl(\dot\sigma^1-\dot\sigma^2\bigr).
\end{align*}
\ts{17}{1}{1:39:10}--\ts{17}{1}{1:41:57} (``it would be clumsy to write the whole new Lagrangian at once; do it step by step''). Schuller then says: ``so there they stand, the Euler--Lagrange equations'', and that one may now write them explicitly; there are two, as the space of accessible configurations is two-dimensional; for the writing out he gives the equations the names of a filled and an empty heart, a filled and an empty diamond, and ``full marks'' (the captions skip about a minute here [?], \ts{17}{1}{1:41:57}--\ts{17}{1}{1:43:00}). Assembled as $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\partial_3L_{\mathcal Z}-\partial_1L_{\mathcal Z}=0$ and $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\partial_4L_{\mathcal Z}-\partial_2L_{\mathcal Z}=0$ and simplified (\emph{not said in class}: my assembly, the equations were not written out on the board in the captions):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pc-ELdouble}
  \begin{aligned}
  (m_1+m_2)\,l_1^2\,\ddot\sigma^1+m_2\,l_1l_2\,\ddot\sigma^2\cos\Delta+m_2\,l_1l_2\,(\dot\sigma^2)^2\sin\Delta+(m_1+m_2)\,g\,l_1\sin\sigma^1&=0,\\
  m_2\,l_2^2\,\ddot\sigma^2+m_2\,l_1l_2\,\ddot\sigma^1\cos\Delta-m_2\,l_1l_2\,(\dot\sigma^1)^2\sin\Delta+m_2\,g\,l_2\sin\sigma^2&=0 .
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
(Check of the first: the terms $-m_2l_1l_2\dot\sigma^2\sin\Delta\,(\dot\sigma^1-\dot\sigma^2)$ and $+m_2l_1l_2\dot\sigma^1\dot\sigma^2\sin\Delta$, the second from $-\partial_1L_{\mathcal Z}$, add up to $m_2l_1l_2(\dot\sigma^2)^2\sin\Delta$; the second equation similarly: $-m_2l_1l_2(\dot\sigma^1)^2\sin\Delta$.)

\begin{exercise}[Small oscillations of the double pendulum (announced for the problem sheet)]\label{ex:pc-small}
Linearise \eqref{eq:pc-ELdouble} for small $\sigma^1,\sigma^2$ (keep terms first order in the angles, so $\cos\Delta\approx1$, $\sin\Delta\approx\Delta$ and the terms in $(\dot\sigma)^2\sin\Delta$ drop), and solve the linear system. Schuller: ``if I asked you to solve this, I would probably say: for small $\sigma^1$ and small $\sigma^2$, and one can see something; and that I might put on the problem sheet'' \ts{17}{1}{1:43:22}--\ts{17}{1}{1:43:43}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{coursenote}[How much work was this? (as said in class)]\label{cn:pc-work}
``But let us look, in earnest, at what we have computed: a really complicated system, the double pendulum, not simple; I have even cheated a bit, since I shoved something away up there; but we have done it essentially on three, three to four boards; and these are the steps you must go through in the exam for \emph{every} system.'' Others can be treated this way: the dumbbells of the beginning, ``and one could do even worse things with the swinging hand'' (Schuller ends, jokingly, with the picture he drew at the start) \ts{17}{1}{1:43:43}--\ts{17}{1}{1:44:39}.
\end{coursenote}

\section*{Key concepts}
\begin{keyconcepts}
\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item List the five steps of the algorithm and say which one is the only one that needs thinking.
  \item For a pendulum in the $xz$-plane: what are $\mathcal Q$, $\mathcal Z$, $h$ (in a chart)? Why does $S^1$ need two charts?
  \item Write the chart representation of $h_*$; what are its $2D$ entries?
  \item Write $L_\omega$ for $N$ particles in a homogeneous gravitational field; what is $L_{\mathcal Z}$ of the pendulum?
  \item Why must one differentiate $L_{\mathcal Z}$ \emph{before} inserting the lifted curve?
  \item Derive the equation of motion of the pendulum; what does it become for small angles?
  \item Repeat the five steps for the double pendulum: $h$, $\partial_jh$, $L_{\mathcal Z}$, the two equations.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Concepts.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The algorithm (\cref{def:pc-algorithm}, \cref{cn:pc-f}).
  \item Step 1: embedding of the pendulum (\eqref{eq:pc-h}); of the double pendulum (\eqref{eq:pc-hdouble}).
  \item Step 2: push-forward in coordinates (\eqref{eq:pc-pushchart}, \eqref{eq:pc-pushpend}, \eqref{eq:pc-dh}).
  \item Steps 3 and 4: $L_\omega$ and $L_{\mathcal Z}=L\circ h_*$ (\eqref{eq:pc-Lfree}, \eqref{eq:pc-LZpend}, \eqref{eq:pc-Ldouble}, \eqref{eq:pc-LZdouble}).
  \item Step 5: Euler--Lagrange equations of the pendulum and double pendulum (\eqref{eq:pc-EL}, \eqref{eq:pc-pendEL}, \eqref{eq:pc-ELdouble}).
\end{itemize}
\end{keyconcepts}
