\section{What is the aim of physics?}\label{sec:bv-aim}

The lecture opens with questions to the audience \ts{1}{1}{0:00}. First: what is the aim of physics?
The first answers are ``to describe nature'', then ``reproducible natural phenomena, not as a historian would'',
``understand how nature works'' \ts{1}{1}{0:21}. Schuller objects that one can also just
look at what happens: throwing a piece of chalk, it flies on something like a parabola, and that is already
``explained'' by watching \ts{1}{1}{1:23}. Asked to a teaching-degree student, ``what is the real punch line of
physics'', the answer is: \emph{predict} \ts{1}{1}{1:48}. That is the right track: physics is the
subject that predicts the future \ts{1}{1}{2:32}.

The next question is what the aim of \emph{theoretical} physics is \ts{1}{1}{3:08}. Student answers: from known
rules to infer other rules; once one understood a partial aspect one can infer other things (``connecting the dots'');
build models \ts{1}{1}{3:34}. But the experimentalist would claim to do that too.
Schuller borrows from Wittgenstein \ts{1}{1}{4:39}:

\begin{definition}[New concepts: aim of theoretical physics]\label{def:bv-aim}
The aim of theoretical physics is to say clearly everything that can be said (Wittgenstein: \emph{what can be said
at all can be said clearly}) \ts{1}{1}{4:59}.
\end{definition}
Clearly saying something means developing a language, and the language is mathematics \ts{1}{1}{5:20}.
There is no way around it, and it would be a pity not to use it: once something is written down cleanly, nothing can
go wrong any more; if it is to mean anything at all, it must be sayable clearly \ts{1}{1}{5:48}.
Why this ``fetish for precision''? In situations where rules of thumb no longer work, e.g.\ shooting the first
rocket to the moon (an old hat), or applying physics where we have little intuition and experience, very far out or
very small, we rely on the precision of the language to infer, from facts already established reliably, other facts
\ts{1}{1}{6:08}.

\section{Theoretical and experimental physics}\label{sec:bv-theory-exp}

What is the relation between theoretical physics and experimental physics \ts{1}{1}{7:13}?

\begin{aside}[Hilbert on pure and applied mathematics]
Told in class \ts{1}{1}{7:37}: Felix Klein of Erlangen, ``Hans Dampf in all alleys'', was to give a talk (at the
German mathematicians' association or an engineers' congress, Schuller is not sure), but could not, and asked
Hilbert, the pillar saint of mathematics, who was known for insisting on pure mathematics and brushing applications
aside, to give it instead, asking him please not to alienate anybody \ts{1}{1}{7:58}.
Hilbert began by saying that it is sometimes claimed that pure and applied mathematics (or the art of engineering)
are hostile to each other; this is not so, and cannot be: how could it be, they have nothing at all to do with each
other. The same joke can be told about theoretical and experimental physicists \ts{1}{1}{9:01}.
\end{aside}

\subsection{The Siamese twins}\label{ssec:bv-twins}

On the problem sheet (task~4, top right) there is a small picture that has nothing to do with the exercise
\ts{1}{1}{9:21}. Philosophising about it gives the whole relation between theory and experiment
\ts{1}{1}{9:42}. It shows two girls joined at the back, i.e.\ Siamese twins (not Janus-headed; the
different viewing directions are just perspective) \ts{1}{1}{10:02}. Now an eight-year-old child asks
what Siamese twins are; we describe them more exactly. The \emph{observation} is: two girls grown together at the
bottom \ts{1}{1}{10:44}. Everybody can agree that this is a valid description of the
situation in the world of goods \ts{1}{1}{12:14}.

The punch line \ts{1}{1}{12:34}: this was experimental physics. One sees something and describes it as aptly as
possible, abstracting many things (the sex was kept: ``two girls grown together at the bottom'')
\ts{1}{1}{12:55}. Now suppose the world were such that \emph{all} girls are born this way, there are only Siamese
twins, and those sitting in the audience were perhaps separated later. Then the same situation, newly born, would
be nothing special; in particular there would be \emph{no} observation of the kind ``two girls'' (why two? it would
be one) and none of the kind ``grown together at the bottom'' (it could just as well be at the shoulder)
\ts{1}{1}{13:16}.

\begin{intuition}[Theory before observation]
A situation in nature can be described with language only if we have, \emph{before} the observation, a theory, in
this case a theory of what the entities of the world are (here: how many ``girls'' there are, what counts as being
``grown together''). Even perception is influenced by it \ts{1}{1}{13:59}.
\end{intuition}
So the conclusion is that one needs a \emph{preceding theory} (a pre-theory) to even begin to capture observations
in language. One could pedantically object that some theory is formed only in connection with the observation; but in
principle one needs one before beginning \ts{1}{1}{15:04}. What can be said about this
preceding theory? The ``what is normal, what do we see most often'' of the Siamese example (a $60:40$ split of the
world shows that it cannot define the usual) was just a pedagogical trick to get the statements Schuller wanted
\ts{1}{1}{15:48}. The truth is that the \emph{concepts} of the theory are, at first, completely arbitrary
\ts{1}{1}{16:29}. To formulate an observation we must first invent a system of concepts (arbitrary), and with it we
look at the world \ts{1}{1}{16:29}.

Example: the chalk falls. To say this at all we need a concept of \emph{time} (``falls faster and faster''), a concept
of \emph{space}, even of distance in space, and a concept of \emph{particle} (``a holy halo around the particle''
is the joke: before one has fixed these concepts, everything is babble) \ts{1}{1}{16:50}.
Without theory no observation: that is the connection between theoretical and experimental physics
\ts{1}{1}{17:55}.

\subsection{Concepts come from the theory, equations from the experiment}\label{ssec:bv-concepts}

Objection: one can make theories as one likes, but what if the observations do not agree? ``Theory'' here means at
first concepts, such as \emph{energy} and \emph{mass} \ts{1}{1}{18:15}. They can be invented freely,
with a special demand that holds in physics (unlike other humanities): the theoretical concepts must be
\emph{mathematical objects}, so that we can handle them; e.g.\ energy is a positive real number, mass is a positive
real number (one could have other ideas) \ts{1}{1}{18:56}. A second demand: each concept must be
connected with a \emph{measurement prescription}: how to determine mass, how to compare masses, in the experiment.
In (modern) physics we want only measurable concepts; the concept is not a mere figment of the mind
\ts{1}{1}{19:37}.

Energy and mass are two completely separate concepts, ``cut apart intellectually'' like the two girls (the scissors
\emph{snip-snap} in the head). The Siamese-twin situation is described as two girls grown together at the bottom.
Then comes observation, and suddenly one finds that when one takes the practical representatives of these objects and
introduces a conversion constant, they belong together (the $E=mc^2$ of the atomic bomb) \ts{1}{1}{20:18}.
So the theory first cuts everything into nice categories that have nothing to do with each other; then the experiment
comes, and the fundamental equations of physics, the laws of nature, \emph{identify} concepts that were separated
intellectually, arbitrarily. That is the feedback of the experiment: \textbf{the concepts come from the preceding
theory, the equations of physics come from the experiment} \ts{1}{1}{21:01}.
A proper analysis would be a lecture of its own; here it only serves to say that theory is not a mere appendage of
the observers \ts{1}{1}{22:04}.

\begin{history}[Faraday, Maxwell, Einstein]
\emph{As said in class (not checked)} \ts{1}{1}{22:24}: Faraday could not do mathematics; he played with magnets (like his French
colleague Ampere) and invented the field lines, ``this insane pedagogical instrument of the school''. Maxwell then
wrote the laws of Ampere and Faraday together and, noticing that the conservation of current required it, added a
term (the displacement current); the Maxwell equations were done. Einstein then looked at them and found that the
world described by Maxwell's equations is special-relativistic; gravity must then fit in, which leads to general
relativity \ts{1}{1}{22:44}.\end{history}

That is why this course, although about classical mechanics, \emph{ends} with special and general relativity
\ts{1}{1}{23:48}. Why was this not found earlier? That is the mercy of hindsight: the experiment
gives us the equation, the law of nature, and thus tells us that we made a mistake in the conceptualisation: every
fundamental equation of physics says that the theorists separated what is not separate \ts{1}{1}{24:09}.

\subsection{Inertial and heavy mass}\label{ssec:bv-mass}

A second example, known from school: $m\cdot a=F$ \ts{1}{1}{24:51}. The mass here has a first name:
\textbf{inertial mass} (träge Masse). The force law for gravity, which one also knows from school, contains a mass
again: the force on an object depends on the mass of the object on which it acts, and on the mass of the body
generating it (a point mass), with a $1/r^2$ (as a vector $\propto \vec r/r^3$ giving the direction) and a constant
because one wants to choose units \ts{1}{1}{25:14}. This mass in the
gravitational law is conceptually the \emph{charge} of the particle that reacts to gravity (the charge that feels this
force), called \textbf{heavy mass} (schwere Masse) or gravitational charge \ts{1}{1}{26:19}.

\begin{equation}
  m_{\mathrm{in}}\,a \;=\; F \;=\; G\,\frac{m_{\mathrm{heavy}}\,M}{r^2}\quad\text{(schematically)}.
\end{equation}
At school one then calmly \emph{cancels} the two masses \ts{1}{1}{26:39}. Is that right or wrong?
Answer: it is right, but why is it allowed? Here ``inertial'' stands and there ``heavy'' \ts{1}{1}{27:01}.
Compare the thread pendulum from experimental physics: the oscillation frequency is $\sqrt{g/\ell}$, \emph{no mass
appears}, neither the inertial nor the heavy one: they were cancelled \ts{1}{1}{27:44}.
(``If I hang up a pendulum of ten kilograms and sit on the bob, swinging along, nothing changes''; the ceiling might not
hold.) The pendulum does not depend on the mass, and that says something not about the pendulum but about the
\emph{universe}: gravitation accelerates all objects, whatever their mass, equally \ts{1}{1}{28:54}. Vacuum,
cannon ball, feather: they fall next to each other; the pendulum and the inclined plane are the same thing
reduced \ts{1}{1}{30:00}.

So we have a fundamental observation (from pendulums, inclined planes, the tower of Pisa, whatever): \textbf{the inertial
mass equals the heavy mass} (in suitable units, otherwise up to a constant factor of proportionality)
\ts{1}{1}{30:20}. Conceptually, inertial mass in Newtonian mechanics is something completely different
from gravitational charge; yet nature says the two concepts are the same \ts{1}{1}{30:41}.

\begin{intuition}[The equivalence principle and the idea of general relativity]
Einstein looked at this and said: if so, then the motion of particles under the influence of gravity alone can be
described \emph{without stating the mass of the particle}, since the motion is independent of it. That is the whole
basis, the core idea, of general relativity: it did not fall from the sky, but comes from a fundamental insight that
concepts which had always been kept apart are fundamentally the same \ts{1}{1}{31:02}.
\end{intuition}
If one day an experiment measures a difference (``no, the cannon ball is accelerated differently in vacuum than the
feather''), then general relativity, our best theory of space and time, is not just a bit wrong, but dead; at the end
of the course it will be shown that it is dead anyway (the remark ``mausetot'' of the lecture; the sense is
that the theory is only as good as the observation underlying it) \ts{1}{1}{31:42}. It is therefore
extremely important to be clear about the preceding concepts and about the observations that follow from them;
the theorist then takes those observations again and tries to make a new theory that might not have these concepts at
all, because they were never separate. That is also what the student said at the start: after writing everything
down pedantically and mathematically precisely, one wants more \ts{1}{1}{32:03}.
Classical mechanics is more than 300 years old in this form, and one can still learn from it today, also in research,
because looking at the same physics in a different conceptual framework gives new results. ``This is not the
beginners' lecture, it is already the master class of theoretical physics'' \ts{1}{1}{32:45}.

\paragraph{Summary so far.} The subject of physics is predicting the future; the aim of theoretical physics is
that everything that can be said is said clearly; the relation to experiment is that theory precedes observation
(concepts), while the experiment feeds back the identification of concepts through equations
\ts{1}{1}{33:27}. After a question round \ts{1}{1}{34:52}: what is the subject of
theoretical mechanics?

\section{The subject: Newton's \emph{Principia}}\label{sec:bv-newton}

The subject is to make precise, ``to the point of no return'', what Newton said in 1687 in his
\emph{Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica}, ``the mathematical principles of natural philosophy''
\ts{1}{1}{35:13}. Natural philosophy means philosophising, forming concepts
of nature, here cast in mathematical concepts. And Newton does not write a single formula: he does everything
geometrically (``geometric pictures''). Schuller thinks no one wrote formulas in the Middle Ages and that
equals signs are a relatively recent invention (not sure when); calculations were done in giant prose: ``take the
square above the diameter and ...'' \ts{1}{1}{36:24}.

Newton says essentially five things (the absolute core, worth looking at in the original) \ts{1}{1}{37:06}.
The underlined words in the following are the ones that must be clarified, i.e.\ given a mathematical definition
\ts{1}{1}{41:05}:

\begin{definition}[Newton's five statements, in paraphrase]\label{def:bv-newton}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \underline{Absolute time} flows evenly, regardless of external influences \ts{1}{1}{37:26}.
  \item \underline{Absolute space} exists and remains always equal and immovable, independent of external influences
        \ts{1}{1}{38:48}.
  \item (first law) A \underline{body} on which no \underline{force} acts moves in an \underline{inertial system}
        \underline{straight and uniformly} \ts{1}{1}{41:05}.
  \item (second law) The \underline{deviation} of the motion of a body from straight uniform motion is proportional
        (in an inertial system) to the force acting on the body \ts{1}{1}{42:38}.
  \item (third law) The \underline{interaction} between two bodies takes place through equal and opposite forces
        (action equals reaction) \ts{1}{1}{44:46}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
The third statement is formulated here ``as it pleases me'' \ts{1}{1}{45:06}.

\paragraph{What do the underlined words mean?}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Absolute time}: ``it ticks along, ticks everywhere, and simultaneously'' sounds good, but what on earth
        is absolute time as a mathematical object? To be clarified, not yet today. And ``flows evenly'': at what speed?
        ``One second per second'', ha \ts{1}{1}{37:47}.
  \item \emph{Absolute space}: ``I am now immovable'' is fine: moving means going from one point of space to another.
        But ``space itself is immovable'': does space not move with respect to space? We will give it sense, but we
        also learn to appreciate that it is not at all clear what is meant. In any case we need a mathematical object, and
        then that object \emph{is} the absolute space; and then it turns out (Schuller's quasi-spoiler) that this object
        is not suited to determine an absolute space (the experiment hits one over the head) \ts{1}{1}{39:09}.
  \item \emph{Body, force, moves, inertial system, straight, uniformly}: nobody knows what a body is; what is a force?
        what does ``moves'' mean? what is an inertial system? what are ``straight'' and ``uniform''? ``All these
        expressions need a precise mathematical definition, otherwise it is babble'' \ts{1}{1}{42:14}.
  \item \emph{Deviation} (second law): presumably acceleration, but proportional to a force, and ``force'' was already
        underlined above. ``One can know what \emph{no} force is without having a quantitative measure of forces''
        \ts{1}{1}{44:04}.
  \item \emph{Interaction and ``equal and opposite''} (third law): what is an interaction, and what is opposite
        \ts{1}{1}{45:30}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The forbidden position vector}\label{ssec:bv-position}

Experimental physics formulates this in an accessible way, familiar from school, or uses \emph{position vectors}
\ts{1}{1}{45:51}. Schuller's statement:

\begin{theorem}[Rule of the course]\label{thm:bv-no-position-vector}
The concept of a position vector is \textbf{forbidden}. There are no position vectors; \emph{places are not vectors}.
Velocity vectors are allowed: velocities \emph{are} vectors \ts{1}{1}{46:12}.
\end{theorem}
Anyone who objects now will see why only when it is written down. One takes the position vector $\vec x$, differentiates
twice and writes $m\ddot{\vec x}=\vec F$ and claims that this is the second law. ``With much love one can say it
is not entirely wrong'', and it is good that the audience did it, otherwise there would be no point in the lecture
\ts{1}{1}{46:35}. Do not tell anybody afterwards, as it is what stands in books, but
the questions that are always asked in a mechanics lecture (``we now consider this so or that object as such and not
as such'') cannot be answered like that: we introduce mathematical concepts and definitions, and they stay what they
are; one cannot say ``let us now consider the position as a vector'' since nobody knows what that is. We will
come back to it \ts{1}{1}{47:16}.

\subsection{Is the first law not a special case of the second?}\label{ssec:bv-first-second}

Student-style question from Schuller \ts{1}{1}{47:58}: ``a stupid question: Newton cannot have had much clue about
actions\,\ldots\ the first law is a special case of the second, is it not?'' If the deviation of the motion of a
body from straight uniform motion is proportional to the force, then with force zero the deviation is zero, i.e.\ the
body moves straight and uniformly: that is the first law. So the first law would be superfluous \ts{1}{1}{48:19}.

\begin{aside}[Joke: how much do theory, experiment and philosophy cost?]
Told in class \ts{1}{1}{49:00}: experimental physicists go to the minister of science and ask for a billion for an
accelerator. The minister says finances are tight; why can they not do as the theorists, who need only paper and
pencil, or even better the philosophers, who do not even need the wastepaper basket?
\end{aside}
No: this is not paper saving or a historical tribute \ts{1}{1}{49:20}. The first law wants something
quite different from the second \ts{1}{1}{50:06}. The first law does not say what a body does; it says what
\emph{straight} and \emph{uniform} mean:

\begin{intuition}[The first law defines the geometry]
Take a big frozen lake and shoot a puck: it moves straight and uniformly until friction \ts{1}{1}{50:06}.
But what is a straight line? A straight line is what a body on which no force acts does; this is an assumption, taken
up later. The first law \emph{defines the geometry of space}, at least the straight lines: if you know all straight
lines you know almost everything about the geometry \ts{1}{1}{50:48}.\par
Suppose the whole Earth surface is an ideal spherical ice surface, all gravity switched off, and the puck is shot from
somewhere, say at the north pole: it runs on a great circle. So in a curved geometry the straight lines are great circles
(a two-dimensional example) \ts{1}{1}{51:30}.\par
Another example: you are lost in space, no landmark in sight, all empty. You take a handful of sand you took from Earth
as a memento and throw it out: each grain follows a trajectory, and the first law instructs you to proclaim
\emph{these are the straight lines}. So the first law does not say how particles move; it is a \emph{measurement
prescription} for the concept ``straight line'', which is a theoretical construct (``has anyone ever seen a straight
line?''). The ``uniformly'' (equal paths in equal time intervals) will be made precise later \ts{1}{1}{52:11}.
\end{intuition}

\paragraph{The problem of force-free matter.} To use the first law as a measuring instrument one needs matter on which no
force acts \ts{1}{1}{53:56}. But by the pendulum insight, on \emph{every} massive particle, whatever its mass, the
gravitational force acts; already in a universe with only two particles, each acts on the other, so there is no
force-free particle. So the first law would have no domain of application, since it is conditioned on the absence of
force and forces act on all massive bodies (``non-massive bodies do not exist in non-relativistic physics, they
appear only in relativity'') \ts{1}{1}{53:56}. Ways out:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A universe with a single body: purely academic \ts{1}{1}{55:19}. (Such things are born from theorists'
        nonsense, today called \emph{brane-world scenarios} and the like; far-fetched, ``maybe one can do something
        like that'' \ts{1}{1}{55:39}.)
  \item Arrange that at the test position the gravitational forces of all masses balance (a universe with much matter
        distributed roughly uniformly: in all directions you are pulled equally, so effectively force-free). A very good
        idea, but you must then arrange a particle here and a particle there, so you can only test straight lines
        at positions of force equilibrium \ts{1}{1}{55:39}.
  \item A far more obvious idea (``the secret''): do \emph{not} regard gravity as a force at all \ts{1}{1}{56:20}.
        ``Total nonsense?'' Gravity is also a force that accelerates, but it can be understood as a feature of the
        foundation, the geometry, of the Newtonian spacetime, and we will do this. Then every particle without any
        further charge (electric charge; classically nothing else) can be used, and these particles satisfy the first
        law \ts{1}{1}{56:40}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Spacetime instead of space}\label{ssec:bv-spacetime}

Then ``straight and uniform'' can be combined: not ``straight in space and uniform in time'', but just \emph{straight
in spacetime} \ts{1}{1}{57:21}. The distinction of the two conditions is owed to the fact that physics is
always looked at in space rather than in spacetime \ts{1}{1}{57:41}. Everybody knows the $s$-$t$ (position-time)
diagram from school: when am I where; add two more space dimensions and one has spacetime, ``not quite so simple, but
almost''. \textbf{Spacetime is the right concept to store all the data we have about the universe}: if two persons met,
one slapped the other, who walked away crying, it is all in the spacetime diagram. So we put our physics onto it and
bring this antiquated formulation up to date \ts{1}{1}{57:41}.

The first law is about ``straight, uniform''; the second law speaks of the deviation of the motion, which is also
geometric, and this is where \emph{force} is defined \ts{1}{1}{58:43}. The third law is about
\emph{interaction between particles} \ts{1}{1}{58:43}.

\subsection{The third law and the love story}\label{ssec:bv-third}

What is special about the third law? It seems to contradict much literature and the dramas of world history: what is a
love story? Traditionally two men, one woman, a duel and all that. What would the two men have done without the woman?
Physics. But now the woman is there: the interaction of the two men (how they look at each other, which projectiles
they throw at each other) depends on whether there is a woman or not \ts{1}{1}{59:06}.

The third law says something else: with three bodies, the two behave differently when the third is there, but you can
reduce the total behaviour to \emph{pairwise} interactions: it is enough to treat couple by couple. There may be a
complicated total effect, but the mere existence of the third particle changes nothing fundamental in the interaction of
the first two \ts{1}{1}{1:00:09}. So ``action equals reaction'' makes an
enormous assumption, which Schuller finds ``rather daring'': he knows no counterexample, but theoretically why
should there not be genuine three-body interactions, rather than only two-body ones \ts{1}{1}{1:00:50}.

\paragraph{Goal.} To give an exact mathematical formulation of all these concepts, to write the laws down and to
derive everything one never wanted to know: the Coriolis force, the centrifugal force, the Euler force. Then, coming
from this spacetime formulation, the famous reformulations of mechanics, the Lagrangian and the Hamiltonian
mechanics (generalised coordinates, which are on every curriculum), follow ``by hand'' without effort: a bit of work at the
beginning to understand what is written, then everything is clear \ts{1}{1}{1:01:11}.

\section{Structure of the lecture}\label{sec:bv-structure}

Now an outline of the course; the recording is available the same day on the internet (useful if one was ill), and it
is helpful to return to this outline during the semester to see where we are, what has been reached and what the
logical status of what we do is \ts{1}{1}{1:02:13}.

\paragraph{Sets.} The fundamental object to control is \emph{spacetime}, and for a clean mathematical definition we start
at the very bottom: the notion of a \textbf{set} \ts{1}{1}{1:03:15}. The points of the
space are the elements of a set; on the coarsest level, with least structure, it is a loose set of objects. With
infinite sets one cannot be as naive as in school (``intersection, union'') and as one often is in the first
semesters, where the lecturer only does things he knows work. There is a proper axiomatisation of set theory
(Zermelo--Fraenkel, plus the axiom of choice), and the course \emph{Geometric Anatomy of Theoretical Physics}
(last semester, online) has several lectures on sets and the foundations; it is absolutely interesting but not needed,
the naive notion of set suffices here \ts{1}{1}{1:04:17}.

\paragraph{Manifolds.} A set is rather wild, with essentially no structure except cardinality, and the next concept is a
big jump: the \textbf{manifold} (Mannigfaltigkeit) \ts{1}{1}{1:05:19}. The idea is
simple, and everybody knows one:

\begin{intuition}[The atlas]
You want to drive around the world by car (a world without oceans, only desert), with an atlas of the world:
many A4 pages, each showing a part of the world distorted (the surface is curved, so flattening distorts it); the earth
surface is paved by such regions, each drawn distorted in the book \ts{1}{1}{1:06:05}.
You start at page 3\,174, drive along the road, and arrive at the edge of the page: you turn to the page which continues
it, which \emph{overlaps} with the previous one (a good atlas, a quality ``manifold atlas'', has overlaps, otherwise
the switching would not work) \ts{1}{1}{1:07:28}.\par
Now suppose you are a captain in a cabin during a storm: out of boredom tear all maps out, take glue and glue them
together along the overlap regions. What do you get? (An audience member says ``a half map'', then ``a globe''.)
A one-sided printed, orderly atlas: you get essentially the \emph{Earth's surface}, a ball, if you pull a bit (the
paper is very patient) \ts{1}{1}{1:08:30}.\par
Now give the man another atlas, from another planet (say Donut), made in the same way: the glued result might look like
an \emph{inner tube} (car tyre). What does it tell us? That \textbf{the global structure of the space is encoded in the
overlap regions of an atlas}: one must look at how one changes from one chart to another; all information about the
global structure of the space whose atlas it is lies there \ts{1}{1}{1:10:58}.
\end{intuition}

\paragraph{Why use an atlas at all?} Why not look directly at the Earth surface? \ts{1}{1}{1:12:00}
Because from the physicist's viewpoint a chart is a piece of $\R^2$ (for the surface), $\R^3$ for space, $\R^4$ for
spacetime. In $\R^2$ one can do a lot: consider curves, differentiate them, consider functions that assign a number to
each point, take gradients, and differentiating requires real numbers (one cannot differentiate over the rational
numbers) \ts{1}{1}{1:12:21}. So one describes the whole world by charts that encode the global
structure in the overlaps, and inside a chart one can do what one always did: differentiate, take the components of
position and of vectors. These are all concepts that \emph{refer to a chart}. And one can choose among different charts
(a Diercke atlas, a Westermann atlas, \dots): one must make a theory that describes the world independently of the atlas,
but \emph{through} atlases \ts{1}{1}{1:13:02}. This is the idea of a manifold. It is
presented at length because we will spend two or three weeks with it, and the idea is brilliant.

\begin{definition}[Programme of the course, part one]\label{def:bv-programme}
Up to now, in school and experimental physics, physics was done \emph{only in the chart}, and chart changes were rarely
considered. Here we study what happens in charts as the image of what happens in the world (the manifold); charts are our
technical instrument \ts{1}{1}{1:13:46}.
\end{definition}
If this is understood technically (``I do my best to explain it precisely'') then one has captured the whole of
theoretical physics technically, ``except for a bit of quantum mechanics and functional analysis''; so this is absolutely
important for mechanics, for all further theoretical physics and for understanding physics as a whole
\ts{1}{1}{1:14:27}.

\paragraph{Affine manifolds (connections).} The next concept is an \textbf{affine manifold}: a manifold on which one
knows what \emph{parallelism} means \ts{1}{1}{1:15:08}. ``But we know that already'': I draw a little vector attached
to a point (the velocity vector of a particle) and another one elsewhere; surely everybody sees they are parallel,
because I can shift one to the other \ts{1}{1}{1:15:08}. But who says one must shift
them \emph{like that}? Perhaps one should shift them in another way to compare them, and then no one would say they are
parallel. In a chart one can say that the components of the vector stay equal; but take another chart, for example polar
coordinates: a vector with components $(0,1)$ in the $r$- and $\varphi$-directions, transported to another point with the
same components, no longer ``points the same way''; if the vector is parallel, it suddenly has components in the $r$
direction (and in $\varphi$), yet we would still call it parallel \ts{1}{1}{1:15:50}.

In short, it is a crazy idea to define the parallelism of vectors by equality of components. There is no absolute
parallelism that would just be there; it is \emph{extra geometric structure} that must be specified. It turns out that at
every point of the manifold one must give $d^3$ numbers (more cleverly, $d^2(d+1)/2$ if one is a bit careful), where $d$
is the dimension: for a four-dimensional spacetime $4\times 10=40$ numbers at every point, with respect to a chart,
to say what parallel transport means (``crazy, but so it is'') \ts{1}{1}{1:16:53}.
Thus a spacetime is a set; with an atlas it is a manifold; and to the atlas one adds something called a
\textbf{connection} (``Zusammenhang''), which looks, not by chance, like the nabla one knows, but is much more subtle:
the nabla of school is only a special case \ts{1}{1}{1:17:56}.
\begin{coursenote}[The number of components, and $\nabla$]
The count in the transcript (``vier mal zehn sind 40'') is the number of independent components $\Gamma^i{}_{jk}$ of a
torsion-free connection in four dimensions, $4\cdot 10$; with torsion one would have $4^3=64$. The statement
``it looks like the nabla from school'' means the covariant derivative $\nabla$.
\end{coursenote}

\paragraph{Physics: particles, forces, inertial systems.} Once these mathematical achievements are understood we can
begin physics and define our first concepts: what are \emph{particles}, what are \emph{forces}, and what are the
famous \emph{inertial systems} of Newton's laws \ts{1}{1}{1:18:38}. First of all we define, mathematically
precisely, what spacetime is, and on that we define particles, forces, inertial systems \ts{1}{1}{1:19:22}.

\begin{example}[Rotation of a rocket]\label{ex:bv-rocket}
Everybody has an intuition about inertial systems: a rocket that is launched is accelerated, hence no inertial system.
But then it begins to spin about its own axis, as in real launches. What does ``to rotate'' mean? Take a completely
empty universe, an empty spacetime, with nothing but you: you rotate, but \emph{with respect to what}? That was
a big debate in physics: with respect to the fixed stars? (``nice excuse'') Or per se? What if you rotate a bucket of
water: the water surface becomes curved \ts{1}{1}{1:19:44}.
The answer: rotating or not is defined with respect to the \emph{parallelism structure}. Straight lines (``always
walking along your nose'') are defined by it as well, and also what a rotation is and, above all, what a
\emph{non-rotating} system is. Since one rotates \emph{in time}, inertial systems can be understood precisely only in
the spacetime picture; in the space picture it is impossible \ts{1}{1}{1:20:46}.
\end{example}
\begin{history}[Newton's bucket]
The rotating bucket is the argument of the Scholium in Newton's \emph{Principia} (1687) for absolute space; Mach criticised
it in \emph{Die Mechanik in ihrer Entwickelung} (1883). Schuller only alludes to the debate (``Newton's bucket and so on'').
(Not said in class; not elaborated.)
\end{history}

\paragraph{Newtonian spacetime.} We do Newtonian spacetime: a set, a manifold, an \emph{affine} manifold (it has the
connection), and then a \emph{function} is added that assigns to every point of spacetime a number, the
\textbf{absolute time}. This gives the first definition: the absolute time is a smooth function whose gradient vanishes
nowhere (``careful what gradient means''; that comes with manifolds) \ts{1}{1}{1:21:28}.
This is a precise mathematical object; on it we say what inertial systems, particles and forces are, and then we can
compute at once how things work: the Coriolis force etc. Schuller looked on Wikipedia: there are the most adventurous
explanations (where do factors of two come from, \dots); on our affine manifolds it is a trivial, absolute child's game
\ts{1}{1}{1:22:09}. (Understanding manifolds and their connections is required; has the audience
heard of the divergence operator in spherical coordinates? the gradient in spherical coordinates? Not necessary to have
done it) \ts{1}{1}{1:22:30}.

Only then, with this mathematical framework, come the physical definitions as mathematical quantities, and only then can
Newton's laws, written down at the start, be formulated precisely. Why so precisely? \emph{Because we want to squeeze
them}: we ``massage Newton's axioms until they are soft''; one always thinks a 300-year-old subject has nothing new to
give, but that is not so \ts{1}{1}{1:22:50}.

\paragraph{Gravitation, and the relativistic spacetime.} The first big example (``green on green'') is
\textbf{gravitation}, first treated as a force, since that is what Newton and everybody did until Einstein said: we may
equally well change our \emph{concepts} (not the truth, not the world) and understand the force as the
\emph{curvature of spacetime} \ts{1}{1}{1:23:53}. Einstein did not speak of Newtonian
spacetime but of his relativistic spacetime, which has the same ingredients up to here, except that instead of a global
time function (which splits the manifold into hyperplanes of simultaneity, $t=$ const.) there is a \textbf{Lorentzian
metric}: at each point of the manifold, in the tangent space (to be explained), little ``cones'' are erected, and how
these cones are oriented in spacetime tells which motions are possible \ts{1}{1}{1:24:35}.
The cones can ``conspire'': far away they look normal, but close to a region they tilt so that an imaginary line arises
(a picture, ``like every picture, wrong, but almost right''): particles may move only inside the cone (their tangent
vectors lie inside it, in a spacetime picture), so they can run in but not out; that would be the \emph{event horizon of
a black hole} \ts{1}{1}{1:25:18}.

The opening of the cone is fixed by the speed of light, the maximal speed anything can have. What happens when
it becomes larger? The double cone opens up, and in the extreme case it becomes a plane (the hyperplane of simultaneity,
$t=$ const.): that is the limit $c\to\infty$ of Newtonian spacetime \ts{1}{1}{1:26:21}.
So the relation between the Newtonian and the relativistic spacetime lies only in the spacetime structure, ``nothing
mysterious''. Schuller says explicitly: if you do not understand parts of this outline, that is intended, it is just an
overview to connect things that one loses sight of; watch the video again at the end of the semester
\ts{1}{1}{1:27:03}.

\paragraph{End of part one; the literature.} With gravitation, and with the physics done, the first part of the course is
finished: the part usually found in books, \emph{Newton's formulation of mechanics}; in fact few books do it like this: what is
presented here is the \textbf{Newton--Einstein formulation} of non-relativistic mechanics \ts{1}{1}{1:27:43}.
A book that does this is Misner, Thorne and Wheeler, \emph{Gravitation} (``very thick, it exists in the library in limited
quantity, otherwise the library floors would break''; one should look at it but rather come to the lecture); it also
treats general relativity, and the same philosophy is followed here. It is the best literature suggestion for part one
\ts{1}{1}{1:28:04}.

\paragraph{Calculus of variations; Lagrangian mechanics.} Next comes a topic of great importance, \textbf{calculus of
variations} (Variationsrechnung) \ts{1}{1}{1:29:06}. In school one solves $5x=7$ and
finds a number; with a differential equation $f=f'$ the result is not a number but a \emph{function}. In a
\emph{functional} equation one imposes a condition on a function, for example that a certain integral over the
function has some property such as a maximum or minimum: to a function one assigns a value, which is called a
\textbf{functional}. Then one can answer questions such as: if we know how to measure lengths, what is the shortest
connection between two points? ``Straight?'' Try on the sphere, or something yet more complicated \ts{1}{1}{1:29:59}.
This is a big part of classical mechanics, and we do it with and without constraints (``Nebenbedingungen''). Once done,
it gives a \emph{reformulation} of Newton's second law, ascribed to Lagrange: \textbf{Lagrangian mechanics}, not a
different mechanics but a different formulation, through the calculus of variations and the differential equations
derived from it \ts{1}{1}{1:31:01}.

\paragraph{``1001 applications''.} Then comes a big topic, favourite for exams: ``1000 and one applications'' (``do not
believe the numbers''). Lagrangian mechanics is great: compared with the usual force balances, one can write, without
much fuss, the equations of motion of, e.g., a triple pendulum, which one can try by the standard methods (laborious)
\ts{1}{1}{1:31:43}.
\begin{example}[The double pendulum lives on a torus]\label{ex:bv-double-pendulum}
Even the double pendulum (``double chin'', as a hinge): what is its configuration space, if one makes the
second rod shorter or longer? Which positions can it take? Think about it: the space in which it can move is effectively
the \emph{surface of a car tyre}, a torus (the ``planet'' of the atlas story); for a double pendulum, that is its
whole world; ``geometrically, a wrinkly Earth is possible, very good'' (a student comment) \ts{1}{1}{1:32:24}.
\end{example}
\begin{exercise}[Configuration space of the double pendulum]\label{ex:bv-torus}
Show that the configuration space of a planar double pendulum (two rods of fixed lengths, hinged at a fixed point and
to each other) is a two-torus $S^1\times S^1$; what changes if the pendulum may also move in three dimensions?
(Added; not said in class.)
\end{exercise}

\paragraph{Hamiltonian mechanics and phase space.} Built on that is another reformulation, the second formulation:
\textbf{Hamiltonian mechanics}, ``in celestial mechanics here is where the action is'' \ts{1}{1}{1:33:06}.
Its basis is the \textbf{phase space} (Phasenraum), the space of \emph{initial conditions}: one looks at things not any more
in position space or spacetime but in the space of all initial conditions of the system, and a function on the
space of initial conditions is called an \textbf{observable}. This is also what one needs in quantum mechanics, but above all
observables are the answers to every question one may ask of a system, and Hamilton's equation tells how the answer to
every possible question evolves in time: ``if that is not a super theoretical formulation, I do not know'' \ts{1}{1}{1:33:27}.

\paragraph{Symmetries and conservation laws, symplectic manifolds.} Then follows the connection between \textbf{symmetries
and conserved quantities} (Noether's theorem): in the Lagrangian formulation it takes two blackboards; in the Hamiltonian
formulation (the Poisson-algebra formulation) it is a one-liner \ts{1}{1}{1:34:08}. Then dynamical
symmetries, the Kepler problem and so on, which Schuller calls (``1001 theoretical applications'', as a mathematician
understands an application) \ts{1}{1}{1:34:29}. If time remains: \textbf{symplectic manifolds}, which is
again about phase space: once understood, ``one draws the picture, stares at it and knows at once what the system does'';
it allows one to solve mechanical problems \emph{qualitatively, without computation} \ts{1}{1}{1:34:50}.

\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What is the aim of theoretical physics, and why is theory logically prior to observation?
  \item Which part of a physical theory comes from the concepts and which from the experiment? Give the example of
        energy and mass.
  \item Why are inertial and heavy mass conceptually different, and what does their experimental equality imply?
  \item Which of Newton's five statements contain terms with no mathematical definition? List them.
  \item Why is the first law not a special case of the second?
  \item Why is the position vector forbidden?
  \item Why does a manifold need overlapping charts, and where is the global structure encoded?
  \item Why is parallelism not defined by equality of components, and how many numbers define it?
\end{enumerate}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Theory before observation; concepts arbitrary and mathematical with a measurement prescription; equations from
        experiment identify concepts (\cref{sec:bv-theory-exp}).
  \item Inertial mass $=$ heavy mass: the idea behind general relativity (\cref{ssec:bv-mass}).
  \item The five statements of Newton and the terms to define (\cref{def:bv-newton}); the first law defines straight lines

  \item Spacetime as the data structure of the universe; the third law and pairwise interaction (\cref{ssec:bv-third}).
  \item Plan: set $\to$ manifold (atlas, charts, overlaps) $\to$ affine manifold (connection) $\to$ Newtonian spacetime
        (absolute time as a smooth function with nowhere vanishing gradient) $\to$ relativistic spacetime (Lorentzian
        metric, light cone, $c\to\infty$) (\cref{sec:bv-structure}).
  \item Calculus of variations, Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, phase space and observables, symmetries,
        symplectic manifolds, general relativity.
\end{itemize}
\end{keyconcepts}
